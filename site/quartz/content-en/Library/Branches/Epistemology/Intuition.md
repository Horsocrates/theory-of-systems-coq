Intuition is the transmission of information to consciousness in response to its request, bypassing discursive mediation: the whole is grasped at once, before the parts and before the justification.

## Etymology

**Intuition** ← Latin *intuitio* ← *intueri*, “to look at, to gaze upon”: *in-*, “at, toward” + *tueri*, “to look, to watch.” The prefix directs the gaze at the object: an attentive looking, a contemplation. The word became a philosophical term in scholasticism: at the turn of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries Duns Scotus distinguished from abstractive cognition the intuitive—the grasp of a singular thing as existing here and now. Modern usage has shifted the meaning toward a “hunch,” an unverified feeling. Here the original sense is kept: the intuitive is a direct grasp that precedes reasoning.

The name of the paired way carries another image: **discursive thought** ← Latin *discursus* ← *discurrere*, “to run to and fro”—the movement of thought from position to position, step by step and unfolded in time. Two roots, two ways of taking: gazing and running through.

> [!note]- History of the concept
>
> **Antiquity.** The Greeks were the first to distinguish two paths of knowing systematically, and they set direct intellection above reasoning: reason articulates what the mind has seen. Plato crowns the ladder of knowing with intellection—νόησις (noēsis): the ascent from the cave ends in a seeing. Aristotle shows in the *Posterior Analytics* that all proofs proceed from principles (ἀρχαί, archai) that are themselves not proved—they are grasped by the mind, νοῦς (nous). In Epicurus the “cast of thought”—ἐπιβολὴ τῆς διανοίας (epibolē tēs dianoias)—is the turning of the mind to its object and the direct grasp of a presentation. In Plotinus reasoning, διάνοια (dianoia), unfolds truth part by part; mind grasps it at once and whole; and the One is above thought: with it only union is possible.
>
> **Scholasticism.** The pair *intellectus* and *ratio* was fixed for good: for Aquinas, reason unfolds into a chain what the intellect sees at once. Nicholas of Cusa carried the line to “learned ignorance” (*docta ignorantia*): the highest knowledge is knowledge of the limits of reason.
>
> **Ishrāq.** The most systematic treatment of direct knowing was given by the Persian philosopher Shihāb al-Dīn Suhrawardī (c. 1154–1191), “the master of illumination.” In 1183 he came to Aleppo and grew close to its ruler al-Ẓāhir, the son of Saladin; in 1186 he completed *The Philosophy of Illumination* (*Ḥikmat al-ishrāq*); he was accused of heresy and executed. By his own testimony he obtained the truth directly—by “tasting” (dhawq)—and only then sought proofs for it; he distinguished sages by their strength in two kinds of knowledge, the discursive and the intuitive, and held the one proficient in both to be perfect. The basis of direct knowledge for him is presence (ḥuḍūr): knower and known are present to each other without intermediaries. On this page presence and intuition are kept apart: presence is the meeting itself, intuition is a channel of knowledge-about.
>
> His ontology is an ontology of light: light is self-evident and needs no definition; bodies are “barriers” (barzakh) in the way of light. Mullā Ṣadrā wrote glosses on his book and put being in the place of light; to European scholarship Suhrawardī was opened in the twentieth century by Henry Corbin.[^1]
>
> Beside this stands an Indian parallel: in Nyāya, direct perception, pratyakṣa, stands first among the sources of valid knowledge, before inference and testimony.
>
> **The modern age.** Locke gave intuitive knowledge the highest degree of certainty: that white is not black the mind sees at once, and demonstration itself rests on a chain of such intuitions (*An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, IV.2). Descartes called intuition the conception of a pure and attentive mind, so easy and distinct that no doubt remains: that two and two make four is seen, not inferred (*Rules for the Direction of the Mind*, III). Spinoza placed *scientia intuitiva*—knowledge “in one glance” (*uno intuitu*)—highest of the three kinds of knowledge.
>
> **Kant and the reply to him.** Kant narrowed the word itself: intuition, *Anschauung*, is in a human being only sensible—space and time are its forms; an intuition through which the very existence of the object would be given could belong only to the primordial being. Hence the dispute of the following century: Fichte and Schelling brought intellectual intuition back; Bergson set intuition against analysis—analysis goes around the thing, intuition enters into it.
>
> **The twentieth century.** Husserl made intuition a method: the seeing of essences, *Wesensschau*, and categorial intuition. His “principle of all principles”: every originally giving intuition is a source of right for knowledge—what is given in it is to be accepted as it gives itself, and within the limits in which it gives itself.
>
> From the other side intuition was brought back by scientific practice: Poincaré and—by his own late account—Kekulé described solutions that came ready-made after long preparation; Polanyi generalized this as *tacit knowledge*—“we can know more than we can tell”; Klein described in firefighters and chess players recognition ahead of deliberation—thousands of cases compressed into one insight; Kahneman, adopting the terms of Stanovich and West, described two modes of thinking—the fast System 1 and the slow System 2, where the second checks what the first delivers. Gödel spoke of mathematical intuition directly: the axioms of set theory “force themselves upon us as being true.”

## Definition

**Intuition is the transmission of information to consciousness in response to its request, bypassing discursive mediation.** Every word of the definition does work:

- *transmission*—a process, not a state;
- *information*—the content that becomes available: by type, [[Epistemology#Types of Knowledge: Knowledge-About, Knowledge-How|knowledge-about]], taken as a whole;
- *to consciousness*—the addressee;
- *in response to a request*—the ground: without a request there is no transmission;
- *bypassing discursive mediation*—the difference from reasoning: the answer comes whole, and is not built from premises.

The act itself—taking the whole at once—is called **insight** below; the step-by-step movement of thought is called **discursive thought**.

## Intuition as a System

An analysis by the [[Theory of Systems]]—from the goal to the rules, the roles, and the elements.

- **The goal** is an answer to a request: the whole, taken at once.
- **The rules** are these: the ground of the transmission is a request ([[Sufficient Reason]]); there are three conditions—a request, the capacity of the witness, an open channel; the whole is grasped at once but understood in order ([[Order]]); what has come is unfolded and checked.
- **The roles** are these: the witness—requests and receives; the source—the objective field: from it the whole comes; the conductor—the body for direction, consciousness for knowledge.
- **The elements** are the request; the whole that has come; the response of the body; the witness’s records.

## The Nature of Intuition

**The ground of the transmission is a request.** Intuition answers: what has come is an answer, and an answer presupposes a request. The same is required by [[Sufficient Reason]]. Contact between the parts of reality is always there; the transmission of information is a separate process, and it must have a cause. The least cause is the turning of consciousness.

**A request is wider than a question.** A question is a request given form ([[Question]]): it sets a definite framework. A request leaves the field open, only pointing to the will to receive an answer. Its forms differ: strained attention to a state of affairs, waiting after long work, openness without a definite focus—and a question, when a framework has been set; what they have in common is one thing—the directedness of consciousness beyond the boundary of what is recorded, to where the answer is still to come. Hence insight brings also that which one could not have asked about: the whole that has come is larger than the question ([[Epistemology#Field, Slice, Boundary|the third zone]]).

**A request presupposes not knowing.** One who is sure of knowing does not request: the answer has nowhere to come. Hence intuition speaks to those who ask and is silent with the categorical.

Hence the **three conditions** of the transmission: *a request*—consciousness is seeking, openly or tacitly; *sufficient complexity of the one who is conscious*—the capacity to take in the level requested; *an open channel*—the filter does not block the signal. When all three meet, the transmission goes; when any is lacking, there is silence or distortion. In the structure of knowledge the conditions have exact places: complexity is the threshold, openness is the channel ([[Epistemology#Depth|depth]]); the request stands apart: threshold and channel limit, while the request acts—it is the activation.

**Intuition is not innate content.** What is built in—instincts, innate structures of perception—works automatically, is switched on by a trigger, and its content took shape before any experience. Intuition is built differently: it shows itself in the moment, adjusts to the particular situation, and requires a request. The layers interact—intuition takes what is built in as material—but it is itself a process, not a content.

## Two Modes

**By request and by conductor.** To “what is to be done?” intuition answers with *direction*: worth it or not; direction carries no explanations, and silence is the absence of a signal, not a third answer. The conductor of direction is the body. To “what is?” it answers with *knowledge*: gradual, from a dim “there is something here” to full clarity; the conductor of knowledge is consciousness.

With direction the conductor is concrete: the responses of the body carry the verdict “worth it—not worth it” without waiting for a formulation. In different people the body speaks differently—the belly, the chest, the skin—but it says one thing: “where to,” not “why.”

> [!example]- Case: the Iowa Gambling Task—the body answers before consciousness
> Participants were asked to draw cards from four decks: two gave large winnings but still larger losses—a loss in the long run; two gave modest winnings with small losses—a gain in the long run. Healthy players gradually switched to the advantageous decks. As the authors of the experiment observed, a skin conductance response appeared before the hand reached for a “bad” deck—earlier than the player could say why the deck was bad: the body already knew what consciousness had not yet said.[^2] Later the conclusion was contested: under more detailed questioning the players showed conscious knowledge about the decks already at the steps where none had been ascribed to them.[^3]
>
> Patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex gave no such response—and kept drawing from the “bad” decks with intelligence intact. Damasio called these signals somatic markers. Their conductors are recognizable both in language and in tradition: the “gut feeling”—the sensations of the inner organs, out of which the sense of the state of the body is composed; the heart—the *qalb* of the Sufis, the “mind brought down into the heart” of the Hesychasts; gooseflesh—a brief sign of significance. All three say one thing—worth it or not—without explaining why.

Knowledge has two dimensions—clarity and purity.

*Clarity*: dim recognition—partial clarity—full clarity, when the structure is seen whole. It does not grow linearly with the time of deliberation: hours of work may leave one in the dim, a moment of detachment may give full clarity; but discursive preparation is usually necessary—intuition comes as the closing of what has been accumulated.

*Purity*: is what is seen the structure of the object, or a template laid over it? Knowledge is distorted by four interferences: the projection of a ready template; another’s authority taken for self-evidence; intellectual laziness—the first explanation in place of insight; the wish for intelligibility—a simple picture in place of a complex reality. They work at the level of concepts: the body does not notice them—checking does.

By its material the intuitive relies either on what is built in or on what has ripened: settled experience that has become tacit. This difference runs through both modes.

## Insight—the Vertical Channel of Knowledge-About

**Intuition is a channel of knowledge-about, not of presence.** Knowledge-about is taken by two paths: insight takes the whole at once; discursive thought goes by details, step by step—slowly and narrowly, but every step can be shown and conveyed. The difference is whether the knowledge is taken whole or by parts. Presence is something else: the meeting itself, direct contact ([[Epistemology#Types of Knowledge: Knowledge-About, Knowledge-How|types of knowledge]]). Intuitive knowledge may come during a meeting too, but these are different things.

**The direction of the channel is upward.** Discursive thought moves along a tier: fact after fact, distinction after distinction. Insight reads the embracing whole—the tier where the parts are already gathered: the forest before the trees, the structure of a proof before its steps, a face before the list of its features.

**Insight passes the whole ladder of knowledge**—as a whole: a whole is presented, a whole is read, a whole becomes sense ([[Epistemology#The Ladder and Its Ground|the ladder]]). And the order of the tiers is intact: understanding goes by [[Order]], tier after tier, while intuition delivers the whole—taken at once and not yet unfolded. One can grasp the whole at once—one can understand it only in order. So it was with Poincaré: the thought that the transformations he had used to define the Fuchsian functions are identical with those of non-Euclidean geometry came as he was stepping onto an omnibus—after long preparation, which had filled the lower tiers and raised the threshold.[^4] The request, meanwhile, held: the problem had been posed and was waiting for an answer. A whole that comes “by itself” is the fruit of a path traversed: intuition crowns order.

**The principles are supplied by insight.** Every proof proceeds from principles that are not proved within it—otherwise there is an infinite regress; Aristotle assigned the grasping of them to the mind (νοῦς)—the capacity to recognize first principles directly. One correction: the last principles have a name—[[Logic]] itself, and it stands by itself: the structure of existence grounds itself ([[Sufficient Reason]]). Aristotle is right about something else—about *how* principles are taken: directly, not by inference. The first records of discursive thought are brought by insight; and when the steps have been gone through, the whole is again taken by insight.

## Reception, Errors, Criterion

**The whole comes as information.** Data are in the external world, in the objective field; they reach the witness through channels. Intuition is a channel, and through it the whole passes already as information: read and made sense of ([[Epistemology#The Ladder and Its Ground|the ladder of knowledge]]). What is retained is knowledge-about taken as a whole; unfolding gives it structure, and checking separates what was received rightly from what was distorted. Error is possible at two levels.

**The channel.** A channel has its own limitations, and they can distort what it brings—as color blindness does with sight: the distortion occurs at the level of the channel ([[Epistemology#Depth|the depth of the channel]]). The data remain the same; it is the information that arrives distorted—less is taken from the data, or it is taken differently.

**Reception.** The errors of reception are of three types:

- *inattention*: the channel is open, the signal comes, but it is not recognized as a signal—the whole of life passes in reasoning, and the intuitive is taken for random background;
- *the projection of expectations*: from direction one expects an explanation, from knowledge a bodily confirmation; the expectation locks the channel onto a familiar form;
- *emotional background as a substitution of the source*: fear, desire, trauma also pass through body and consciousness—one who has been betrayed “senses” betrayal everywhere, one in love “knows” that the partner is ideal; this is background, not intuition.

**The criterion.** The signal of intuition may be barely discernible or plain: the strength of a signal is not a counterfeit. But **a signal cannot insist**: data, the channel, what has come cannot argue, repeat themselves, press. It is the person who insists—when a projection and an interpretation drawn from that person’s representation of oneself, the ego, have been laid over the signal. The ego itself may be true—corresponding to how things are—or false: an illusion ([[Metaphysics#The Ego Zone|the Ego zone]]). One insists when one takes the representation of oneself for oneself: then one defends the record—both the true one and the false. A false representation also compels one to insist: an illusion has to be defended against every check. Emotional background betrays itself in the same way: it insists, repeats itself, and grows stronger under opposition. To recognize a signal is another matter: one whose connection with one’s own body is good, and in whom intuition shows itself by definite signs—gooseflesh, for example—says “this is certainly intuition” fittingly when the sign has come. The sign says that this is intuition; whether what has come is understood rightly is decided by checking.

The criteria of purity differ for the two modes. Pure direction is confirmed by the body: the response is calm and definite—without the anxious clenching of fear and without the excitement of desire. Pure knowledge withstands the test of coherence and of extension: carried into a neighboring domain, it keeps working.

**The discipline of reception is the improvement of contact with oneself:** slowing down—a pause between the request and the answer; honesty—a readiness to see that the “intuition” has conveniently coincided with what one wished; silence—until the inner noise has died down, the signal cannot be heard. A compass works only if the one who holds it can keep still: every sharp swing is a movement of the hand, not of the needle.

**Articulation and checking.** For what has been grasped to enter common knowledge, the whole is unfolded into a sequence of details: the old pair *intellectus* and *ratio* receives an exact sense—the intellect sees the whole, reason unfolds what is seen into a chain open to checking and to conveying ([[Metaphysics#The Intellect|the Intellect]]). What has been unfolded is tested like any knowledge-about.

**Intuition begins, discursive thought checks.** Intuition grasps the whole; reason lays it out into elements. One without the other is incomplete: a whole without elements remains inexpressible, elements without a whole are a meaningless collection.

The extreme case is mystical experience: a direct insight with a change in the very state of the knower—it comes by itself, without a question having been posed, lasts a short time, is never fully expressible, and carries deep cognitive content. The demands here are higher than usual: the channel is opened by the conformity of consciousness to the conditions of the experience, not by the pursuit of it; and what it brings enters knowledge by the same road as everything else—by unfolding and by checking, and the farther the source is from the steps of reasoning, the stricter they are.[^5]

**Intuition is given to everyone**—to a child, to an old man, to one who does not know the word “philosophy”; all that can be learned is to hear it.

## In Reasoning

**Intuition is the vertical dimension that runs through all six [[Domains of Reasoning|domains]].** In [[1 Recognition|recognition]] it suggests where to look; in [[2 Clarification|clarification]], what is essential; in [[3 Framework Selection|framework selection]], which framework answers the object; in [[4 Comparison|comparison]], what is comparable; in [[5 Inference|inference]], it senses agreement or strain; in [[6 Reflection|reflection]], it sees what has been missed. It replaces no domain—it gives each a support ([[Architecture of Reasoning#Intuition and Reasoning|“Intuition and Reasoning”]]).

In ethics the same line is continued: conscience is a type of intuitive response to the status “right—wrong” ([[Ethics]]).

## Summary

1. **Intuition is the transmission of information to consciousness in response to its request, bypassing discursive mediation:** the whole is taken at once.
2. **The ground of the transmission is a request.** A request is wider than a question: a question sets a definite framework; a request leaves the field open, only pointing to the will to receive an answer. There are three conditions: a request, the capacity of the witness, an open channel.
3. **Two modes.** Direction answers “what is to be done?”, and its conductor is the body; knowledge answers “what is?”, and its conductor is consciousness. Knowledge has two dimensions: clarity and purity.
4. **Intuition is a channel of knowledge-about, not of presence:** insight reads the whole—the tier where the parts are already gathered.
5. **One can grasp the whole at once—one can understand it only in order.** Intuition crowns order; it also brings reasoning its principles.
6. **The whole comes as information:** data are in the external world, and what passes through the channel is already information; what is retained is knowledge-about taken as a whole: unfolding gives it structure, checking separates what was received rightly from what was distorted. Error is possible both in the channel—its limitations distort—and in reception: inattention, the projection of expectations, emotional background.
7. **The criterion: a signal cannot insist**—it is the person who insists, when taking the representation of oneself for oneself or defending an illusion. A recognized sign is another matter: “this is certainly intuition” is fitting for one whose connection with one’s own body is good; whether what has come is understood rightly is decided by checking.
8. **Intuition begins, discursive thought checks:** a whole without elements is inexpressible, elements without a whole are a collection.
9. **In reasoning** intuition is the vertical dimension of all six domains; in ethics its response is conscience.
