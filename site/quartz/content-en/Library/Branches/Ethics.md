**Ethics is an action, a process whose goal is the maintenance of order through the right arrangement of roles.** It is not postulated but derived: below is the chain of its derivation from “*A* exists.” As a system it is the evaluation of choice.

Each step of the chain is numbered; in parentheses are its premises—the steps it follows from—or the mark “support” or “definition”; under a step, where needed, comes a commentary. There are six supports; they belong to [[Logic]], the [[Theory of Systems]], and [[Metaphysics]], where they are derived in their own course. Ethics has no postulates of its own.

## Etymology

**Ethics** ← Greek ἠθική (ēthikē) “pertaining to character” ← ἦθος (ēthos): at first, an accustomed dwelling place (in Homer, of animals), then character, a settled disposition. Beside it stands ἔθος (ethos), “habit, custom.” Aristotle linked the two words: ethical virtue arises from habit and takes its name from it; intellectual virtue comes mainly from teaching.[^1] The Latin *moralis* was coined by Cicero—from *mos*, *mores* “custom, character,” on the model of the Greek word.[^2] The Russian *nrav* belongs to the same series: disposition, custom; *norov* is the same word in its native Russian shape.

The domain the Greeks called ethics is named by other traditions in their own words, and the primary images of those words differ.

> [!note]- A map of words: how the traditions name this domain
>
> | Language | Word | Primary sense |
> |---|---|---|
> | Greek | ἦθος (ēthos) | accustomed dwelling place → settled disposition, character |
> | Greek | ἔθος (ethos) | habit, custom |
> | Greek | ἠθική (ēthikē) | “pertaining to character”; ethical virtue comes from habit (Aristotle) |
> | Latin | *mos*, *mores* → *moralis* | custom, character; *moralis* is Cicero’s word |
> | Russian | *nrav* → *nravstvennost’* | disposition, custom; *norov* is the same word in its Russian shape |
> | German | *Sitte* → *Sittlichkeit* | custom → morality; in Hegel, the ethical life of the family, civil society, and the state |
> | Pali | *sīla* | habit, disposition, moral conduct |
> | Sanskrit | *dharma* (root *dhṛ*) | that which holds and sustains: order, support |
> | Vedic | *ṛta* | the due order, law; by one etymology, “what is fitted” |
> | Hebrew | *halakha* | “the walking”—the path one walks |
> | Chinese | *dào* · *lúnlǐ* | the path · the order of human relations |

There are four primary images: a disposition grown out of habit; the upholding of order; a path; the structure of human relations—who stands to whom as what: the arrangement of roles. The word “ethics” itself carries the first; the other three are carried by the words with which other traditions name the same domain. Brought together, the images yield the definition of ethics—step 7 of the chain.

## The Supports

**Step 1** *(support)*. To be is to be determinate: “*A* exists.”

The starting point of the whole theory: to exist is to carry determinacy. From this beginning both the laws of [[Logic|logic]] and the triad of a system are derived. Ethics introduces no new ground—it stands on the same one.

**Step 2** *(support)*. Logic is self-identical (*A* = *A*) and stands by itself; therefore it is objective and cannot deviate from itself.

The laws of logic are given together with being ([[Sufficient Reason]]). For ethics this is the basis of objectivity: the status “right” rests on structure, not on opinion.

**Step 3** *(support)*. Every determinacy draws a distinction: the pair “yes—no” is exclusive (Non-Contradiction) and exhaustive (Excluded Middle).

Distinction is the mechanism of determinacy itself. Below it returns as the polarity of the status “right—wrong” (step 14, the Law of Polarity).

**Step 4** *(support)*. Every system is analyzed by the triad: Rules—how it is arranged; Roles—what an element is significant for; Elements—what exists. The generating order runs from the Rules through the Roles to the Elements.

The triad of the [[Theory of Systems]]. The rules determine the roles and the selection of elements—this order is at work in every step below.

**Step 5** *(support)*. The rules underdetermine their realization: there is the permitted but unspecified—the space of choice.

The frame holds the structure but does not dictate every move. Where the rules leave freedom, the place for ethics will appear (step 10).

**Step 6** *(support)*. The operator has a will. The manifestation of the will is attention; free will is two choices: of direction—where to direct attention—and of response—how to answer an experience. The choice of response is the subject of ethics.

The will and the operator are analyzed in [[Metaphysics#The Operator and the Will|Metaphysics]]: there free will is derived. Ethics takes it as a support (step 14, the Law of Free Will).

## Definition and Goal

**Step 7** *(definition)*. Ethics is an action, a process whose goal is the maintenance of order through the right arrangement of roles.

The definition is taken from the words with which the traditions name this domain (the section “Etymology”) and sets the subject of the derivation; steps 8–13 show its place in the structure of a system.

**Step 8** *(from steps 4, 7)*. The goal of ethics is the maintenance of order. What is meant is the existing order—the arrangement that is kept or broken; it is kept by the fulfillment of the rules ([[Order#In Ethics|Order]]). The goal has two conditions: (a) the rules exist—if they are not given, it must be established what they are; (b) action is directed at meeting their demands.

The goal divides into two parts: the rules exist—and their demands are met. Both will be needed: (a) unfolds into the question of forming a system, (b) into the question of acting within it (step 12).

**Step 9** *(from steps 2, 8)*. Laws cannot be broken: a law is a law of logic, and the laws of physics are the manifestation of the laws of logic with respect to the interaction of physical systems. Rules are constructions composed for the goal of a system, in society social constructions; they can be broken. Rules assign a status to any choice; a wrong choice violates the existing order, while the laws remain in force.

A stone does not break gravity, and in reality nothing breaks Identity ([[Identity]]); a traffic rule can be broken. To break a rule is to choose against it; in the system of that rule such a choice is wrong (step 27). But a rule may itself be wrong when it diverges from the goal of the system (step 32) or breaks the enclosing order (step 38); then the right thing is to correct it.

**Step 10** *(from steps 5, 9)*. Ethics exists only where a wrong choice is possible.

Action directed at meeting the demands of the rules has meaning only where a wrong outcome is possible too. Where it is impossible, there is execution, not ethics: a stone is not “in the wrong.”

**Step 11** *(definition; from step 10)*. Systems fall into two kinds: a **soft** system—the rules allow a right and a wrong choice, while the operator does not know in advance which is which; a **hard** system—a wrong choice is impossible.

The section [[Theory of Systems#Soft and Hard System|“Soft and Hard System.”]] Ethics lives only in soft systems; hard ones are exhausted by execution.

**Step 12** *(from steps 8, 11)*. The two key questions of ethics: **(a) the formation of a system**—what the rules of a soft system should be; **(b) action within a system**—how to act in accord with the rules.

Question (b) is the root one: “how to choose rightly?” To each question answers a definition of its own: **an ethically rightly built system** is the efficient work of the elements through right assignment to roles according to the established rules; **an ethically right act** is a choice in accord with the role and the rules of the system.

## Ethics as a System

**Step 13** *(from steps 6, 10, 11)*. Ethics has four conditions of existence: (1) a system whose rules allow a right and a wrong choice—a soft one; (2) an operator with free will; (3) options of choice with consequences; (4) the polarity of the status “right—wrong,” drawn by the rules. Without any one of them there is no ethics.

A check by contraposition: a hard system is execution; without an operator there is no one to choose; without options there is no choice; without polarity there is nothing to be right.

**Step 14** *(from steps 2, 3, 4, 6, 9)*. Ethics as a system has five rules, and all of them are manifestations of the laws of logic:

- **The Law of Polarity**—Non-Contradiction and Excluded Middle: the statuses “right” and “wrong” exclude each other, and there is no third status;
- **The Law of Conformity**—the Law of [[Order]]: to conform to the rules is to maintain order;
- **Violation of Order**—a consequence of the Law of Conformity: a wrong choice is a violation of the existing order; the laws remain unbreakable (step 9);
- **The Law of Free Will**—the Sufficient Reason of the system itself: ethics is possible only where both a right and a wrong choice are open to the will; ethics itself does not derive the will—it only points to its necessity (step 6);
- **The Law of Judgment by Role**—Identity: an act is judged by its conformity to its role; and Sufficient Reason: the ground of a choice is the understanding of one’s role and of the rules of the system.

All five laws of [[Logic|logic]] are covered: Identity and Sufficient Reason in the Law of Judgment by Role, Non-Contradiction and Excluded Middle in the Law of Polarity, Order in the Law of Conformity and its consequence; Sufficient Reason also in the Law of Free Will. Ethics adds no new laws to logic: its five rules are laws of logic applied to free choice, and therefore they cannot be broken (step 9).

**Step 15** *(from steps 6, 13)*. The roles of ethics: the **operator**—the one who chooses: the will inclines toward one side of the choice—and the **option of choice**—the bearer of the status being assigned. Evaluation is not a role: an evaluation is the assignment of a status according to the rules; there is no “evaluator” in the system.

The operator does not produce the evaluation—it tries to understand which evaluation stands behind a particular choice; its representation of the rules is fallible, and it can be checked by the result. The analysis of the operator and the will is in [[Metaphysics#The Operator and the Will|Metaphysics]].

**Step 16** *(from step 15)*. An evaluation is the correspondence of the result to the intention; the intention is the result the operator desires. **Ethics lives in intention—in the operator’s goals.**

Wanting the result *A* and choosing between two options, the operator tries to understand which of them leads to *A*. There are two correspondences in an evaluation: of the result to the intention—whether the means was chosen rightly—and of the intention itself to the role and the rules (step 17).

**Step 17** *(from steps 14, 16)*. The right goal is to be in one’s own role (*A* = *A*) and to act in accord with it. To take up one’s role is: (a) to learn one’s role; (b) to learn its rules; (c) to act in accord with both.

Free will includes the possibility of not taking up one’s role—therefore “to be in one’s own role” is a goal, not a given.

**Step 18** *(from step 17)*. A distinction: **“right—wrong” and “good—evil” are different polarities.** Rightness—conformity to the role and the rules—is relative to a system; good and evil have a different bearer.

One who does not know the rules of the encompassing system sees another’s fulfillment of a role as wrong; having accepted its rules, as right. An example at the limit: if the goal of the encompassing system is the knowledge of good and evil, it needs roles that create the possibility of such knowledge; the fulfillment of such a role by its rules is right, although its content is evil. The two polarities measure different things; their bearers are set apart in step 22.

**Step 19** *(from steps 13, 15, 16)*. The elements of ethics are what occupies its roles: a particular operator with its intention—the intention is within the operator—and particular options of choice with their consequences: a consequence is determinate and follows from the choice, but at the moment of choice it is unknown to the operator.

Evaluation is stretched between the inner and the outer: the intention is within, the consequence is outside and afterward. “Determinate but unknown” is the source of fallibility, the ground of checking by the result and of honest ignorance.

## Three Polarities

**Step 20** *(from step 19)*. Benefit and harm are the poles of the consequence; they are measured by the levels of the systems affected. The link with good and evil is not rigid: not every harm is evil, and not every benefit is good.

A lion ate a man—harm, but not evil. A man who, with intent, locked another where a lion ate him committed evil; without intent, it is harm and an error, but not evil. One who was deceived, whose good intentions led to harm, is not evil but ignorant: ignorance led to the harm. One consequence receives different evaluations at the different levels affected—a result has no single measure outside systems.

**Step 21** *(definition; from steps 6, 16, 20)*. Good and evil are the polarity of intent: **evil is an action against the will of another for one’s own ends, out of attraction to one’s own; good is an action for the sake of another, in accord with the other’s will, without personal gain and without an attraction of one’s own—out of repulsion from the wrongness of not doing.** Evil begins in intent: without intent there is no evil.

In the definitions the will stands on both sides: the free will of the operator meets the will of another—the polarity of intent lives between operators. The poles are asymmetric in the mechanics of the will: evil moves from attraction, good from repulsion, and repulsion from the wrong leads to the manifestation of a good nature. Good is not ascribed to oneself: the doer may see in the act simply rightness—it is the observer who classifies it as good. The matrix of the polarity, its derivation, and the derived affective plane are below.

> [!note]- The matrix of the polarity of intent, its derivation, and the affective plane
>
> Two features—the will of the other and whose interest it is—give four combinations; in the cell of accord the mechanics of the will decides:
>
> | | for one’s own ends | for the other’s ends |
> |---|---|---|
> | **against** the other’s will | **evil** | paternalism—neither evil nor good |
> | **in accord with** the other’s will | exchange, cooperation—outside the polarity | a right act; **good**—if without attraction, out of repulsion |
>
> **Paternalism** is an action against the will of another for the other’s benefit. The claim “I am going not against the person’s will but against the misfortune that has bound it” is checked by **the addressee and the time of the action**: a genuine action against a misfortune is addressed to the misfortune and comes earlier; an action that arises only at the moment of another’s choice and is aimed at blocking it is addressed to the will. A divergence of the declared goal from the structure of the action is rationalization. **Exchange, cooperation**—an action in accord with the will of another for one’s own ends—lies outside the polarity “good—evil.”
>
> **The polarity is derived from the true order (step 37), not postulated.** Evil—through the will: the free will of the operator is a condition of ethics itself, so the wills of operators belong to the true order; an action against the will of another for one’s own ends is an exception made for oneself: the law of Non-Contradiction is not applied to oneself, and the true order is distorted where it concerns wills. Good—through the sufficient reason of the act: when an act for the sake of another has neither gain nor an attraction of one’s own, the only ground left is the nature itself, turned toward order—which is why good is recognized from outside, by excluding personal grounds.
>
> **It is the observer who classifies good**—an operator with manifested [[Metaphysics#The Manifestations of Logic|consciousness]] who is in contact with the situation: present or affected. The observer is fallible, like anyone who comes to know an evaluation: it is corrected by the check by addressee and time and by the check by consequences; when observers diverge, [[Logic|logic]] itself decides. The doer can become the observer of its own act—by reflection, after the fact. One who carries the true order from within does first of all what one wants oneself: to act rightly; when such right acts bring positive consequences, the observer calls them good consequences. Good also differs from the ought: the ought is “what is held to be required,” a demand; good is the manifestation of a nature.
>
> **The affective plane**—pleasure; pain and suffering; love and joy—is a derived layer of the polarity of intent, and it is asymmetric: **evil has a kind whose personal interest is pleasure in another’s suffering.** Good has no such kind: good is an action without personal gain and without an attraction of one’s own will; pleasure in the joy of others is already a personal interest, and such an act is right but not good.

**Step 22** *(from steps 18, 20, 21)*. A summary: three polarities on three bearers; they combine independently.

| Polarity | Measure | Bearer |
|---|---|---|
| right—wrong | conformity to the role and the rules | the system |
| good—evil | intent | within the operator |
| benefit—harm | result | the consequence of the choice |

The three axes measure different things, and the examples of step 20 show their independence: harm without evil, evil with another’s harm, a good intention with a harmful outcome. Each axis has its own yardstick: rightness is measured by the rules of the doer’s system, intent lives within the operator, benefit and harm are measured by the levels of the systems affected.

## The Root Question and Knowledge

**Step 23** *(from step 12)*. The root question of ethics is “how to choose rightly?”; its three words are the three parts of the task: “how”—method and process; “to choose”—a free operator; “rightly”—the polarity by the rules.

Question (b) of step 12 unfolds into the program of the whole further derivation: each following section answers one of the parts.

**Step 24** *(from step 23)*. “How” is knowledge of the mechanism of choice: ethics is derived through logic, and logic is a fact; the right mechanism of choice, once understood, is written down—and that is a code.

What remains a process is the mastering of the mechanism and the knowing of particular rules; the mechanism itself is constant. That is how the [[Code of Ethics]] is put together: a record of what is derived, applied to a system.

**Step 25** *(from steps 6, 15)*. The operator carries two distinct capacities: the [[Metaphysics#The Operator and the Will|will]]—it chooses—and the system of evaluation—it comes to know the criteria and keeps them stable.

To choose and to understand an evaluation are different capacities; the next two steps stand on this difference.

**Step 26** *(from step 25)*. Since the capacities differ, it is possible to know the right—and choose otherwise: akrasia—ἀκρασία (akrasia), incontinence. The root question includes this too: how the will is to follow the evaluation.

**Step 27** *(from steps 9, 25; supports 1, 2)*. The source of the criteria is the rules of the system: right means in accord with the rules of the system in which the choice is made. The criteria in the operator’s system of evaluation are its partial, fallible representation of the rules; the source is the rules themselves. The question “why is it right” goes back to the constitution of the system and, at the limit, to the beginning of the chain: “*A* exists,” the laws of logic, the triad.

Rightness is objective through structure: it is relative to a system (step 18).

**Step 28** *(from steps 26, 27)*. Ethics rests on the theory of knowledge: “how to choose rightly” is to find out the rules (knowledge) and to choose in accord with them (will). Everything that exists is knowable, but knowing is never completed—hence fallibility and the distinction of errors: an error of knowing—one missed while trying to act rightly—differs from akrasia: one knew, and chose otherwise.

## The Right, Order, the True Order

**Step 29** *(from step 4)*. Order in a system is its orderedness: fitting elements in the roles according to the rules; disorder is unfitting and arbitrary assignments.

The word “order” stands here in two senses. Order as a law is the Law of Order, a property of Logic: what is distinguished stands in sequence and by levels; the law is unbreakable ([[Order]]). Order as a property of a system is orderedness: it is kept and broken; this is the existing order.

**Step 30** *(from step 29)*. The striving for order is objective: fitness is a fact about correspondence, arbitrariness is an assignment without a ground. Fitness is determined from the goal of the role—its function in the encompassing system; to establish fitness is a task of analysis, of knowing.

Roles and assignment—steps 17, 31, 44–46; the formation of a system—[[Theory of Systems#The Goal of a System: Task and Efficiency|the goal of a system: task and efficiency]].

**Step 31** *(from steps 5, 30)*. Disorder arises where there is no filter of fitness, not where one chooses among the worthy: the threshold of fitness gives a pool of candidates; within the pool the choice is free—how exactly is analyzed in steps 44–46.

**Step 32** *(from steps 14, 30)*. Right means corresponding: an element to the goal of the role, or a rule to the goals of the system, when the operator itself lays down the rules. This is the primary and decidable status.

The decidability is proved: from an act, the status “right” can be computed ([[Formalization]]).

**Step 33** *(from step 32)*. Benefit is doing right and its fruit: benefit is a consequence of correspondence, not a thing; the right bears benefit, the wrong bears harm.

The poles of the consequence, their measure by the levels affected, and the non-rigid link with good and evil—step 20.

**Step 34** *(from steps 6, 32)*. The ought is to do right while seeing the wrong: the command is switched on by the perception of the wrong and presupposes freedom—only the free are obliged. The ought is a demand: “what is held to be required.”

When one is obliged is answered: on seeing the wrong. Why one is obliged—step 86.

**Step 35** *(from step 2)*. Logic is objective and always right: it is self-identical, stands by itself, and cannot deviate from itself. A distortion of logic is contradiction, illusion, the wrong.

**Step 36** *(from steps 29, 35)*. The ground: **the right (the rightful) is order, and order is an aspect of logic.** The right is the orderedness of a system (step 29); what is an aspect of logic is order as a law. The striving for order is the striving for logic; this is derived, not adopted as a value-axiom. The wrong is disorder, distortion, illusion.

The word “good” is absent from this formula: good is the polarity of intent (step 21), while the axis of correspondence carries “the right.”

**Step 37** *(from steps 29, 36)*. Order can be of different kinds: any arrangement of things is a definite order. **The true order** is the one that ought to be according to logic itself, objectively: the best one—the one that realizes the optimal roles and optimally places the elements in the roles. To carry the true order is to intend the best, not just any.

**Step 38** *(from steps 27, 37)*. The measure of trueness is agreement upward: the task of a system is itself a role in the encompassing system, and its rightness is measured by the same correspondence one level higher—and so on up to the limit, logic itself.

An order that is efficient within its own task but breaks the encompassing one is not true. Trueness is the closing of the axis of rightness along the whole vertical, not a new axis.

**Step 39** *(from step 36)*. The way out of the “is—ought” gap: “right” is rooted in logic, and logic is at once the structure of being and the command to be non-contradictory: a movement toward “*A* is not *A*” is a movement toward non-being. Therefore ought follows from being.

This is the load-bearing move and the position of this derivation: in philosophy it is disputed; here it rests on reasoning from supports 1 and 2.

**Step 40** *(from steps 33, 34, 36)*. The placement: the right is primary; benefit is the fruit of the right; the ought is the command of the right. The move is the reverse of consequentialism: the right is primary and bears benefit. A practical consequence: restitution is for an error, punishment only for guilt (step 63).

What sets this derivation apart from Kantian deontology is the foundation—correspondence to the order of being; in Kant the law is given to itself by a rational will—the autonomy of the will. Objectivism under fallible knowledge demands humility in application. The primacy of the right is the position of this derivation; there is no general agreement of philosophers about it.

## Status: Two Values and Degrees

**Step 41** *(from steps 14, 32)*. The core: status is two-valued—exactly “right” and “wrong”; the values exclude each other, and there is no neutral third.

On the decidable fragment this is proved constructively ([[Formalization]]).

**Step 42** *(from step 41)*. There is no “neutral zone”: apparent neutrality is one of two things: several options on the side of the right (step 46) or a choice outside the domain of the polarity (step 43). The values of status are two.

**Step 43** *(from steps 10, 29)*. The boundary of the domain: the polarity is switched on if and only if the existing order is at stake; where order is not touched, there is no status. This is the absence of a question, not a third value: ethics does not concern every choice. The status of a choice is assigned according to the system in which the question is posed.

To establish whether order is at stake is a task of recognition (the [[1 Recognition|first domain]] of reasoning), not the verdict itself.

**Step 44** *(from steps 31, 32)*. Status passes through two stages: (a) the sharp threshold of sufficient reason—the correspondence passes the threshold, the candidate is in the pool; (b) gradual height—above the threshold the best is discernible and attainable. The sharp and the gradual are two stages of one axis, not two values.

**Step 45** *(from step 44)*. Selection proceeds in one of two ways. The first is the best for this role taken by itself. The second is “to measure”: the least deviation from the yardstick, since an excess over the goal takes an element away from a system with higher demands—so selection takes into account the distribution of elements among systems. Which way is fitting is decided by the sufficient reason of the encompassing order; there is no single way for all cases.

**Step 46** *(from steps 5, 38, 44, 45)*. Several equally best is a tie in selection: this is the breadth of the side of the right, as distinct from its height. What decides the tie depends on the context—whether other systems lay claim to the same elements. Where other systems do not need the element, or it can stand in both at once, all the options of the tie are right and the choice among them is free: the rules do not dictate a single one. Where the roles are incompatible—an element occupied in one system will no longer go to the other—a tie within one system is not yet a tie in the encompassing one: the options differ in what each leaves to the other system, and the right one turns out to be the option under which both receive what fits them (steps 38 and 45); when that does not work out, the encompassing system decides. Where the order of assignment is given, the place decides: the status goes to the first that fits ([[Order#Order and the Assignment of Statuses|Order]]).

Systems intersect in elements: one element can occupy roles in many systems ([[Theory of Systems#The Interaction of Systems|the interaction of systems]]). Therefore the question “is the choice free” is asked together with two others: who else needs this element, and are the roles compatible.

> [!example]- Case: what to take a cup with
> Right is what leads to the desired result (steps 16, 32). Before the question “which hand?” comes the question “what to take it with?” Taking it with a foot did not work—the cup broke: so it was wrong. Hence it is right to take it with a hand. Which hand—right or left—is already the element on the role (steps 30, 46). There are exceptions: a person without hands can master a foot as a hand—then for that person the role of the hand is played by the element foot, and for that person this is right. Order in the system “taking a cup” is a matter of the role (what does the taking—the function of the hand) and of the element (with what exactly).

**Step 47** *(from steps 41, 44)*. The gradualness of the polarity is a family of scales: the height of selection, the depth of the wrong, harm, development; there is no single measure. The main thing is two independent axes: the **kind**—the intention, which is binary—and the **depth of distortion**, which is gradual: a deep honest error and a shallow lie differ on both axes at once.

The independence of the axes is proved ([[Formalization]]); on it the two kinds of responsibility stand below.

## The Operator and Virtue

**Step 48** *(from steps 5, 6)*. The operator is a free chooser; ethical choice takes place in it. The will is the potential between the known and the knowable; free will is the choice of direction and the choice of response (step 6), and on the side of the system what answers to it is underdetermination by the frame (step 5). Ethics lives in the domain of this freedom; its subject is the choice of response (step 6).

The full analysis of the will is in [[Metaphysics#The Operator and the Will|Metaphysics]]; for ethics three features suffice: freedom, attention as its manifestation, inalienability (step 50).

**Step 49** *(from steps 21, 47, 48)*. The moral line is composed of three: the objective status, the orientation of the will, and the action. The status is often closed to knowledge—therefore the moral weight falls on the intention. The intention decides: directed toward truth, it yields an error when it misses; directed against the will of another for one’s own ends, evil (step 21). Evil is chosen freely; it never comes by unnoticed drift.

**Step 50** *(from step 48)*. Freedom is inalienable: the freedom not to do—even under coercion refusal remains, as long as it is the operator that acts—and the freedom to acknowledge: the turn toward truth is available at any step.

**Step 51** *(from steps 26, 36, 48)*. Virtue toward oneself is a stable active disposition: to keep the will to the measure of the objective—of truth, of order—and to carry this out in deed, paying the price: the pain of dismantling the false. Virtue is the stable closing of the gap of akrasia (step 26); vice is its mirror: the disposition to bend the will toward private gain, refusing to pay the price of truth.

A disposition is the limit of repeated choices (steps 75, 78).

**Step 52** *(from steps 2, 51)*. Virtue toward another has three parts: the demonstration of logic—to show the rules without imposing conclusions; the absence of pressure on another’s will; the protection of those who are pressured. The ground: the way of logic is to show itself by being itself; therefore its bearer too shows and does not impose.

The proposition “shows and does not impose” rests on reasoning; it has no machine check. In the Code these are the obligations of the philosopher ([[Code of Ethics]]).

**Step 53** *(from steps 21, 52)*. Imposition is the binding of another’s freedom: the other’s free knowing is replaced by an alien will. It contradicts the very way of logic: one who imposes does not represent logic—such a one is an aggressor.

This is step 21 in the domain of knowing: an action against the will of another for one’s own ends.

**Step 54** *(from steps 52, 53)*. The problem of sophistry: since the right stands on logic, what remains is to tell logic from its counterfeit, and the chief danger is sincere self-deception. The defense is knowledge of logic: what it is and what it is not; therefore ethics is bound up with the knowledge of [[Logic|logic]].

The instrument of defense is the architecture of reasoning: the [[Domains|domains]] and the [[Fallacy Catalog|catalog of fallacies]]. Recognition is fallible—constant vigilance is needed.

## Evil: Origin and Status

**Step 55** *(from steps 3, 21)*. Evil is instrumental: an action against the will of another is chosen as a means to one’s own ends; no one desires evil as evil.[^3] There is no evil for evil’s sake: a will to “*A* is not *A*” as a final end would be a will to non-being, and non-being cannot be desired as an end.[^4] All evil is parasitic on a supposed benefit for oneself.

**Step 56** *(from steps 21, 47)*. The place of evil is the intention: evil is determined by the kind of intent; the depth of the miss is a measure of knowing. A subtlety: one can recognize the truth—and refuse it by will; this is a choice, not a miss. Such a refusal becomes evil when it is turned against the will of another for one’s own ends; toward oneself it is harm (step 58).

**Step 57** *(from steps 21, 33)*. Two stages, two axes: the distortion is primary—the will chooses against; the harm is secondary—the suffering of the one who undergoes it. The axes diverge: harm can be right—the surgeon, just retribution: harm for the sake of order. Causing harm does not make one evil; refraining from harm does not make one good.

This is the axis of step 20—benefit and harm—meeting the axis of intent.

**Step 58** *(from steps 20, 21)*. Evil lives between operators—it needs another. Toward oneself there is not evil but harm: untruth toward oneself spoils the reliability of one’s own judgment and opens the spiral (step 59); it becomes evil when it is turned against the will of another for one’s own ends (step 21). The threshold of guilt and punishment is an action that touches another; without it there is responsibility before oneself.

**Step 59** *(from steps 51, 58)*. The origin of evil has four steps: an error—not yet evil; the pain of realization—the dismantling of a mental construction, familiar to everyone; the lie as an avoidance of pain, addressed to another—here the error turns into evil; the spiral of lies: a new lie hides the previous one. Each turn of the spiral is a free choice, but the spiral diverges.

The lie is the place where error turns into evil; the course of the spiral is closed by steps 60 and 79.

**Step 60** *(from steps 50, 59)*. The way out is acknowledgment at any step: the freedom to acknowledge is inalienable, and the turn toward truth is always possible. Acknowledgment stops the spiral and leads out of guilt; the depth traversed and the harm done remain a fact.

**Step 61** *(from steps 21, 59)*. The ontological status of evil: it is positively real—a real choice, illusion as content, a real spiral; it has being—what it lacks is correspondence. Both extremes are rejected: “evil is simply nothing” and evil as a substance. Evil is a parasite on order: to distort, there must be an order; it is a movement toward “*A* is not *A*” that does not reach non-being.

Language in places says the same: etymologists link the Russian *zloj* “evil” with Old Indic *hvárati* and Avestan *zbaraiti*—words for a crooked, winding gait; the English *evil*, by one hypothesis, meant “overstepping the measure.”[^5] Evil is named as a deviation from the straight.

## Responsibility

**Step 62** *(from steps 43, 48)*. Responsibility is freedom made accountable. It exists if and only if: (1) the choice is free—one whose body is moved by another’s force does not answer: the ground of the act is not in that one (steps 50 and 66); (2) the consequences carry a status—the existing order is at stake; hence (3) there is an affected witness. The relation is three-place: who, for what, before whom—oneself included.

It is proved that one who is coerced is not responsible ([[Formalization]]).

**Step 63** *(from steps 47, 57, 62)*. Responsibility is of two kinds, and they are independent—like the kind of intent and the depth of distortion (step 47): **restitution** is for an error: for the harm from a wrong choice—whether a miss or intent; **punishment** is for guilt, the distortion of intent: only where it is present. An honest error with harm: restitution—yes, punishment—no. Evil with harm—both. Harm for the sake of order—the surgeon, just retribution (step 57)—requires no restitution.

This is the practical consequence of step 40, now derived in full.

**Step 64** *(from steps 28, 63)*. Honest ignorance—the intention to know, with an object whose being unknown could not itself be known—removes guilt; restitution remains. The duty to find out covers what can be found out; one who neglects it is guilty. Diligence distinguishes the kinds of responsibility; it does not become a third kind.

**Step 65** *(from steps 27, 64)*. The rules are knowable: they are reconstructed from the elements and the roles together with the goal of the system—or else grasped by insight. A “closed” status is one not yet derived: it too is knowable; therefore the duty to find out grows stronger, and the domain of honest ignorance narrows to what is truly not yet accessible.

**Step 66** *(from steps 50, 62, 63)*. The degrees are separate for the two kinds. Punishment measures the doer: freedom; access to knowledge—it enters into effective freedom; entrenchment—it aggravates: earlier free choices answer for the forming of a disposition. Restitution measures what was done: the extent of the harm caused; there is no general law of restitution. Under coercion the guilt passes to the one who coerces in the measure of the coercion: as long as it is the operator that acts, refusal remains with the operator (step 50).

**Step 67** *(from steps 36, 58, 62)*. There are several instances, and they go by levels: before oneself—inner responsibility; to the one affected—restitution, horizontally; to the senior system—punishment: the victim is not the judge, otherwise there is revenge and a new spiral; the ultimate instance is logic itself, and particular instances are approximations of its verdict.

**Step 68** *(from steps 59, 67)*. The consequences are built into the structure itself: untruth by itself undermines the reliability of judgment and locks the spiral—evil punishes itself; uncorrected harm is a lasting distortion: it does not mend by itself, and a free act of restoration is needed. The asymmetry is exact: evil punishes itself, harm does not make restitution for itself. An institution is secondary—and it is just if and only if its verdict coincides with the verdict of order itself.

**Step 69** *(from steps 60, 63)*. Repentance is acknowledgment, a turn of the will toward truth: it removes punishment—the evil orientation is no longer there, and there is nothing to punish; restitution remains: the fact has happened and is irreversible. The asymmetry is derived from the two kinds of responsibility (step 63).

## Justice

**Step 70** *(from steps 29, 58)*. Justice is the same order where there are many operators: society as a system, operators in the roles according to the rules. Injustice is disorder at the level of society: arbitrariness, the absence of a filter, non-reciprocity; it has no separate essence.

**Step 71** *(from steps 31, 70)*. The first layer is roles by fitness. Analysis gives the threshold of safety—the minimum for entry; within the pool it is the operator’s pull that chooses, and “to measure” is born of the unfolding of potential in the work itself: it is the outcome of the path, not a condition of entry. A just arrangement is the threshold of safety, the freedom of pull, and the conditions for unfolding.

**Step 72** *(from step 70)*. The second layer is the network of mutual provision: a role with a function requires conditions that it may not itself have—a group that has the capacity or the resource is obliged to give another the conditions for its function. Order is dynamic—it is a living network; injustice is non-reciprocity, the breaking of it. The bounds of the duty: only what is needed for the function; mutually; by capacity, not by estate.

The bound of the duty: “ought” presupposes “can”—the duty lies on the one who has the capacity. The ground of the duty: the refusal cannot be made a general rule—the maxim “the one who has refuses” destroys the functions themselves.

**Step 73** *(from steps 34, 72)*. Particular duties: order requires analysis, and analysis requires capacity; the capable are obliged to carry order, the holders of a resource to give the capable their conditions; the two duties close on each other. “Philosopher” and “elite” are roles by capacity, not estates; the duty repeats at every level.

In the Code these are the obligations of the philosopher and the step of reciprocity ([[Code of Ethics]]).

**Step 74** *(from steps 67, 68, 70)*. The criterion of the justice of an institution: an institution is just if and only if its verdict coincides with the verdict of order itself; an unjust law itself distorts order. The administration of justice exists because the victim is not the judge: punishment goes upward from the victim.

The social arrangement read off from these steps is unfolded in the branch [[Social Systems]].

## The Path and Wisdom

**Step 75** *(from steps 48, 56)*. The path is the operator in time: the filling of a structure with truth or with untruth; the mechanism of filling is one—the content differs. The axes are independent: technique—how one fills; will—with what. The field has four corners: the wise; the sophist; the truthful simpleton; the inept deceiver; the ethical core is the axis of the will. Both images are carried by the words with which the traditions name this domain: habit become disposition (Greek ἦθος, Russian *nrav*) and the path one walks (*halakha*, *dào*)—the section “Etymology.”

**Step 76** *(from steps 23, 75)*. Wisdom in action is to find truth rightly: the unity of both axes around truth. Two arguments by contraposition: technique without the will to truth is the sophist—there is no truth; will without technique is a good intention without a result. There is no need to divide wisdom into “ethical” and “epistemic”: these are two necessary conditions of one. Wisdom closes the root question (step 23) as the stable capacity to answer it.

**Step 77** *(from steps 4, 76)*. Wisdom is a state of the knower, not a store of knowledge: the wise see the root of a system, know the common form of structure—the triad, are aware of the limits of their own knowledge, command the direction of deliberation, and know the rules of truth. Hence it carries over from one domain to another ([[Philosophy#Wisdom|Wisdom]]).

**Step 78** *(from step 75)*. A disposition is the limit of repeated choices, not a thing. Wisdom has both a degree and a limit: the degree belongs to the wholeness of what is understood, the limit to completeness; the present degree is possessed; toward completeness one goes.

**Step 79** *(from steps 51, 60)*. Restoration goes in two phases: the turn of the will—acknowledgment, which is instantaneous; the technical work—the dismantling of the illusion and the filling with truth anew, which is a process. It is always possible, there is no point of no return, but the price grows with the depth; the barrier is the pain of dismantling, and to bear it is a virtue. What is restored is the disposition, not the history; the scar enters the disposition as experience—the wisdom of one who has recovered.

**Step 80** *(from steps 43, 66)*. The boundary of the theme: the mechanism by which a disposition forms is a descriptive question and belongs to psychology; the nature of a character that remains itself, to metaphysics. What remains to ethics: the status of a disposition, the responsibility for forming it, and the command to keep the disposition to measure.

## How the Right Is Learned

**Step 81** *(from steps 17, 27)*. The hierarchy of rules: every system has its own; the senior ones set the frame for the nested ones. Ethics is relative to the role: what obliges are the rules of the system in whose role the operator acts. Where a rule is not specified, a participant ascends to the senior system, and a creator lays it down.

**Step 82** *(from step 81)*. There is no conflict of levels: an action unfolds in one system, and a “conflict” is an error of recognizing in which one. The passage to the senior system is determined by restorability: an action has gone out into the senior system when its scale has exceeded the restorative capacity of the junior one; the tension between neighboring systems is resolved by ascending to their common encompassing system.

**Step 83** *(from steps 28, 65)*. The channels for finding out the rules: observation, inference, tradition, trial with feedback, intuition. Inference serves as the check: the rules are reconstructed from the elements and the roles together with the goal of the system (step 65); the other channels bring hypotheses. Intuition brings the whole at once, and inference unfolds and checks it ([[Intuition]]). There is no separate “moral organ”—what works is general knowability applied to the rules; the striving to know is a duty.

**Step 84** *(from step 83)*. Conscience is a type of intuitive response to the status “right—wrong.” The word—Latin *conscientia*, like the Church Slavonic *sovest’* that Russian inherited—is a calque of Greek συνείδησις (syneidēsis): “knowing-with,” knowledge shared with another or with oneself; here it is read as knowledge in accord with the objective order. Conscience has two sides: the orientation toward truth—given before experience—and the content of the response—which is fallible. Conscience obliges at once, yet it is fallible and can be formed—therefore to form it is a duty; in the wise the response is exact: what has ripened comes whole.

**Step 85** *(from steps 32, 83)*. Given rules differ from objective ones: what is accepted—tradition, enacted law—is fallible and changes; a given rule is valid if and only if it corresponds to the objective order. Moral progress is the convergence of the given rules toward the objective ones: without relativism and without raising the accepted to an absolute; the critique of the accepted is a duty, a consequence of the duty to know.

## Obligation, Boundaries, the Open

**Step 86** *(from steps 2, 34, 59, 60, 68)*. Why the free are obliged by order. Logic stands by itself: content that accords with what is, is held by the connected field—by what is itself. An illusion has no roots in what is—it is held only by continuous effort and concealment: one stop, one check—and it falls, and every new lie widens the area that has to be held. Therefore obligation is **a condition of one’s own path**, not an external law with a sanction: to will against order is to tear up one’s own road to the essence. Acknowledgment restores the root: the hidden ground is shown, the road is returned.

This closes the question of step 34: when one is obliged—on seeing the wrong; why—the path against order destroys the one who walks it. The same is said of truth: it requires no effort, it is held by what is itself ([[Truth and Absolute Truth#Untruth and the Lie|“Untruth and the Lie”]]).

**Step 87** *(the boundaries of the whole chain)*. There are four boundaries. (1) Structure is not yet obligation: the machine checks decidable relations, while “good is good” is not a theorem; normativity rests on reasoning (steps 39 and 86). (2) The primacy of the right and objectivism are the position of this derivation: it is coherent, but there is no general agreement of philosophers about it. (3) Objectivism under fallible knowledge demands humility in application. (4) The proposition “shows and does not impose” rests on reasoning. The whole chain stands on the will; its derivation is given in [[Metaphysics#The Operator and the Will|Metaphysics]].

**Step 88** *(from steps 48, 75)*. A bridge to artificial intelligence: ethics is defined over any free operator, whatever its bearer; an AI is a particular case, if it is a free chooser. Technical safety does not equal alignment: a technically flawless act may carry a will to untruth—a sophist AI; alignment requires both axes—technique and the orientation toward truth. Responsibility is decided by the same: a free AI answers for itself; for an unfree one its creators answer, as under coercion; for an honest AI—restitution, not punishment. Whether an AI has a will is an open question.

It is proved that a technically safe yet deceiving operator exists in the model ([[Formalization]]).

**Step 89** *(from steps 87, 88)*. The open and the deferred. One thing remains open: whether an AI has a will (step 88). Deferred to their own analysis: the kinds of evil, the details of restoration, the typology of virtues, the theory of the channels by which rules are known. The chain is closed on the boundaries named: it can be changed only by changing a premise or a step.

## Intention and the Lie

The analysis of the act of will ([[Question]]) gives ethics exact addresses. **Intention is the goal and the direction of an act**: the goal is the result the operator wants—it is from this side that intention is taken in step 16; the direction is where attention is turned; the occasion is the active ground; the act is the path. The distortion of intent (step 63) is read by addresses: either the goal is replaced—what is sought is not truth but victory, gain, appearance—or the direction is led away: attention is turned from what was recognized.

**Untruth and the lie are set apart.** Untruth is a status of content: “is not the truth”; it requires no intent—an honest error is possible. The lie is a status of the act: the will is deliberately directed at untruth. The lie has a double structure of root: **the root of the act is always there**—a motivating ground—while **there is no root of the content**: no path of grounds leads from what is to what is asserted. The projection itself is legitimate—it is a thought-form of the layer “can be, but is not,” where hypotheses and designs live.

The lie is committed by **the assignment of a status**: the possible is given the status of what is—without grounds. And the lie has an addressee: its direction is another witness, “so that it be taken for the truth”; this is work over logic—in the structure of illusion, a projection onto the structure of the field. The objective field remains whole: what is, is; what is damaged is a record—another witness’s and the liar’s own: a false ground is placed into another’s acts. The sophist hides both: the root of the act—the motive—and the absence of a root in the content ([[Truth and Absolute Truth#Untruth and the Lie|“Untruth and the Lie”]]).

**Evil does not erase truth—it turns away.** Stating what is, is an act of the witness not subject to the will, and records are kept: there is no destruction. The refusal of truth is the leading of attention away from what was witnessed. Leading attention away from what was witnessed is also an answer to experience: a choice of response, made by attention. And the inalienable core of freedom—the freedom not to do, the freedom to acknowledge—stands on the will itself: the scale of the will does not go below one, and this minimum can be taken away only together with being ([[Metaphysics#The Operator and the Will|Metaphysics]]).

## Egoism: An Orientation toward the Record

**Egoism is an orientation:** the Ego zone—the region of the witness’s records about itself—stands stably at the center of attention as a goal. The word is exact: the French *égoïsme* (eighteenth century) is formed from Latin *ego* “I” with the suffix *-ism*, which turns the occasional into the systematic. The representation of oneself may itself be true or false ([[Metaphysics#The Ego Zone|the Ego zone]]); egoism is defined by the place of this representation: it is placed at the center of attention—true or false. By this egoism also differs from the lie: the lie is a status of a single act in which the place of truth is taken by something else; egoism is the orientation out of which such acts grow.

**In the matrix of intent, egoism is an orientation, not a cell.** It sets the side “for one’s own ends”—and by itself is not yet evil: exchange with the other’s consent is legitimate. It becomes evil when action against the will of another is added to it.

**The boundary with care for oneself is replacement.** Care is legitimate: truth remains at the center of attention, and the person is a part of the order that ought to be cared for. In egoism the Ego zone takes the place of truth. The boundary runs through the center of attention, not through whom the act benefits: two acts may serve the doer equally—and one will be care, the other egoism.

**Egoism serves the record, not the witness.** The Ego zone is records; records do not will—the witness wills. Placing the image at the center, the egoist puts the will in the service of what can neither want nor answer: it is the image that is served, not being. Hence its self-destructiveness: the will is spent on an ego-agent that does not exist ([[What Is Not and Cannot Be]]).

**The pair “egoism—altruism” is false.** Altruism as an orientation places another’s zone of records at the center—the same replacement of truth, only on a different bearer. The opposite of egoism is the orientation toward truth and toward the order in which both the person and the other stand in their places.

**The operator’s egoism is the root of injustice.** The egoist assigns roles and statuses by what is advantageous to its image, not by fitness: the assignment is distorted—status diverges from fitness (step 70).

**Egoism blinds.** The image must stand—and the records about oneself cease to be updated, while the person changes. A frozen record diverges without limit from how things are: the representation of oneself becomes an illusion, the illusion of knowing oneself grows, and self-destruction proceeds through not knowing oneself.

## Summary

**The derivation in one sentence: a free operator, discerning the status of correspondence to the objective order, chooses to measure: by an intention directed toward truth—not against the will of another for one’s own ends; manifesting order by demonstration, not by imposition; bearing responsibility—restitution for an error, punishment only for guilt; and growing in wisdom along the path of filling oneself with truth.** There are seven load-bearing axes.

1. **The ground.** The right (the rightful) is order, and order is an aspect of logic. Ethics is derived from “*A* exists”; ought follows from being, and the free are obliged by order: the path against order tears up one’s own road to the essence.
2. **The axis of correspondence.** Right means corresponding, and this is primary: benefit is the fruit of the right, the ought is its command. Status is two-valued; laws are unbreakable, rules are constructions of a system and can be broken; a wrong choice violates the existing order. There are three polarities, on three bearers: right—wrong, good—evil, benefit—harm.
3. **Freedom.** Ethics exists only where a wrong choice is possible; its subject is the choice of response, and the moral weight is carried by intention. Freedom is inalienable: not to do, and to acknowledge.
4. **The relation to another.** Evil is an action against the will of another for one’s own ends; good is an action for the sake of another, in accord with the other’s will and without personal gain, out of repulsion from the wrongness of not doing. Toward oneself there is not evil but harm. Responsibility is of two kinds: restitution for an error, punishment for guilt; justice is the same order where there are many operators.
5. **The way.** Logic shows itself and does not impose: virtue toward another is demonstration, the absence of pressure, protection.
6. **Discipline.** The rules are knowable: they are reconstructed from the elements and the roles together with the goal of the system—or else grasped by insight. Inference serves as the check; the defense against the counterfeit of logic is the architecture of reasoning; conscience is an intuitive response, fallible and formable.
7. **The path.** The filling of oneself with truth; the path has two independent axes—technique and will. A disposition is the limit of repeated choices; wisdom in action is to find truth rightly; restoration begins with acknowledgment.

The derivation is 89 steps on six supports. Its application is the [[Code of Ethics]]: the roles of the Path of Wisdom and their obligations.
