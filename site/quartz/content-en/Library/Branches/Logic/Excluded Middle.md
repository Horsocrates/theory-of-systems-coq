The Law of Excluded Middle: ***A ∨ ¬A*** · ***tertium non datur***

**The boundary divides everything without remainder: there are two options—*A* or not-*A*—and no third.**

The law completes the demarcation given by the two primary laws. [[Identity]] gives stability: *A* is *A*. [[Non-Contradiction]] gives exclusivity: *A* and not-*A* stand on opposite sides of the boundary. Together they leave no room for anything in between: of such a thing one could say neither that it is *A* nor that it is not-*A*. Excluded Middle makes this completion explicit.

## Etymology

**Excluded middle.** The Latin formula is *tertium non datur*, “no third is given”; the law’s name is *principium exclusi tertii*: *excludere* means “to shut out,” from *claudere*, “to shut.” English “middle” follows the Latin variant *exclusi medii*; Aristotle’s word is μεταξύ (metaxy), “between”: between contradictories there is nothing. The word carries the thesis of this page: the boundary leaves no room between its sides.

## Place among the Laws

Among the five laws ([[Logic]]), Excluded Middle is a **derived** law: a stable distinction ([[Identity]]) with mutually exclusive sides ([[Non-Contradiction]]) leaves no room for a third. Its property is *exhaustiveness*: the boundary divides everything into *A* and not-*A* without remainder. “Neither *A* nor not-*A*” would be something that satisfies neither the criterion for *A* nor the criterion for not-*A*; but not-*A* is defined precisely as everything that does not satisfy the criterion for *A*. Identity and Non-Contradiction are the primary laws; [[Sufficient Reason]] and [[Order]] are meta-laws.

The formula *A* ∨ ¬*A* is read here as a list of options: *A* or not-*A*, and no third. Two theses stand under the name of the law. **Binarity**—“no third is given”: there are two options, *A* or not-*A*. Exhaustiveness follows from it: the two options cover the whole field of what can be. Binarity is derived—also where the assignment has not yet taken place; this is proved ([[Formalization]]). **Totality**—“the assignment has always already happened”: as if every thing were already assigned to one of the options. It does not follow from binarity: two options do not say which of them has come out, or whether either has. Assignment is an action, a separate process, and it answers to [[Sufficient Reason]]: why it was *A* that came out. Options may remain unassigned; totality is therefore a mode of reckoning, not a thesis about the world. One reckons so about what is present—where the assignment has taken place; the formalization adopts this mode explicitly, as a separate assumption.

## History

The law was formulated by Aristotle: there can be nothing intermediate between contradictory statements; of one thing we must either affirm or deny any one thing.[^1] He was also the first to see what the law does not say: which of the two options will come out. Tomorrow there will be a sea battle or there will not—this is necessary; but neither the one nor the other, taken separately, is yet necessary.[^2] The distinction “will be or will not be” is already binary today; the assignment takes place tomorrow. Leibniz set the three principles side by side—identity, contradiction, and the absence of a middle between the true and the false; they became the triad of “laws of thought” in the school logic of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.[^3] The triad took shape from the standpoint of the rules of human thinking. From the ontological standpoint the third law stands differently: it completes the demarcation given by the first two.

## What the Law Makes Possible

In being, the law makes **difference** possible. A difference is drawn without remainder: for everything that can be there are two options—*A* or not-*A*. In knowing, three things stand on this.

**Question.** The question “*A* or not-*A*?” is well posed: the answer is one of the two options. A question that admits a third outcome is imprecisely posed: its sides have not yet been brought to *A* and not-*A*.

**Division.** Classification without remainder: every object of the domain falls either under *A* or under not-*A*.

**Negation.** “Not-*A*” is a determinate side, not a void: in negating, too, we say something determinate.

## Brouwer’s Objection

At the beginning of the twentieth century the Dutch mathematician Luitzen Egbertus Jan Brouwer challenged Excluded Middle. Brouwer was one of the greatest mathematicians of the century: one of the founders of modern topology and the author of the celebrated fixed-point theorem.[^4] In his dissertation *On the Foundations of Mathematics* (1907) he laid the groundwork for intuitionism, and a year later, in the article “The Unreliability of the Logical Principles,” he attacked the law itself for the first time.

For Brouwer mathematics is “an essentially languageless activity of the mind” having its origin in the perception of time. Mathematical objects are not discovered in a Platonic world of ideas; they are constructed by consciousness. A statement is true only if we can prove it, and false only if we can refute it; where there is neither proof nor refutation, it has no truth value at all. Hence the rejection of Excluded Middle for infinite domains. Goldbach’s conjecture—every even number greater than two is the sum of two primes—has to this day been neither proved nor refuted; and for the intuitionist the statement “Goldbach’s conjecture is true or false” has no force until there is a proof or a refutation.

Brouwer’s objection was opposed by David Hilbert—perhaps the most influential mathematician of his time. From his chair in Göttingen he set the agenda of a whole era: his twenty-three problems, announced in 1900, gave mathematics its direction for decades, and his work transformed geometry, number theory, analysis, and even the foundations of physics. Hilbert understood mathematics as a formal system: signs and axioms from which theorems are derived, with the reliability of the whole secured by a proof of consistency. To formalize all of classical mathematics and to prove its consistency once and for all by finite, surveyable means—this program he regarded as his life’s work.

That is why intuitionism was for him not a philosophical subtlety but a direct threat. Giving up Excluded Middle cut away a huge part of classical mathematics: non-constructive existence proofs, work with Cantor’s completed infinities, whole branches of analysis. “From the paradise that Cantor created for us, no one shall be able to drive us out,” Hilbert replied,[^5] and Excluded Middle was for him the very air of that paradise. Hence the famous retort: “To take this *tertium non datur* away from the mathematician would be like forbidding the astronomer the telescope or the boxer the use of his fists.”[^6]

> [!note]- The Dispute and Its Price
> The dispute grew into personal enmity. In 1928 Hilbert, ill and fearing for the fate of the leading mathematical journal of the era, secured Brouwer’s removal from the editorial board of *Mathematische Annalen*—an episode that Einstein, also a member of the board, mockingly called a “war of the frogs and the mice” and in which he refused to take part. Brouwer, shaken, fell almost silent. And there is an irony in his fate: in his later years he acknowledged that his own topological results lacked an intuitionistic foundation, since they rest on Excluded Middle. Philosophical consistency outweighed mathematical fame.

Behind the outward sharpness stood a genuine question: what makes a mathematical statement true. For Brouwer, it is our ability to construct it; for Hilbert, its derivability in a consistent formal system. Intuitionism was no mere formal exercise: it offered a different ontology of mathematics and gave rise to a whole school—constructive logic.

## The Reply

Two theses stand under the name of the law, and Brouwer’s objection bears on them differently.

**Binarity is untouched by the objection.** A distinction has two sides; there is no third even where the assignment has not yet taken place. The intuitionist does not introduce a third either: for him “neither true nor false” means “not yet assigned,” not a third value.

**On totality we agree with Brouwer.** For any particular even number the question is decidable: the check is finite, and its outcome is determined by the structure of numbers. “For all even numbers” is a statement about an unbounded process: it has no finite check. Infinity as a completed domain does not exist: **infinity is a process, not a collection**;[^7] so the answer does not lie ready in it either. Assignment is a separate action, and it answers to Sufficient Reason. The ground here can be one of two things: a reason in the very structure of numbers why every even number is a sum of two primes, or a counterexample somewhere in their series. Proof and search trace this ground; they do not create it. Where there is neither, there is no assignment: the question has two sides, and neither is occupied.

We part ways on two points. The first is truth. The intuitionist calls our *knowledge* truth: what is true is what has been constructed. Here **truth is logic**: what is true is what accords with the structure of what exists, and that structure is logic ([[Truth and Absolute Truth]]). The false departs from it: it says of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is.[^8] Truth precedes knowing: a proof traces it. The ground of the answer, whichever it is, lies in the structure of numbers, not in the mathematician’s mind. The second is number. For Brouwer, numbers are built by the mind. Here number derives from the act of distinction: to distinguish *A* from not-*A* is to obtain a unit; counting is the repetition of units.[^9]

Intuitionism shows where a constructive procedure falls short of the scope of a statement, and it warns against completed infinity. It does not reject the primary laws, Identity and Non-Contradiction: it is on them that it proves its theorems.

## Violations

In reality the law is unbreakable; it is broken in thinking: the contrary is taken for the contradictory, or a middle is sought where there is none.

*[[Either Or Reasoning|False dilemma]].* “You are either with us or against us” is a rhetorical device that artificially narrows the choice: between “with us” and “against us” there are many positions—neutrality, partial agreement, indifference. The law itself says something else: between “with us” and “*not* with us” there is no third. “Against us” is only one of the forms of “not with us.”

*A sham third.* Between “yes” and “no” a middle is sought: “the truth lies somewhere in the middle.” Between contraries there can be a middle; between contradictories there is none.

Both errors are one confusion: the *contrary* is taken for the *contradictory*. The same confusion yields the spurious refutations of the law: a middle between contraries is passed off as a “third.”

## Summary

1. **The Law of Excluded Middle: *A* ∨ ¬*A***—there are two options and no third. Its property is exhaustiveness; the law is derived from Identity and Non-Contradiction.
2. **The law speaks of the options for what can be:** this is binarity. Assignment is a separate action; it answers to Sufficient Reason. Totality—“the assignment has always already happened”—is a mode of reckoning, not a thesis about the world.
3. **It makes difference possible:** a difference is drawn without remainder. In knowing, three things stand on it: question, division, and negation.
4. **Brouwer’s objection leaves binarity untouched.** We agree with Brouwer that infinity is a process and that no ready answer lies in it; we part ways on truth and on the nature of number.
5. **In reality the law is unbreakable.** In thinking it is violated by the false dilemma and the sham third.

The demarcation is complete: the distinction is stable, exclusive, and exhaustive. A question of another level remains—*why* it was *A* that came out. This is the question of the meta-law: [[Sufficient Reason]].
