Philosophy is the love of wisdom: the discipline that brings knowledge into accord with what is.

## Etymology

**Philosophy.** Greek φιλοσοφία (philosophia) is composed of φιλία (philia), “love, friendship”—from φιλέω (phileō), “I love”—and σοφία (sophia), “wisdom, skill.” The axis of meaning is **the love of wisdom**: the first part of the word, φιλ- (phil-), places a person in a relation of striving. Wisdom is not taken—one walks toward it. Cognates: from φιλ-, philology, philanthropy, bibliophile; from σοφ- (soph-), sophia, sophist, theosophy. The word itself is Greek and entered the languages of Europe whole, untranslated; where wisdom was called by a native word, the same subject is seen from another angle—Latin *sapientia*, “wisdom,” from *sapere*, “to taste, to discern” (whence *Homo sapiens*), and Sanskrit *darśana*, “seeing”—the name of Indian philosophy as a way of *seeing* what is.

According to tradition, the word was introduced by Pythagoras. Cicero and Diogenes Laertius relate his conversation with Leon, ruler of Phlius: asked who he was, Pythagoras answered with an unfamiliar word—“philosopher”—and explained it by comparing life to the games. Some come to the games to compete for a prize, others to trade; the best come as spectators—to watch, to understand, to appreciate. So it is in life: some are slaves of fame, others of gain; the philosopher observes and understands—he seeks absolute truth. And when Pythagoras was called a sage, he objected: only a god is wise; a human being can only love wisdom and strive toward it.[^1]

From this comes an old distinction. The word σοφιστής (sophistēs) meant “expert, master”; it acquired its bad sense in Athens at the end of the fifth century BCE, and that sense was fixed by Plato. The **sophist** lays claim to possession, posing as one who has wisdom and trading in its illusion. The **philosopher** acknowledges that wisdom cannot be appropriated; one can only be seized by it—as one is seized by love. So far this sounds like a legend about modesty. The analysis of the arrangement of the system will show that it is an exact reading of structure.

## Definition

**Philosophy is the discipline of bringing knowledge into accord with what is**; from the side of method, it is **the discipline of knowing reality through reasoning**. Both formulas are unfolded below: the first is the task of the system, the second is the same norm taken from the side of method. They stand here at the threshold so that the road can be seen.

## Philosophy as a System

An analysis of philosophy as a system, in the generative order: goal, rules, roles, elements ([[Theory of Systems]]).

- **The goal** is to bring [[Epistemology|knowledge]] into accord with what is. This is the task of the system, for the sake of which all the roles stand; the definition unfolds it, in the section “The Discipline of Absolute Truth.”

- **The rules** are [[Logic|the laws of logic]]: Identity, Non-Contradiction, Excluded Middle, Sufficient Reason, Order; every transition is made on grounds. And there is one supreme norm that holds the rest: **absolute truth above all**—first of all, above self-love.

- **The roles** are these: reality carries the role of the **object**—it is what is known, and every record is checked against what is; knowledge carries the role of the **growing corpus**, in which every record supports the rest; the philosopher carries two roles at once: the **witness** (who perceives) and the **[[Metaphysics|operator]]** (who directs attention by will, choosing the region to be known). The [[Question|question]] serves as the direction of each act; reasoning is the path along which the act moves step by step, on grounds.

- **The elements** are three: the **philosopher**—the observer in the full sense of [[Epistemology|the theory of knowledge]] (the witness; the channels of perception; the field of knowledge in which what is distinguished is held); **knowledge**—records obtained at the boundary of the known and bound together by understanding; **reality**—what is, the object toward which everything else is turned.

Wisdom, for its part, is a **quality** of the system: **the degree of wholeness of what is understood**—how far the records of the corpus are bound into a single understanding. How it is composed is the subject of the next section.

## Wisdom

**Wisdom is the wholeness of what is understood.** It is composed as follows.

A knower holds a thing in three ways: knows *what* it is; understands *why* it is so; and has the skill—knows *how*. Knowledge holds, understanding grasps the structure, skill is acquired by going through the thing oneself ([[Epistemology]]). In wisdom all three converge into one living whole. This is the same triple by which the laws of logic are set apart: *what*—Identity, Non-Contradiction, and Excluded Middle; *why*—Sufficient Reason; *how*—Order ([[Logic#The Unity of the Laws|the unity of the laws]]). Wisdom repeats in the knower the structure of logic itself.

**Wisdom is understanding.** Knowing-that is a record about a thing. Understanding links the thing with its structure: one sees why it is so and what it is connected with ([[Truth and Absolute Truth|the stages of representation]]). That is why wisdom is not reached by the knowing of absolute truth alone: that would be knowledge.

**What is understood links into a whole.** Separate understandings may still lie apart. The structure of every system is one in form—rules, roles, elements ([[Theory of Systems]])—and the “why” of one thing links with the “why” of another. Hence wisdom is a state of the knower, not a store of information. The wise see the root of a system, know the common form of structure, are aware of the limits of their own knowledge, command the direction of deliberation, and know the rules of truth ([[Ethics]]). Hence, too, wisdom carries over from one domain to another.

The corpus is filled along a path, and the path has two independent axes: technique—how it is filled—and will—with what. Wisdom rests on both at once—on the technique of working with data and on the will to truth; neither yields wisdom by itself. In ethics the same wisdom is taken from the side of action: to find truth rightly ([[Ethics]]).

Wholeness has **a limit by construction**: the corpus grows together with [[Epistemology|the field of the knowable]]; at every step only the finite is actual, while the next step always lies open ahead—completeness remains a limit toward which the corpus converges but which it reaches at no step. It is proved that there is no completed wisdom as a finished object ([[Formalization]]). The present degree can be possessed; completeness cannot: toward a limit only a path and love are possible.

Hence the objection of Pythagoras becomes a **theorem**, and the name becomes exact. Love is precisely the relation of a finite observer to this limit—the pull of the will. The will is the potential between the known and the knowable ([[Metaphysics]]): it creates the striving to fill the gap and is exhausted at no step. “The love of wisdom” is the structural name of the occupation. The distinction between the philosopher and the sophist becomes structural as well: the sophist passes off the present degree of understanding as completeness attained and trades in the illusion of wisdom; this is the substitution of the present for the limit. The philosopher knows that completeness is a limit, and so is the first to know their own limit, leaving room for not-knowing, without which no honest question is asked.

## The Philosopher

**The philosopher is one who loves wisdom.** The love of wisdom is expressed in **an attunement to absolute truth above all**. But the philosopher does not stop at knowing absolute truth—otherwise this would be knowledge: **wisdom is understanding**, a link with the structure itself and its interrelations ([[Truth and Absolute Truth|the stages of representation]]). Hence the philosopher is one who is able to analyze order and who carries wisdom. This is a role of capacity: it unfolds in doing, and fitness is the outcome of the path ([[Code of Ethics]]). The analysis of the system has given the philosopher’s portrait: two roles at once—the witness, who perceives, and the operator, who directs attention by will; wisdom is held as a degree of wholeness whose completeness is a limit; and so the philosopher is the first to know their own limit and leaves room for not-knowing. Philosophy is open to everyone who is ready to ask honestly.

**The sophist** is one who has the technique but lacks the will to truth ([[Ethics]]). At the center of the sophist’s attention stands not what is but the Ego zone—the records about oneself ([[Metaphysics|the Ego zone]]); egoism here is an orientation toward the record ([[Ethics#Egoism: An Orientation toward the Record|ethics]]). Hence the substitution: the sophist passes off the present degree of understanding as completeness and trades in the illusion of wisdom.

> [!note]- History: the path and the shift of meaning
>
> The first Greek philosophers—Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus, Parmenides—asked about ἀρχή (**archē**): the first principle of all that is. Out of what does everything arise, into what does it return, what remains unchanged behind the changeability of appearances? Thales held the first principle to be water; Anaximander, the apeiron (ἄπειρον), the boundless; Heraclitus, fire and the logos, the law of change; Parmenides maintained that only being truly exists, unchanging and one. The answers differed, but the question itself defined philosophy as the inquiry into the nature of things—into what lies at the base of appearances.
>
> Beside this search stood the mystery. In the forecourt of the temple at Delphi were written the sayings of the seven sages—“Know thyself,” “Nothing in excess”;[^2] here too the Pythia gave her oracles. For the Greeks there was no contradiction in this: reason and revelation were two paths to absolute truth. The chief of the sayings became the program of the life of a man who did not serve in the temple—and it was his thought that made Delphi a source of European philosophy.
>
> Socrates maintained that he knew only that he knew nothing. Behind this stands a story. His friend Chaerephon asked the Pythia whether anyone was wiser than Socrates; the oracle answered that no one was. Socrates was puzzled—he did not consider himself wise, and the god could not lie; so the answer had to be understood. He began to question those reputed to be wise—politicians, poets, craftsmen—and found that all of them think they know, but on examination turn out not to know; those skilled in their own craft imagine that they understand everything else as well. Thus the oracle was solved: Socrates is wiser in one thing—he knows of his own not-knowing.[^3] Out of the solution a method was born. Socrates wrote no treatises; his instrument was *dialogue*, a joint inquiry through questions. He called himself a midwife: he helped his interlocutor give birth to a thought and tested whether it was true—destroying the illusion of knowledge and leaving him at the point from which alone a genuine search is possible.[^4] A mystical riddle led to a philosophical method: the Pythia gave the riddle—dialogue became the way to solve it. The philosopher is first of all one who knows how to ask honestly.
>
> Plato gave the search an image that has become lasting. In the allegory of the cave people sit with their backs to the light and take the shadows on the wall for reality; the philosopher is the one who is freed from the fetters, turns toward the light, and goes out to see things as they are. The ascent goes by steps—from opinion to knowledge, from separate things to the structure that holds them, up to the Idea of the Good, the source of the being and knowability of everything—and for Plato philosophy is not a profession or an amusement of the mind but a path that changes the one who walks it. That is why, according to Plato, the one who sees the good should rule—and for that very reason does not seek power.[^5]
>
> Aristotle opened the *Metaphysics* with the words “All men naturally desire knowledge”—this desire is not a means but a manifestation of human nature itself. He also built a hierarchy of the sciences: physics studies nature, mathematics studies quantitative relations, but there is a science that investigates being as being—what is common to everything that exists. Aristotle called it **first philosophy**: it gives the other sciences their ground—the particular sciences take their premises as given, while philosophy investigates the premises themselves. The word “metaphysics” itself was born almost by accident: according to tradition, Andronicus of Rhodes, arranging Aristotle’s works, placed these treatises after the *Physics*—“those that come after the physics.”[^6]
>
> After Aristotle the emphasis shifted toward practice. The Stoics taught living in accord with nature and reason—accepting what is not in our power and perfecting what is; the Epicureans sought ataraxia (ἀταραξία)—tranquility, freedom from fear; the Skeptics practiced epochē (ἐποχή)—the suspension of judgment that leads to peace of mind. Philosophy here is a way of living: as Pierre Hadot showed, for the ancients to philosophize meant to exercise—in the discipline of the mind, in the control of the passions, in preparation for death—and the schools were communities of practitioners.[^7] All had one aim—*eudaimonia* (εὐδαιμονία), the good life; here it is read thus: **the state of a soul living in accord with absolute truth.**
>
> With the establishment of Christianity philosophy entered a new context: absolute truth is already given—in Revelation—and the thinker’s task became to understand and to ground what has been revealed. The formula *philosophia ancilla theologiae*—“philosophy is the handmaid of theology”—defined the age: the status was kept, but as a subordinate one. The summit of the synthesis was scholasticism: Thomas Aquinas integrated the pagan Aristotle into the Christian picture of the world—a risky project, since Aristotle taught the eternity of the world and knew nothing of creation out of nothing—and showed that the contradiction was only apparent: reason and revelation are two paths to one absolute truth. Philosophy remained the love of wisdom; wisdom itself was understood as divine, and the path to it as the path of faith, which reason assists.
>
> Greece is not the only source. In India six darśanas took shape—“visions,” ways of comprehending reality. *Nyāya* worked out a doctrine of the sources of valid knowledge—perception, inference, comparison, testimony; *Vaiśeṣika* proposed an atomism—a doctrine of indivisible particles that echoes Democritus; *Vedānta*, interpreting the Upaniṣads, asked about Ātman and Brahman—the identity of the innermost “I” and the first ground of everything (“Thou art That”). Outside the six schools stood *Buddhism*: it rejected an unchanging self—behind the stream of experiences there is no permanent subject. In China Confucius sought the restoration of order through the perfecting of the human being—*ren*, humaneness, realized in relations between people, and a nobility defined by the cultivation of the virtues rather than by birth; *Daoism* answered differently: the dao is the inexpressible first ground, and right action is *wu wei*, action without violence to the natural order, like water, which flows around an obstacle and overcomes stone.
>
> Islamic *falsafa* preserved and continued the Greek heritage: al-Fārābī, “the Second Teacher,” was the first to present philosophy in the Islamic world as an integral system; Ibn Sīnā, who by his own account read the *Metaphysics* forty times before he understood it thanks to a work of al-Fārābī’s bought by chance, distinguished essence and existence—a distinction that reached Aquinas; Ibn Rushd, “the Commentator,” returned Aristotle to Europe; Suhrawardī joined the discursive and the intuitive paths of knowing—the very ones treated on the page [[Intuition|Instruments of Cognition]].
>
> The methods and the answers differed; the questions converged: *what is real? what can be known? how should one live rightly?* The convergence of questions amid the difference of answers is a sign that the questions are born of the very structure of human existence, and that philosophy is turned toward one reality.
>
> The Renaissance, which is usually called the rebirth of antiquity, revived something more ancient as well—the Hermetic tradition with its idea of a *prisca theologia*: a single original wisdom that had passed through a chain of prophets—Hermes, Orpheus, Pythagoras, Plato. The beginning was made by the Council of Florence: the attempt to reconcile the churches failed politically, but the lectures of the Byzantine Plethon on Plato awakened in Florence an interest in Plato, and Cosimo de’ Medici conceived the plan of reviving the Platonic Academy. An episode of 1463 is telling: Cosimo commissioned Marsilio Ficino to translate Hermes first—the *Corpus Hermeticum*, newly brought to Florence—and only then Plato: the older meant the closer to the source.
>
> Around Ficino an academy grew up—a community at the villa in Careggi: Pico della Mirandola with his manifesto on the dignity of man, Poliziano, Lorenzo the Magnificent. The language of the academy became the language of painting as well: Botticelli’s *Primavera* is read from right to left, as an ascent of the soul—from earthly passion through Venus as virtuous Humanity to Mercury-Hermes dispersing the clouds; in Raphael’s fresco *The School of Athens* Plato points upward and Aristotle toward the earth: two gestures, two paths of knowing that complement each other; and their faces are those of contemporaries: according to tradition, Plato is painted from Leonardo and Heraclitus from Michelangelo. On the floor of Siena Cathedral Hermes Trismegistus is called “a contemporary of Moses”—an Egyptian sage on the threshold of a Christian church, and for the Renaissance there was no contradiction in this.[^8]
>
> The seventeenth century changed the arrangement. René Descartes sought a ground that cannot be doubted and found it in the very act of doubting; philosophy regained its autonomy—an inquiry resting on reason alone.[^9] At the same time the separation of the sciences began. Physics gained its independence with Newton—his chief work is still called the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*; chemistry, biology, and psychology followed physics, each taking for itself territory that had belonged to philosophy—by the nineteenth century only “residual” questions were left to it. Kant redefined its role: not knowledge of the world but the investigation of the conditions of the possibility of knowledge—philosophy as the critique of reason.
>
> The father of the new physics himself kept to the old line: Newton devoted more pages to alchemy and the Hermetic texts than to mechanics—he was seeking a *prisca sapientia*, an ancient wisdom that, as he was convinced, Moses and Pythagoras had known. The division of the rational and the contemplative is a later construction: useful for specialization, dangerous for understanding as a whole. The discursive path—logic, argument, analysis—gives the frame; the intuitive path—direct apprehension—gives the content; philosophy requires both.
>
> The modern research university was born in Berlin: Humboldt made the professor a producer of new knowledge. Philosophy fitted itself into this system: Hegel, taking the Berlin chair, gave the model of the philosopher as system-builder—a chair, a journal, a school; by the end of the nineteenth century philosophy had acquired degrees, professional societies, and refereed journals—and had become an academic profession. Nietzsche—himself a university professor—saw in this a betrayal of the meaning of the work: the ancient philosophers were healers of the soul, the modern professor is a specialist in texts; the result he called the “cultural philistine”—a person who knows everything and understands nothing.[^10]
>
> The twentieth century added a split within philosophy itself. The analytic tradition—from Frege, who created symbolic logic, through Whitehead and Russell with *Principia Mathematica* to the Vienna Circle—made the logical analysis of language its method: only what is verifiable by experience or derivable by logic is meaningful. The continental tradition ran from Husserl’s phenomenology to Heidegger: for him, with the question of the Nothing “the fate of the reign of ‘logic’ within philosophy is decided,” and thought returns to the question of being.[^11]
>
> The traditions parted: Carnap declared Heidegger’s sentence “The Nothing itself nothings” to be nonsense on a level with “Caesar is a prime number”;[^12] after the war the split was institutionalized—different societies, journals, chairs, languages. Within each tradition further specialization went on: a philosopher could work a whole career on the semantics of conditionals—and never read a line about the hermeneutic circle.
>
> And the question central for Socrates and the Stoics, “*how should one live?*”, passed to psychotherapists, coaches, and the authors of self-help books. Dissenting voices remained in the margins: Hadot recalled that for the ancients philosophy was a spiritual exercise—a practice of transformation; the later Wittgenstein came to philosophy as therapy—untying the knots into which language ties us.[^13] The academic machine went on working.

## The Discipline of Absolute Truth: Deriving the Definition

The norm is given by the system’s task, found in the analysis: **philosophy is the discipline of bringing knowledge into accord with what is.**

Word by word.

**Discipline** is a strict practice that demands effort: as a musician trains the ear, the philosopher trains attention and honesty. Its form is given by the rules of knowing: the ladder of knowledge, the channels, the question, the check ([[Epistemology]], [[Question]]).

**Bringing into accord** is unceasing work, because accord does not hold by itself: preferences, fears, and habits of attention continually distort the reading ([[Objectivity and Subjectivity]]).

**With what is** names the object against which one checks: reality, not our opinions about it. The philosopher asks “what is this?”—and by this question philosophy remains knowing.

The same norm, taken from the side of method: **philosophy is the discipline of knowing reality through reasoning.**

The method is discursive—this is what distinguishes philosophy both from mystical illumination and from empirical experiment. This does not exclude intuition: intuition gives the direction and grasps the whole, and philosophy *articulates* what has been grasped—it unfolds an instantaneous insight into a sequence of steps open to checking and to transmission; the discipline of reasoning prepares direct seeing.

The norm is common to cultures: the historical sketch has shown the convergence of questions amid the difference of answers—philosophy arose independently and was everywhere turned toward one reality. The position that the definition sets is realist: reality exists independently of our knowing and is knowable; and rationalist: reasoning is the path to absolute truth. The statement itself is normative: it says what philosophy must be in order to fulfill the task of the system.

## Philosophy as a Practice

Philosophy is a practice that forms a way of existing.

**Alignment with absolute truth.** To practice philosophy is to return thinking, again and again, into accord with what is: a discipline of resisting the distortions, which do not retreat by themselves.

**Absolute truth above all.** The honest question stands on the same thing ([[Question]]): absolute truth above all, and first of all above self-love. The philosopher is ready to change an opinion if absolute truth demands it—to admit a mistake, to leave a position in which years have been invested; this is strength, and it separates the philosopher from the sophist. The Sufis spoke of polishing the mirror of the heart; philosophy does the same with the mirror of the mind—layer by layer it removes prejudice and projection, so as to let reality be reflected clearly.[^14] This is the removal of the filter of perception ([[1 Recognition]])—feasible, endless, and obligatory.

**The forming of character.** Through practice there take shape attentiveness, honesty, humility before the facts, patience in inquiry, and the courage to follow one’s conclusions: philosophy is not information that can be absorbed but a way of being that can only be practiced.

**Every act is a choice.** In every act of thinking there stands a choice: to serve absolute truth or oneself, to seek clarity or to defend a position, to see what is or what one would like. There is no neutral territory—and the repeated choice forms not separate thoughts but the character as a whole.

## Summary

Philosophy is the love of wisdom, not the possession of it. This is a theorem read directly off the arrangement of the system: wisdom is the degree of wholeness of the growing corpus of what is understood, whose completeness is a limit by construction; toward a limit only a path and love are possible—the pull of the will between the known and the knowable. This is what distinguishes the philosopher from the sophist: the latter passes off the present degree of understanding as completeness and trades in the illusion of wisdom; the philosopher knows their own limit and leaves room for not-knowing—like Socrates, wisest precisely in this.

Wisdom itself is the wholeness of what is understood: knowledge, understanding, and skill—*what*, *why*, and *how*—converge into one, repeating in the knower the structure of logic itself. It rests on technique and on the will to truth, and it carries over from one domain to another because the structure of every system is one in form.

Philosophy is the discipline of bringing knowledge into accord with what is: a strict practice—the ladder, the channels, the question, the check—and an unceasing one, for accord does not hold by itself. And it rests on one thing: absolute truth above all, and first of all above self-love. It is open to everyone who is ready to ask honestly; and [[Epistemology|the theory of knowledge]] itself is philosophy in action—knowledge brought into accord with its own structure and therefore knowing its limit.

One thing remains. The path of knowing requires the ability to walk it—step by step, on grounds, without straying and without deceiving oneself. This ability is reasoning; its arrangement, its laws, and its breakdowns are a separate branch: [[Architecture of Reasoning]]. Here let us end with what a person begins with on first seeing their own boundary: the love of what lies beyond it.
