The Law of Non-Contradiction: ***¬(A ∧ ¬A)*** · ***principium contradictionis***

**What is determinate stands on one side of the boundary: nothing is *A* and not-*A* at once, in one and the same respect.**

All determinacy rests on this exclusivity: where something is *A* and not-*A* at once, the difference between *A* and not-*A* is erased, and with it everything that could be said about that something. Non-Contradiction holds the very difference between the true and the false.

## Etymology

**Contradiction.** The word is Latin, built like its Greek counterpart. Greek ἀντίφασις (antiphasis): ἀντί (anti), “against,” and φάσις (phasis), “statement.” Latin *contradictio*: *contra*, “against,” and *dicere*, “to speak”—whence the English word. The Russian *protivorechie* is a calque of the same build: “speech against.” In each of these languages a contradiction is something *said*: speech against speech. The root carries the thesis of this page: contradiction lives in speech and in thought; in things themselves there is none.

## Place among the Laws

Among the five laws ([[Logic]]), Non-Contradiction is the second of the two **primary** laws: it articulates the *exclusivity* of the sides of the distinction. The distinction places *A* and not-*A* in mutually exclusive positions on opposite sides of the boundary; to be both is to be on both sides of the boundary at once—that is, to be determinately nowhere. A contradiction describes the collapse of a state of affairs: a contradictory “something” has no determinate character.

The first primary law is [[Identity]]: neither of the two derives from the other. [[Excluded Middle]] is derived from their joint operation; [[Sufficient Reason]] and [[Order]] are meta-laws.

## History

Aristotle called this law “the most certain of all principles”—the one about which it is impossible to be mistaken, and the first on which every demonstration rests. He placed it in the *Metaphysics*, among the discussions of being, rather than in the logical treatises: the law says what everything that is must be like. It cannot be demonstrated directly—any demonstration already uses it—and Aristotle defends it indirectly: he undertakes “to demonstrate by refutation,” ἀποδεῖξαι ἐλεγκτικῶς (apodeixai elenktikōs). This continues the Socratic ἔλεγχος (elenchos), testing by refutation.[^1]

The argument is simple. To say “the law of non-contradiction is false,” one must hold that this statement is true, and that “false” means precisely “false.” The denial of the law uses the law. This is more than a counterargument: the law cannot even be meaningfully denied—whoever denies it has no position from which to speak. Aristotle asks one thing of the denier: to say something significant—to himself and to another. Having said a significant word, he has already drawn a distinction: this, and not that. With one who stays silent there is nothing to argue about: such a person, according to Aristotle, is like a plant.[^2]

For the scholastics the law is the first indemonstrable principle. Thomas Aquinas: “one cannot affirm and deny at once,” and this principle “is founded on the notion of being and non-being.”[^3] Leibniz placed it at the foundation of knowledge beside sufficient reason—the two great principles on which all reasoning rests. To non-contradiction he subordinates the *truths of reason*, necessary truths whose denial is impossible because it leads to contradiction; the *truths of fact* rest on the other principle, that of sufficient reason. Thus the law received its exact place: it is the boundary of the possible as such, the touchstone of what can be thought without self-destruction.[^4]

Kant called non-contradiction “the supreme principle of all analytic judgments”—and thereby limited it. For Kant it is only a *negative* criterion: it screens out the impossible but gives no substantive truth, and no substantive, synthetic knowledge can be built on it alone. The place of the law shifts as well: from the structure of being it becomes a rule of general logic—a necessary condition of every judgment, which abstracts from all content and says nothing about the object itself.[^5] The position adopted here is closer to Aristotle’s: non-contradiction is a feature of being itself, and therefore a form of thinking. A thought that accepts a contradiction thinks nothing determinate: there is none in what is.

In 1910 Jan Łukasiewicz subjected Aristotle’s defense to rigorous scrutiny. He separated three versions that Aristotle had run together—the ontological (nothing can at once belong and not belong to one and the same thing), the logical (two contradictory judgments are not true together), and the psychological (one cannot at once believe *A* and not-*A*)—and showed that Aristotle’s arguments, the refutation included, do not amount to a rigorous proof. Out of this crack grew the many-valued logics—systems with truth values other than “true” and “false.”[^6] This analysis does not touch the law. There is no rigorous proof and there cannot be one—any such proof would already use the law; the strength of the refutation lies elsewhere: it shows that the denier is using the law. The ontological version—the one adopted here—is left unshaken by Łukasiewicz: he disputes whether the law is *provable*, not whether it is *true*. And many-valuedness relabels the values on the map while leaving the territory untouched: every distinction still has two sides ([[Excluded Middle]]).

## What the Law Makes Possible

In being, the law makes **interaction** possible: it keeps the sides of an interaction apart—*A* acts on *B* only while the two stand on opposite sides of the boundary. The relation between them is itself given by [[Order]]. In knowing, three things stand on this.

**Truth value.** If a statement can be at once true and false, the difference between the true and the false disappears, and with it the meaning of the word “true.”

**Decision.** Every choice presupposes that to choose *A* is not to choose not-*A*. Where one can have both *A* and not-*A* at once, there is nothing to choose between.

**Assertion.** To assert is to exclude: to say “yes” is not to say “no.” Once it is allowed that “yes” may mean “no,” speech ceases to convey anything.

## Attempts to Get Around the Law

Attempts to get around the law have been made more than once. On closer inspection each deals with an *apparent* contradiction: behind it stands a difference of levels, a difference of respects, or an error of construction.

**Dialectical synthesis.** Hegel’s dialectic is often presented through the triad “thesis–antithesis–synthesis.” Hegel himself, however, did not use these terms—they belong to Fichte (1794); moreover, Hegel directly criticized such formalistic use of triads, calling it a “lifeless schema” imposed on the content from outside.[^7] Genuine Hegelian dialectic works differently. Its key term is *Aufhebung* (sublation): a transition in which the initial concept reveals its one-sidedness and gives rise to its opposite, and both moments are at once negated, preserved, and raised to a higher level. About contradiction he speaks plainly: “all things are in themselves contradictory.”[^8] But his “contradiction” is a clash of opposed determinations that is sublated in the transition to a higher level; it contains no assertion of “*A* and not-*A* in one respect.”

**Quantum superposition.** A particle is in a superposition of states until a measurement is made. But whichever interpretation of quantum mechanics we adopt—Copenhagen, many-worlds, Bohmian—the result of a measurement is always determinate: *A* or not-*A*. Superposition is not a contradiction in reality but a mathematical structure carrying the potential for determinate outcomes.[^9]

**Paraconsistent logics.** Modern logic has built systems that tolerate contradictions without “explosion”: in them not everything follows from a contradiction. But these systems are tools for working with contradictory *information*, not claims that *reality* is contradictory. The map may contain contradictions; the territory does not. Dialetheism goes further: according to Graham Priest, some contradictions are true.[^10] But whoever asserts “some contradictions are true” excludes the opposite—and is already using the law. The chief witnesses of dialetheism are paradoxes such as the Liar. And the [[Liar Paradox|Liar]] is an error of construction: the statement presupposes what it is supposed to determine, and no contradiction forms in it. There are no “true contradictions”: there are ill-formed statements ([[Paradox Catalog]]).

Why does the territory contain no contradictions? To be is to be determinate, and what is determinate stands on one side of the boundary. A “contradictory thing” would stand on both sides at once—that is, it would be nothing determinate: this is the collapse of a thing. Experience confirms it: every apparent contradiction, on analysis, turns out to be a difference of levels, a difference of respects, or an error of construction. Such constructions are analyzed in the [[Paradox Catalog|paradox catalog]]: there are no paradoxes in reality.

## Violations

In reality the law is unbreakable; it is broken in thinking. An open contradiction—“*A* and not-*A*” in a single sentence—is too conspicuous, so the violations are hidden: spread out in time, kept apart in different contexts, covered by a qualification. Hence the typical [[Fallacy Catalog|fallacies]].

*Inconsistency.* At the beginning of an argument *A* is asserted, at the end not-*A*, and the conclusion rests on whichever of the two is more convenient. The contradiction is spread across the text and therefore goes unnoticed; gathered into a single sentence, it would fall apart at once.

*[[Two Truths|Doublethink]].* The mind holds two mutually exclusive beliefs and accepts both without bringing them together. Orwell defines it as “the power of holding two contradictory beliefs in one’s mind simultaneously, and accepting both of them.”[^11] The contradiction is not resolved but hidden behind a partition between compartments of consciousness.

*A sham “in different respects.”* The qualification “in one and the same respect” is legitimate: in different respects *A* can be both *B* and not-*B* (what moves relative to one thing is at rest relative to another). The violation begins where the respect is switched after the fact: one and the same respect is passed off as two in order to hide an actual contradiction. The nearest form in the catalog is the [[Big But Fallacy|“big but”]]: a principle is affirmed and at once lifted for a “special case.”

## Summary

1. **The Law of Non-Contradiction: ¬(*A* ∧ ¬*A*)**—nothing is *A* and not-*A* at once, in one and the same respect. Its property is exclusivity; it is the second of the two primary laws.
2. **It makes interaction possible:** it keeps the sides of an interaction apart; the relation between them is itself given by Order. In knowing, three things stand on it: truth value, decision, and assertion.
3. **The law can be neither proved directly nor meaningfully denied:** every significant word already draws a distinction.
4. **Contradiction is the collapse of the determinate.** Dialectic, superposition, and paraconsistent logics yield no contradiction in reality; there are no “true contradictions.”
5. **In reality the law is unbreakable.** In thinking, contradiction is hidden: inconsistency, doublethink, a sham “in different respects.”

Stability ([[Identity]]) and exclusivity together complete the demarcation of the distinction, leaving no room in it for a third. That is their joint consequence: [[Excluded Middle]].
