The Law of Sufficient Reason: ***nihil est sine ratione***

**For everything there is a ground for its being just so and not otherwise.**

Identity, Non-Contradiction, and Excluded Middle say what everything that is must be like; this law answers the question *why* it is precisely so. For every fact and for every true statement there is a ground. Sufficient Reason stands at the base of the other laws: it grounds their very presence.

## Etymology

**Reason, ground.** English has two words for one thing. *Reason* comes through French from Latin *ratio*, from *reor*, “to reckon”: a count, a calculation, an account. Leibniz also called the law *principium reddendae rationis*—the principle by which an account can be rendered for everything; in Greek, λόγον διδόναι (logon didonai), “to give an account.” The school name is *principium rationis sufficientis*: a ground is sufficient when it is enough for things to be just so. *Ground*, like German *Grund*, is the bottom, the soil. The Russian *osnovanie* comes from *osnova*, the warp: the lengthwise threads on which a fabric is woven. Three images of one thing: that on which something rests, and that by which one can give an account.

## Place among the Laws

Among the five laws ([[Logic]]), Sufficient Reason is a **meta-law**: it stands at the base of the others and makes everything else possible. The primary laws and the derived law say what any determinate state of affairs must be like; Sufficient Reason grounds the very presence of what is and the very force of the other laws: to be identical to itself and non-contradictory, something must *be*. Its property is *self-grounding*: every distinction has a sufficient reason for being this distinction rather than another, and the primary distinction is grounded in the very difference between *A* and not-*A*. The paired meta-law, [[Order]], answers the question *how*: in what form distinctions stand.

[[Excluded Middle]] gives the options—*A* or not-*A*. Which of them has come out is answered by Sufficient Reason: assignment is an action, and it answers to a ground.

A simple example gathers all the laws at once. The door is either open or not open—[[Excluded Middle|excluded middle]]. It cannot be open and not open at once—[[Non-Contradiction|non-contradiction]]. An open door is an open door—[[Identity|identity]]. But *why* is it open? Someone turned the handle, the wind pushed it, the hinges broke. Without a ground the door does not pass from the closed state to the open one. First the handle is turned, then the door opens—[[Order|order]]. Three laws say what everything that is must be like; Sufficient Reason speaks of the link between ground and grounded: why precisely this state of affairs and not another.

It cannot be proved without presupposing it: any proof already uses the notion of a ground.[^11] Nor can it be meaningfully denied: the statement “not everything has a ground” itself calls for a ground—why does precisely *this* have none? The self-confirmation of Sufficient Reason runs deeper than that of the primary laws: it concerns the very possibility of being grounded.

## History

The first known use is attributed by Aristotle to Anaximander: the earth rests at the center because it has no ground for moving in one direction rather than another.[^1] It was **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz**—philosopher, mathematician, diplomat, one of the creators of the differential calculus—who made the principle a separate, named law. In the *Monadology* (1714) he wrote: “*…no fact can be found true or existent, no statement true, without there being a **sufficient reason** why it is so and not otherwise, although these reasons most often cannot be known to us*.”[^2] In the same year, in the *Principles of Nature and Grace*, Leibniz posed the question that was to become central to metaphysics: “Why is there something rather than nothing?”—and answered: the sufficient reason of the world lies outside the series of contingent things, in a necessary being.[^3] With Leibniz the law took its place beside non-contradiction as the second of the two great principles of reason; German school philosophy (Wolff) then raised it into a systematic principle.

The most thorough analysis was given by Schopenhauer: he devoted a whole dissertation to the law (1813) and singled out its four roots—the ground of becoming (causality in the physical world), of knowing (logical consequence), of being (spatiotemporal and mathematical relations), and of acting (the motivation of the will).[^4] Each works in its own domain, but all are expressions of one principle: nothing exists without a ground; we cannot think a single object without asking, at least implicitly, why it is so and not otherwise. In the twentieth century Heidegger returned to the principle (*Der Satz vom Grund*, 1957), reading in Leibniz’s *nihil est sine ratione* the destiny of the whole of Western metaphysics.

## What the Law Makes Possible

In being, the law makes **everything else** possible: nothing arises without a ground—the world contains no “brute facts” that simply are. In knowing, two things stand on this. Both rest on what has just been said of being: thinking can demand grounds because there are grounds in reality itself.

**Knowledge.** Knowledge differs from opinion in being grounded. An opinion can be held without a ground; knowledge cannot: to say “I know” is to take on the obligation to present a ground. At the same time our *knowledge* of grounds is limited: Leibniz adds that the reasons “most often cannot be known to us.” The ground exists; we are not always able to find it.

**The discipline of reasoning.** Every step must have a ground. One cannot pass from premise to conclusion without explaining why the conclusion follows; one cannot choose a framework of comparison without grounding the choice. “It seems” is not a ground. “It is obvious” is not a ground. “Everyone thinks so” is not a ground. The law also works as a criterion of completion: each domain is complete when there is a sufficient reason for moving on to the next ([[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|movement through the domains]]).

## Causa and Ratio

Latin *causa* (cause) and *ratio* (ground) are not the same. A cause is what produces an effect in time: the blow of the hammer is the cause of the nail’s entering the wood. A ground is what makes this intelligible, what explains *why*: steel is harder than wood—that is the ground of the nail’s entering the wood rather than the wood’s entering the nail. **A cause acts; a ground explains.** Every cause is a ground, but not every ground is a cause: mathematical truths have grounds but no causes. In Spinoza the two still stand joined by “or”: “for every thing a cause, or ground, must be assigned, both for why it exists and for why it does not exist.”[^5] Leibniz already speaks of the ground—*ratio*, *raison suffisante*; Wolff separated the two explicitly, and Schopenhauer made the distinction central. The law covers both.

The law gives the cause: behind every effect stands a ground. The relation of cause and effect itself—the influence of one on the other, their interaction, and their sequence—is given by the paired meta-law, [[Order]].

## Why Is There Something?

The question is asked about reality itself—about the absolute meta-system, not about a thing within it. Existence is a status: “something exists” means “can be—and is.” The law asks: what grounds must there be for anything at all to be able to receive this status? There are two: **Logic**—form, structure—and the **Source**—content, the fullness of potential ([[Metaphysics]]). Without form, content is indeterminate, that is, not distinguished from nothing; without content, the structure has nothing to arrange. This is the sufficient reason of existence itself. It has no generating step: no action precedes it that might have failed to happen. Therefore the very possibility of existence, given its grounds, is necessarily realized: if the conditions are fulfilled, reality is. Its status is “cannot not be.”

For the possible within reality, things stand otherwise. Here, between “can be” and “is,” there stands an action—a cause that moves the status from “can be, but is not” to “can be—and is.” Without such an action the possible remains possible and may never be realized at all. Hence the distinction: *existence as such* is necessary; *what* in particular exists is not. Which of the possible becomes actual is decided by a ground; the sequence in which it does so is given by [[Order]].

## The Regress of Grounds and the Trilemma

The law raises a question: what is the ground of the ground itself? This is the problem of regress, already known to the ancients;[^6] it admits only three ways out. *Infinite regress*: the chain of grounds runs to infinity; but an infinite chain cannot be traversed, so nothing would ever be grounded. *Circle*: the chain returns to its beginning; but circular grounding is no grounding at all. *Starting points*: the chain rests on what itself requires no external ground; this is the only way out that makes grounding possible.

The laws of logic are such starting points: they have no external ground—any attempt to give one already uses them. They confirm themselves: their denial is impossible without their use. Here the regress is blocked at the very root: the primary distinction is self-grounding—the ground of the distinction *A* | not-*A* is the very difference between *A* and not-*A*. This is no circle: the ground is internal to the structure of the distinction. Subsequent distinctions find their grounds in preceding ones; the chain ends at the primary one. The grounds of the laws lie in Logic itself: the laws are its properties. Logic and the Source are necessary systems: their status is “cannot not be” ([[Metaphysics]]). Schopenhauer, too, ends the series in a law: the chain of grounds of knowing always terminates somewhere—in truths that express the conditions of all thinking and knowing; the principle of sufficient reason is the principle of all explanation and therefore is not itself subject to explanation.[^12]

## Objections

**Kant’s objection.** Can the impossibility of denial prove anything about reality itself—or only a necessity for thinking? The categories of the understanding, according to Kant, are necessary for ordering experience, but their application to things in themselves is illegitimate: we know the world only as it appears to us. Sufficient Reason, on this logic, is a law of our thinking about being, not of being itself.[^7]

Yet Kant’s objection rests on a tacit assumption: that there is “thinking” on one side and “being” on the other, with a gulf between them. This assumption itself calls for a ground. If thinking itself belongs to being—and it does, for the one who thinks exists—then the laws without which thinking is impossible are laws of this being: its inner arrangement. And if everything that is obeys one logical structure—otherwise it could not interact ([[Non-Contradiction]])—then the laws of thinking turn out to be laws of being by identity of structure. Kant himself left formal logic its universal force: his categories are confined to the sphere of appearances, while the laws of identity and non-contradiction hold for all thinking, whatever it is about. We extend this universality to the whole logical structure. We do not impose logic on the world: we discover it, because we ourselves are part of this structure.

**Hume’s objection.** Hume challenged the very idea of a necessary connection between cause and effect: we see that one event follows another, but we do not see that it *must* follow; custom, not reason, makes us expect that the sun will rise tomorrow.[^8]

What Hume doubts is the *connection* of cause and effect—the relation between them—and that is the province of [[Order]]. He goes further: a beginning without a cause is conceivable without contradiction. But conceivability tests Non-Contradiction alone; a ground is the demand of another law, and it is not derivable from the first. A ground need not produce; it explains: even if the mechanism linking fire and warmth is hidden, the answer “it is warm here because a fire is burning” remains a ground. Moreover, Hume himself uses the law when he explains *why* we believe in causality: he gives a ground for our belief—custom—and confirms the law in the act of criticizing it. Hume points to the limits of our knowledge of causal connections; this does not cancel grounds in reality.

**The objection from quantum mechanics.** Quantum mechanics, on the standard reading, describes events without full causal determination: the moment of the radioactive decay of an individual atom has no exact cause in time—there is only a probability. But the law does not promise that the future can be computed from the present. It says: whatever is has a ground. Before the decay, the question “decayed or not decayed” has two sides, and neither is occupied; the ground of the possibility is there—the nucleus is unstable, and the probability is set by its structure. The moment of decay itself is the execution of Sufficient Reason: the assignment has taken place, the status has changed from “can be, but is not” to “can be—and is.” From the past this moment cannot be derived—both continuations are lawful; the law requires a ground, not predetermination.[^9]

**Russell’s objection.** The demand for a ground for everything leads to the question of the ground of the world itself—and to this question there may be no answer: “I should say that the universe is just there, and that’s all”—the famous phrase from Russell’s debate with Copleston (1948). Later, what simply is, with no ground, came to be called a “brute fact.”[^10]

Let us go back to the starting points. “Just there” means: it might not have been, and there is no ground. But “might not have been” presupposes that “non-existence” is a meaningful alternative. An alternative is a choice between *A* and not-*A*, and a choice is possible only where there is a logical structure that distinguishes *A* from not-*A*. This structure is already *something*. For “nothing” to be a possible alternative to being, there must exist a logical space in which this alternative is meaningful—but logical space is not nothing. “Absolute nothing”—the absence even of a structure of possibility—cannot be an alternative: there is no place where it could *be* an alternative. The existence of logical structure is necessary: even to conceive of its absence is already to use it. This is the first level of the question: reality itself “cannot not be.” That this particular world has been realized in it is the second level: what has passed from possible to actual has a cause. Russell points to the limits of explanation, but his “brute fact” itself rests on a logical structure that he declines to explain.

Here the meta-character of the law becomes visible: the primary laws and the derived law operate *within* the logical structure; Sufficient Reason concerns this structure itself—its existence is necessary. **Being is logical not by accident—it is logical by necessity.**

## Violations

In reality the law is unbreakable; it is broken in thinking: a ground is imitated or replaced.

*[[A Priori Argument|Rationalization]].* The conclusion is drawn first, and a ground is then fitted to the ready answer: a ground is presented, but the conclusion preceded it. The same move recurs in medical diagnoses, investment decisions, intelligence assessments. The paired law, [[Order]], is violated as well: the ground is placed after the conclusion.

*A sham ground.* In the place of a ground stands what does not give one: an impression, obviousness, [[Bandwagon Fallacy|the opinion of the majority]].

*[[Non Sequitur|The gap]].* A ground is named, but the conclusion does not follow from it.

## Summary

1. **The Law of Sufficient Reason: *nihil est sine ratione***—for everything there is a ground for its being just so and not otherwise. Its property is self-grounding; it is a meta-law.
2. **It makes everything else possible.** Excluded Middle gives the options; which of them has come out is answered by Sufficient Reason: assignment is an action, and it answers to a ground. In knowing, two things stand on it: knowledge and the discipline of reasoning.
3. **The cause is given by Sufficient Reason;** the relation of cause and effect is given by Order.
4. **Why there is something:** for reality itself the conditions of existence are Logic and the Source; they are fulfilled, and reality exists—it “cannot not be.” The possible within reality passes to “can be—and is” only through an action, a cause—and may never be realized.
5. **The regress of grounds is blocked at the root:** the primary distinction is grounded in the difference itself.
6. **The objections of Kant, Hume, quantum mechanics, and Russell do not remove the law:** thinking is part of the same structure; the law requires a ground, not predetermination; the existence of logical structure is necessary.
7. **In reality the law is unbreakable.** In thinking a ground is imitated: rationalization, a sham ground, the gap.

Yet even a grounded step can be taken in the wrong place. Sufficient Reason does not say *in what order* grounds are to be built: the demand “why” is carried out within the right form of “how.” The form is given by the paired meta-law—[[Order]].
