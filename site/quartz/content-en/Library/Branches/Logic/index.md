The laws of logic are properties of being itself, given together with it. Here they are derived from a single fact: “something exists.”

## Etymology

**Logic.** Greek λογική (logikē), from λόγος (logos); Latin *logica*. The verb λέγειν (legein) means first of all “to gather, to pick out, to count”—and only then “to speak”: to say is to gather words into order. Hence the breadth of λόγος: word, speech, reckoning, ratio, proportion, reason, law. Logos is that which gathers: the order of thought and the order of things alike. The root is itself the thesis of this branch: logic is order itself, and not only speech about it.

## What Is Logic?

**Logic is the structure of existence.**

Heraclitus of Ephesus was one of the first thinkers to attempt to express the structure of reality in philosophical concepts. It was Heraclitus who introduced the concept of the Logos as a philosophical category. He wrote: “*This Logos is forever, yet people prove unable to understand it—both before they hear it and when they have first heard it*.”[^1] For him the Logos is not human speech but the cosmic law to which all that exists is subject.[^2] Wisdom consists in recognizing this law and living in agreement with it.

Parmenides of Elea—the founder of the Eleatic school, sometimes called the “father of metaphysics”—went further. In his philosophical poem *On Nature*, the goddess reveals to him two ways of inquiry: “the one, that it is and that it is impossible for it not to be … the other, that it is not and that it is necessary for it not to be.”[^3] The second way is impassable: “for you could not come to know what is not—that cannot be accomplished—nor could you utter it.”[^4] Parmenides adds: “for to think and to be are one and the same.”[^5]

We can think logically because reality itself is logical. The Stoics developed this idea into an entire philosophical system. Zeno of Citium, the founder of the school, was the first to define the goal of human life as “living in agreement with nature”[^6]—which for the Stoics meant the same as living virtuously. But “nature” here does not mean forests and rivers. The universe, according to Stoic teaching, is pervaded by a rational principle—the *logos*, which is at once cosmic law and divine reason. Marcus Aurelius puts it this way: “For the world is one, made up of all things, and god is one, pervading all things, and substance is one, and law is one—the reason common to all rational beings—and truth is one.”[^7]

Human reason is nothing other than a particle of this cosmic logos: “the spirit that Zeus has given to each as guardian and guide, a fragment of himself. And this is each one’s mind and *logos*.”[^8] Wisdom, then, is the alignment of individual reason with cosmic reason.

Heraclitus, Parmenides, and the Stoics saw one and the same truth: the structure of right thinking and the structure of reality are one and the same.

Then came a narrowing. *Logica* gradually turned from a cosmic principle into a practical instrument—first of rhetoric, then of syllogistic, and finally of a formal system of correct thinking. What had begun as a cosmic principle of order was confined to the human art of argumentation. Philosophy increasingly replaced the question of being with questions about beings, about representation, about truth as correspondence—and in the end being itself slipped out of view. Logic became a technique cut off from ontology.

Martin Heidegger, one of the most influential philosophers of the twentieth century, diagnosed this situation as the “oblivion of being.” Already in the first section of *Being and Time* he states: “The aforementioned question has today fallen into oblivion, although our time counts it as progress that it once again affirms ‘metaphysics.’”[^9] For Heidegger this is more than academic negligence: it is the gradual loss of the connection between thinking and that which *is*.

Heidegger distinguished the “first beginning” (*der erste Anfang*)—the original Greek experience of being, which was then “closed off” by the metaphysics of Plato and Aristotle—from the “other beginning” (*der andere Anfang*), which has yet to be prepared.[^11] *Dasein*—“being-there,” the human being as the place where being discloses itself—is, according to Heidegger, the crisis between the first and the other beginning.[^12]

His diagnosis applies to logic as well: cut off from ontology, it loses its ground.

But this narrowing was a necessary stage: to study logic in detail—its forms, laws, and operations—it had to be abstracted from ontology for a time. Aristotle’s syllogistic, the formal systems of the twentieth century, mathematical logic—all of this became possible through the narrowing of focus.

Now, after centuries of such study, reintegration is possible. The way forward is to join the detailed understanding of logic-as-technique with the ancient understanding of logic-as-the-structure-of-being.

A clarification is needed here. The link between the Heraclitean Logos and Aristotelian syllogistic is not one of simple continuity. These are different conceptions: a cosmic principle of order and a technique of correct inference. What unites them is not identity but the fact that the second turns out to be a *manifestation* of the first in the realm of thought. Formal logic is not the whole of the *Logos*, yet it is nothing separate from it either. It is the *Logos* grasped under the aspect of rules of inference. Reintegration means seeing, behind the rules of inference, the structure of which they are a special case.

A step toward such reintegration is taken here. Logic is the structure of being itself: reality exists independently of our ideas about it and is accessible to knowledge.

## Conditions of Existence

René Descartes identified the Archimedean point: the existence of the one who thinks is the first and most certain of all truths.[^16] But before answering how what exists is structured, it is worth asking a deeper question: **why is there something at all, rather than nothing**?

This question is the first and the most fundamental. Gottfried Leibniz formulated it in the *Principles of Nature and Grace*.[^17] Martin Heidegger made it the starting point of metaphysics: “Why are there beings at all, and not rather nothing?”[^18] Yet Leibniz himself gave, at the same time, the key to the answer—a principle that here receives the status of a meta-law.

**The [[Sufficient Reason|Law of Sufficient Reason]] states: everything that exists and everything that happens has a ground for being just so and not otherwise.**

It is a **meta-law**—the first and deepest of the laws of logic, standing at the foundation of the others rather than alongside them. Identity, Non-Contradiction, and Excluded Middle say what everything that is must be like; Sufficient Reason answers the question why it is at all.

The law is applied three times. To the very fact of being: why is there something? To the existence of logic: why is there logic itself, and why is it precisely as it is? To any fact of reality: why so and not otherwise?

Sufficient Reason **gives the cause**: behind every effect stands a ground—that by virtue of which it is just so; behind the actual stands the potential. The relation between cause and effect itself—the influence of one on the other, their interaction, and their sequence—is given by [[Order]].

The law is confirmed by the very attempt to deny it: a denial requires a ground, and that is already Sufficient Reason applied to the act of denial itself.

**Why, then, is there something?** The law’s answer, in brief: **the very possibility of existence, given its grounds, is necessarily realized.** If reality *can* be, then there are conditions under which it can be—the sufficient grounds of existence. If these conditions are fulfilled, it *must* be. This is the Law of Sufficient Reason in its deepest application.

What are these conditions? Existence is a status: “something exists” means “can be—and is.” The law asks: what grounds must there be for anything at all to be able to receive this status? There are two, and each is necessary in its own right. **Logic** is the form: the structure in which something is determinate. The **Source** is the content: the fullness of potential that the form determines. Without form, content is indeterminate, that is, not distinguished from nothing; without content, the structure has nothing to arrange. They have no generating step: they must be and always are; their status is “cannot not be” ([[Metaphysics]]).

So it stands with reality itself. What is possible within it needs something more: an action—a cause that carries it from “can be, but is not” to “can be—and is” ([[Sufficient Reason]]). Where, then, does the derivation itself begin?

## The Ground

We can say with certainty that existence *is*. Hence **the first principle: something exists.** Let this be designated as *A*. This is not an axiom of a formal system; it is the condition for any inquiry whatsoever. Whoever denies it (“nothing exists”) is already asserting something—and thereby presupposes the existence of the one asserting, the act of assertion, and the assertion itself. The first principle presents itself: every act of thought, perception, or doubt confirms it; to question it is to confirm it.

This was seen before. Augustine: “if I am mistaken, I am.”[^10] Descartes: “I think, therefore I am.”[^16] The first principle takes less from this experience: not “I exist” but “something exists.”

The first principle is minimal: it does not say *what* exists, *how much* exists, or what the nature of existence is. It claims one thing: there is at least one determinate *something*.

**The first consequence is distinction.** If *A* exists, *A* exists determinately: as this rather than that; as something rather than nothing. Existence without any determinacy is indistinguishable from non-existence—and indistinguishability from non-existence is non-existence. Determinacy, in turn, requires distinction: to be is to be something, and to be something is to be distinguished from what is other. Distinction is the first consequence of the first principle: not a separate starting point but a direct derivation from existence itself.

It should be noted that this derivation is epistemic, not ontological. The path of derivation runs: existence → distinction → recognition of the laws. The order of being runs the other way: the laws of logic are **conditions for the possibility of determinate existence; *ontologically, they are prior***. Existence does not generate the laws—it presents them; we recognize them by analyzing what determinate existence requires. For more on the ontological grounds of existence, see [[Metaphysics]].

## The Act of Distinction

**The structure of primary distinction.**

The primary distinction has a definite form: ***A* | not-*A***, where the bar is the boundary of the distinction. On one side stands the distinguished (*A*): that which has been marked out. On the other stands the background (not-*A*): everything from which *A* has been distinguished.

The distinction is **exhaustive**: it divides all that is into two regions—what satisfies the criterion of *A* and what does not; in the primary distinction there is no third region (“neither *A* nor not-*A*”).

The distinction is **exclusive**: *A* and not-*A* occupy mutually exclusive positions, and the boundary between them is sharp. Vagueness appears only in subsequent, derivative distinctions.

**What arises in distinction.**

The primary act of distinction generates the initial concepts:

- **Unit**—the distinguished (*A*) constitutes a unit, the first quantitative concept. A unit is the result of an act of distinction: everything distinguished is one.

- **Boundary**—the demarcation between *A* and not-*A*. The boundary is binary: *A* differs from not-*A* by its essence—by that which makes *A* this *A*. The boundary is a logical structure that separates *A* from everything else.

- **Relation**—*A* and not-*A* are co-constituted: each is defined through the other (*A* is not-not-*A*; not-*A* is everything that is not *A*), and they can be conceived only together. This is the primordial relation.

- **Structure**—the arrangement *A* | not-*A* has a form: the positions of the distinguished and the background, the boundary between them, the exhaustiveness and exclusivity of the division. This is the primordial structure.

## The Laws

The five laws of logic are structural properties of the primary distinction.[^19] Each law articulates an aspect of what it means for distinction to occur—and thus an aspect of what must hold for anything to exist determinately.

The laws of logic are not rules applied to reality from outside. Logic is not located *within* existence; existence is *in accordance with* the laws of logic. Logic is the form that gives existence its structure. Without structure, the existence of anything is *impossible*.

Here lies the boundary with Kant. For Kant, we know a priori of things only what we ourselves put into them: the forms belong to reason.[^13] Here the forms belong to the things themselves: reason finds them because it is itself structured by them.

In its essence, logic is **laws**: laws that constitute **the very possibility of existence**—neither a convention nor a description of observed regularities. Each law gives existence one of its features. Identity gives stability: something remains what it is. Non-Contradiction gives exclusivity: what is determinate stands on one side of the boundary. Excluded Middle gives exhaustiveness: the boundary divides everything without remainder. Sufficient Reason gives a ground to everything that is. Order gives the relation between what is distinguished—sequence and levels.

In reality these laws are unbreakable: they are its structure. A broken law of logic does not create another reality; it creates an [[Paradox Catalog|illusion]] that falls apart under examination. But these laws **can be broken in thinking**—and then thinking diverges from what is. A [[Fallacy Catalog|fallacy]] is no accident: it is an attempt to exist outside the laws that make existence possible. Such an attempt fails by definition. [[Architecture of Reasoning|Thinking that follows the laws of logic]] moves together with reality; thinking that breaks them creates illusions.

Each law has its own page with a detailed analysis, history, and objections: [[Identity]] · [[Non-Contradiction]] · [[Excluded Middle]] · [[Sufficient Reason]] · [[Order]]. On the one who reasons by these laws, see [[Philosophy]].

### Identity — Stability

***A = A***: everything is identical to itself.

For the distinction to occur, the distinguished must remain the same throughout the act of distinction: if *A* changed in the very act of being marked out, there would be no determinate *A* to mark out.

The **[[Identity|Law of Identity]]** makes differentiation possible. *A* is *A*; not-*A* is not-*A*. For something to exist as determinate, it must be identical to itself and distinct from what is other. Without identity everything merges into the indistinguishable—and ceases to exist as determinate. Differentiation is the Law of Identity in manifestation. For a detailed analysis—how identity allows for change, what exactly remains the same, the criteria of identity—see [[Identity]].

### Non-Contradiction — Exclusivity

***¬(A ∧ ¬A)***—it is not the case that (*A* and not-*A*): nothing is *A* and not-*A* at once, in one and the same respect.

The distinction places *A* and not-*A* in mutually exclusive positions on opposite sides of the boundary. To be both is to be on both sides of the boundary at once—that is, to be determinately nowhere.

The **[[Non-Contradiction|Law of Non-Contradiction]]** makes interaction possible. Interacting entities cannot be at once themselves and not-themselves in the same respect—otherwise interaction would lose the boundaries within which alone it is possible. Without non-contradiction, interaction collapses into chaos. Interaction is the Law of Non-Contradiction in manifestation. Non-Contradiction keeps the sides of an interaction apart; the relation between them is itself given by [[Order]]. For a detailed analysis—Aristotle’s defense of the law and the attempts to get around it: dialectic, superposition, paraconsistent logics—see [[Non-Contradiction]].

### Excluded Middle — Exhaustiveness

***A ∨ ¬A***—*A* or not-*A*: the boundary divides everything without remainder; there is no third.

“Neither *A* nor not-*A*” would be something that satisfies neither the criterion for *A* nor the criterion for not-*A*; but not-*A* is defined precisely as everything that does not satisfy the criterion for *A*. Whatever is not *A* is, by definition, not-*A*.

The **[[Excluded Middle|Law of Excluded Middle]]** makes difference possible: a difference is drawn without remainder, and for everything that can be there are two options—*A* or not-*A*. Difference is the Law of Excluded Middle in manifestation.

Excluded Middle occupies a special position: it is **derived** from the joint operation of Identity and Non-Contradiction—a stable distinction with mutually exclusive sides leaves no room for a third. Under the name of the law stand two theses. **Binarity**—“no third is given”; exhaustiveness follows from it: the two options cover the whole field of what can be. Binarity is derived, including for an uncompleted act; this is proved ([[Formalization]]). **Totality**—“the assignment has always already happened”—does not follow from binarity: it is a mode of reckoning about what is present, not a thesis about the world; the formalization adopts it explicitly, as a separate assumption. Which of the options has come out is answered by [[Sufficient Reason]]: assignment is an action, and it answers to a ground. For a detailed analysis—contraries and contradictories, Brouwer’s objection, and the reply to intuitionism—see [[Excluded Middle]].

### Sufficient Reason — Self-Grounding

**Every distinction has a sufficient reason for being that distinction rather than another.**

Sufficient Reason is a **meta-law**: it operates over the others as the very working of logic in which grounding is possible at all, and thereby makes everything else possible. Each moment of the act of distinction is grounded, and the act as a whole has a ground for occurring as it does and not otherwise; without Sufficient Reason the other laws would remain empty formal requirements.

The primary distinction is **self-grounding**: the ground of the distinction *A* | not-*A* is the very difference between *A* and not-*A*. Nothing prior to the distinction (it is primary) and nothing external to it (that would require another distinction) can be its ground. This is not a circle: the ground is internal to the structure of the distinction—the difference between *A* and not-*A* is constitutive of what *A* and not-*A* are. The the traditional formulation (Leibniz) invites a regress of “the ground of the ground”; here the regress is blocked at the root: the primary distinction is self-grounding, and subsequent distinctions find their grounds in prior ones. For a detailed analysis—*causa* and *ratio*, the trilemma, the objections of Kant, Hume, quantum mechanics, and Russell—see [[Sufficient Reason]].

### Order — Ordering

**The structure of distinction exhibits inherent sequence and hierarchy.**

The distinguished and the background occupy asymmetrical positions: *A* is what is singled out, not-*A* is what remains; there is a direction—from the undistinguished to the distinguished. This asymmetry is the template for all subsequent hierarchical relations: that which operates presupposes that upon which it operates. And subsequent distinctions depend on prior ones: the distinction of *B* within *A* presupposes the distinction of *A*—this is how the hierarchy of levels is structured.

The **[[Order|Law of Order]]** gives the relation between what is distinguished—the influence of one on another and their interaction, in sequence and by levels—and thereby makes becoming possible: the cause precedes the effect, the ground precedes the grounded, and one level of reality determines the conditions for another. The law has two dimensions. The **Principle of Sequence** (the horizontal) sets the direction: the stages go in a determinate order, and each creates the conditions for the next. The **Principle of Hierarchy** (the vertical) sets the levels: elements → operations on elements → operations on operations; each level presupposes the one below.

Order here is a law, and in reality it is unbreakable; the existing order of things—an arrangement that is kept or broken—is the subject of [[Ethics|ethics]]. What is broken in thinking is the existing order of reasoning; the law itself stays in force, which is why the violation yields an error. There are three ways. *The skipped stage*: a stage is not passed, and the conclusion stands without support. *Reverse movement*: reasoning runs from the conclusion to the grounds. *Level confusion*: an operation is taken as its own object, the organizer is identified with the organized—this is how [[Russells Paradox|Russell's Paradox]] and the [[Liar Paradox]] are built; it is an error of construction, impossible in a well-formed statement. The skipped stage and reverse movement are violations along the horizontal; level confusion is a violation along the vertical. Gödel’s incompleteness theorems—theorems, not paradoxes—show the necessity of hierarchical order: formal systems are grounded by systems of a higher level, ascending to logic itself as the ground. For a detailed analysis—places and the assignment of statuses, the preservation of distinctions, the history of both dimensions—see [[Order]].

## The Unity of the Laws

The five laws are five aspects of a single phenomenon: the structure of primary distinction. Each law answers its own question, but all the questions concern one act. Their unity is the unity of a structured whole: the aspects occupy different positions within it.

| --------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| [[Identity]]          | Stability       | Differentiation                                                           | Primary                                     |
| [[Non-Contradiction]] | Exclusivity     | Interaction                                                               | Primary                                     |
| [[Excluded Middle]]   | Exhaustiveness  | Difference                                                                | Derived from Identity and Non-Contradiction |
| [[Sufficient Reason]] | Self-grounding  | Everything else; gives the cause                                          | Meta-law—grounding                          |
| [[Order]]             | Ordering        | The relation between what is distinguished: sequence and levels; becoming | Meta-law—the law of structuredness itself   |

*In the author’s articles the laws are designated L1–L5, in the order of the rows of this table, and the principles P1–P4, in the order of the section “From Laws to Principles.”*

**The order of dependence:**

- **Sufficient Reason is a meta-law.** It is the condition under which the others hold with force; without it their form is empty: remove it, and the structure loses its ground for being this structure rather than another.

- **Order is the second meta-law, the law of structuredness itself.** It makes the configuration a structure: it gives sequence (stages in a determinate order) and hierarchy (levels that presuppose lower levels). Identity, Non-Contradiction, and Excluded Middle say *what* must hold in a distinction; Sufficient Reason says *why* it holds; Order says *how*: in what form distinctions stand in relation to one another, in what sequence and on what level. It is irreducible to the first three: stability, exclusivity, and exhaustiveness are silent about the directionality of distinction and about the dependence of later distinctions on earlier ones.

- **Identity and Non-Contradiction are primary.** Each articulates an irreducible feature of the act of distinction—stability and exclusivity—neither of which derives from the other. Together they give the demarcation of the distinction.

- **Excluded Middle is a consequence** of the joint operation of Identity and Non-Contradiction: given a stable distinction with mutually exclusive sides, the exhaustiveness of the division is no longer a further condition but a conclusion.

**Why there are five laws.** The structure of distinction has five distinguishable aspects: something is distinguished (Identity), exclusively (Non-Contradiction), exhaustively (Excluded Middle), on a ground (Sufficient Reason), in an ordered structure (Order). Five is the count of the distinguishable features of one structure, read at their proper levels. Traditional logic knew three of them—Identity, Non-Contradiction, and Excluded Middle; since Leibniz, Sufficient Reason was added to them as a fourth, though it was often assigned to [[Metaphysics|metaphysics]] rather than to logic; the Law of Order is set out here as the fifth law of logic. Further principles—concerning boundaries, limitation, and the construction of systems—are derived from these five: the laws serve as the basis from which the subsequent logical and mathematical principles follow, the principles that underlie all [[Theory of Systems|existing systems]].

## From Laws to Principles

From the five laws four principles are derived, on which the [[Theory of Systems]] is built as a foundation for mathematics—an alternative to axiomatic set theory. The difference is significant: Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory postulates axioms in order to avoid the paradoxes—a pragmatic measure; here the same paradoxes are blocked by structural necessity, which flows from the nature of determinacy.

- **Hierarchy**—a system and its elements stand at different levels: a system is never an element of itself. From Identity and Order: the organizer and the organized have distinct functional roles; identifying them collapses the distinction that constitutes the system. It does the work of ZFC’s Axiom of Regularity—but as a derived necessity rather than a stipulation.

- **Criterion Precedence**—the criterion that defines a system is established before questions of membership. From Order (sequence): the question “Is x a member of system S?” is meaningful only once S has been defined. It blocks impredicative definitions—the source of paradoxes.

- **Intensional Identity**—systems are identical when their defining criteria are equivalent; sameness of elements does not give identity. From Identity and Sufficient Reason: identity is grounded in the criterion of distinction.

- **Finite Actuality**—any system is finite at any moment; infinity is a quality of processes (their unboundedness), not a quantity and not a completed object. From Order: hierarchical order requires the completion of a level before the transition to the next.

Set theory begins with the undefined notions of set and membership; the [[Theory of Systems]] begins with distinction and derives the concept of system as organized multiplicity.

**Infinity as process.** What exists is the successor operation and the process of generating numbers; “the set of all natural numbers” is a manner of speaking for this unbounded process. Hence: the comparison of infinities is a comparison of rates and structures of generation; the continuum is an indefinitely refinable structure (between any two reals, new ones can be distinguished: the process of refinement is unbounded); analysis is limit, continuity, and convergence in terms of what can be achieved by a continued process. Mathematics proceeds in full rigor with potential infinity—this is the course of the [[Mathematics]] branch.

## The Architecture of Reasoning

A correct inference is built according to the laws and passes through the six [[Domains|domains of reasoning]]—Recognition → Clarification → Framework Selection → Comparison → Inference → Reflection (the sequence is the horizontal dimension of Order). Each domain is governed by a principle derived from the laws, and every fallacy receives an address in this architecture.

A violation of the laws yields a characteristic error. The classical [[Paradox|paradoxes]] are diagnosable violations of the structure of distinction—errors of construction:

- **[[Russells Paradox|Russell's Paradox]]** (“the set of all sets that do not contain themselves”)—a violation of Hierarchy: a system is asked to be at once the organizer (the bearer of the criterion) and an organized element. This is level confusion; an entity occupying two levels in the same respect is impossible—the construction is ill-formed.

- **[[Liar Paradox|The “Liar”]]** (“this statement is false”)—a violation of Criterion Precedence: the truth value is determined through that very value before it has been established. The statement presupposes what it is supposed to determine—a violation of sequence. Along the vertical it is level confusion: evaluation stands one level above what is evaluated, and in the Liar it is placed inside it. The process of determination has no ground.

- **[[Cantors Paradox|Cantor's Paradox]]** (“the set of all sets”)—a violation of Hierarchy and Finite Actuality: the collection of all sets is taken as a completed object capable of being an element of another collection. With infinity as process, there is no completed collection of all sets—there are only indefinitely expandable domains.

The framework gives more than protection against paradoxes—it gives their explanation: each turns out to be a specific violation of the structure of distinction, and recognizing the violation shows where the contradiction comes from. A well-formed statement makes the paradox impossible: there are no paradoxes in reality.

The taxonomy of fallacies is built by the same architecture: **[[Fallacies]] = a violation of the architecture of reasoning**; five types of violation exhaust all kinds of failure—from the substitution of manipulation for reasoning to systemic syndromes.

All of this is the subject of a separate branch: the [[Architecture of Reasoning]] (the domains, the catalogs of fallacies and paradoxes).

## Logic as the Structure of Being

Logic is not an instrument that we apply to reality. It is the structure without which there is no reality. We neither create it nor choose it. Even the attempt to think the “illogical” uses logic for its own construction. Beyond its bounds there is nothing that could exist.

If logic is the structure of reality itself, our relation to it changes. When we reason rightly, we are doing more than “following rules.” We *align* consciousness with the architecture of reality—we orient it toward truth and make reasoning a path to ***absolute truth***. Thinking becomes participation in the Logos rather than a manipulation of abstractions.

Fallacies are more than violations of formal rules. They are a rupture of the connection between the mind and the structure of being. What does this mean in practice? When we draw a conclusion from an unverified premise, our conclusion does not correspond to what is. We think one thing; reality is another. Actions based on such a conclusion miss their goal and only breed illusions. This is the “rupture”—the mismatch between our picture and what *is*.

Aligning consciousness with the structure of reality is the key shift in the paradigm of a thinking person. Instead of creating projections and illusions, or using logic to justify one’s own position, the [[Philosophy|philosopher]] strives to purify perception in order to see what already *is*. Otherwise reasoning serves preference, selects the evidence that confirms prejudices, defends positions instead of seeking clarity, and avoids inconvenient truths.

We do not impose logic on the world. We *discover* it—because we ourselves are part of this structure. Our thinking works not in spite of reality but through participation in the same principle that organizes all that is.

## Summary

1. **Logic is the structure of existence.** Its laws are properties of being, given together with it: thought finds them rather than establishes them.
2. **Why there is something:** the very possibility of existence, given its grounds, is necessarily realized. The grounds are Logic and the Source: they must be and always are. What is possible within reality needs, in addition, an action—a cause.
3. **The derivation begins with the first principle**—“something exists”; its first consequence is the distinction *A* | not-*A*.
4. **The five laws are five aspects of one act:** Identity (stability), Non-Contradiction (exclusivity), Excluded Middle (exhaustiveness), Sufficient Reason (self-grounding), and Order (ordering).
5. **Two meta-laws.** Sufficient Reason makes everything else possible and gives the cause; Order gives the relation between what is distinguished—sequence and levels.
6. **From the laws come the four principles** of the Theory of Systems: Hierarchy, Criterion Precedence, Intensional Identity, and Finite Actuality; infinity is a process, not a completed object.
7. **Reasoning is correct when it follows the laws.** A violation of the laws in thought yields an error; in reality the laws are unbreakable—there are no paradoxes in it.
