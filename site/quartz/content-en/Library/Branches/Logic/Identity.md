The Law of Identity: ***A = A*** · ***principium identitatis***

***A* is *A*: everything is identical to itself; a thing is what it is.**

The law seems too obvious to be worth naming. Yet all determinacy stands on it: to be something is to be itself. If *A* does not remain itself, words lose their meaning, concepts their boundaries, and thought its footing.

## Etymology

**Identity.** Late Latin *identitas*, from *idem*, “the same”: identity is being the same. The Greek ταὐτότης (tautotēs), from τὸ αὐτό (to auto), “the same thing,” is built in the same way, and so is the Russian *tozhdestvo*, from Church Slavonic *tozhde*, “the same.” The three languages say one thing: to be identical is to be the same. Hence the question of this page: what exactly remains the same?

## Place among the Laws

Among the five laws ([[Logic]]), Identity is one of the two **primary** laws: it articulates an irreducible feature of the act of distinction—*stability*. For the distinction to occur, the distinguished must remain the same throughout the act of distinction: if *A* changed in the very act of being marked out, there would be no determinate *A* to mark out. Identity nonetheless allows for change: it requires only that *A* be identical to itself at any given moment and in any given respect; change is the transition from *A*-at-t₁ to *A*-at-t₂, and each state is identical to itself. The failure of identity is the collapse of determinacy: if *A* ≠ *A*, there is simply no single *A*—only a confusion of different things under one sign.

The second primary law is [[Non-Contradiction]]: neither of the two derives from the other. [[Excluded Middle]] is derived from their joint operation; [[Sufficient Reason]] and [[Order]] are meta-laws.

The law has three facets. *Ontologically:* each thing is identical to itself at every moment; through all change and development, *A* remains *A* in its essence (what the essence is—see “What Exactly Remains” below). *Logically:* every statement, if true, is true. *Epistemologically:* a concept must keep its meaning throughout the course of reasoning.[^1] All three facets express one principle: *something is what it is*.

## History

Parmenides was the first to speak of identity explicitly. In the poem *On Nature* (first half of the fifth century BCE) he asserts “it is”—and that “it is not” is unthinkable. Whatever can be thought, is; there is no non-being. This is the first ontological identity: being is identical to itself.[^2] Plato, in the *Sophist*, sets the same beside the other: each of the three kinds—being, rest, motion—is “other than the remaining two, but the same as itself.”[^3] Aristotle does not single out identity as a separate law, but he keeps it at the foundation. He defines identity—sameness—as a kind of unity: a thing is said to be the same as itself when one thing is treated as two. And the question why a thing is itself he calls an empty one.[^4] For Aristotle, the laws of logic are the structure of being: they say what everything that is must be like. That position is adopted here.

In the early fourteenth century Antonius Andreae, a pupil of Duns Scotus, named identity the first principle, ahead of non-contradiction: *ens est ens*, “what is, is.”[^5] The explicit formula “*A* is *A*” was given by Leibniz: in Leibniz, identity stands beside non-contradiction at the foundation of every true judgment.[^6] Leibniz also gave the law its reverse side—the principle of the identity of indiscernibles (*principium identitatis indiscernibilium*): if two things are indiscernible in all their properties, they are one and the same thing.[^7] From *A = A* only the converse follows: one thing coincides with itself in everything. The principle itself rests here on the act of distinction: to be two is to be distinguished; where there is no difference in anything, there are not two things.

Fichte went further still. In the *Foundations of the Entire Science of Knowledge* (1794–1795) he made “I am I” the first, unconditioned principle of all knowledge: the certainty of the formula *A = A* rests on an original *act*—the self-positing of the I.[^8] Identity turns from a tautology into a deed-act (*Tathandlung*): something is what it is because it holds itself as itself. There is a kinship: here, too, identity is a feature of an act. The ground is different: for Fichte the law rests on the act of the I; here the law is given together with being, and the act of distinction presents it.

Hegel rebelled against the law of identity as a first principle. Abstract identity *A = A*, he argued in the *Science of Logic*, is empty and one-sided: it is “the expression of an empty tautology,” whereas genuine identity carries difference within itself.[^9] The objection does not touch the law. The law is not meant to be informative: it holds one thing only—that *A* is what it is. This is a condition: the form within which content can exist and change at all (see “What Exactly Remains” below). Distinctness stands *on* identity: for *A* to differ from *B*, each must first be itself. This is what it means to say that identity makes differentiation possible.

Finally, Frege showed where identity becomes informative. “*A = A*” is empty, but “*A = B*” can carry a discovery: that “the morning star” and “the evening star” are one and the same planet, Venus, was learned from astronomy. How can one and the same relation of a thing to itself be now empty, now informative? Frege’s answer is the distinction between *sense* and *reference* (*Sinn* and *Bedeutung*): “*A*” and “*B*” have one reference—the thing itself—and different senses: the ways in which it is given.[^10] This fits the position adopted here exactly: a thing is identical to itself—that is a property of the thing; the informativeness of “*A = B*” lies on the side of our access to it. The different criteria of “the same” are examined below.

## What the Law Makes Possible

In being, the law makes **differentiation** possible: something is itself and therefore differs from what is other. In knowing, three things stand on this.

**Recognition.** We recognize things because they remain themselves: an apple is an apple through all the variety of cultivars. Without identity, every moment would be an encounter with the unknown. The first domain of reasoning stands on this—[[1 Recognition|registering what is there]].

**Definition.** We can say what “justice” or “triangle” is because these concepts keep their meaning. If “justice” means one thing at the beginning of a conversation and another at the end, the conversation is meaningless. Socrates took care to agree on the definition of a subject before reasoning about it. The second domain stands on this—[[2 Clarification|clarification]].

**Communication.** Words work because they keep their meaning. When I say “table,” you understand what is meant. Without identity, language is impossible.

## What Exactly Remains: The Characteristics of *A*

The law gives stability. What exactly in *A* is stable when *A* lives and changes: *A*₁ → *A*₂ → *A*₃? Everything determinate is organized as a system: rules, roles, elements ([[Theory of Systems]]). An analysis along this structure gives a precise answer. A characteristic of *A* is a **role** in the system; the way the characteristic is manifested is an **element** occupying the role, and the manifestation is changeable for all characteristics. Roles differ in status. **A critical characteristic is a necessary role**: it must be filled as long as *A* is—without it *A* is no longer *A*. **An optional characteristic is a permitted role**: the rules allow it, and its filling is contingent—an element may or may not be there.

The tradition knows this pair through Aristotle—as the essential and the accidental. In its first sense, the accidental (συμβεβηκός, symbebēkos) belongs to a thing “neither of necessity nor for the most part”; Aristotle’s example is a treasure found while digging a hole for a plant.[^11] Here the pair is read differently: the optional is not a matter of chance—the role is given by the rules; only its filling is contingent.

The essence of *A* is its constitution: the rules and the necessary roles; it is what holds the identity of *A*. A change of manifestation *A*₁ → *A*₂ is a change of elements in roles and the filling or vacating of permitted roles under an unchanged constitution. A human being remains human with a five-fingered hand, with a six-fingered one, and with no hand at all: “hand” is a permitted role; the hand itself, whatever it is like, is the element occupying it; without a hand the role is empty.

Heraclitus already said this: “Upon those who step into the same rivers, other and yet other waters flow.”[^12] The river is the same; the waters are different: the role stands, the elements change.

Two theorems of stability follow from the structure of *A* (both proved—[[Formalization]]). First: **the essence is always manifested as long as *A* is**—a necessary role without an element would mean that there is no *A*; the potential status (“can be, but is not”) is available only to optional characteristics. Second: **“a change of essence as a change of *A*” is an impossible construction**: a change in the status of a necessary role is not a change of *A* but another *A* (registry entry: [[What Is Not and Cannot Be]]). Changeability of manifestation, meanwhile, is the norm for critical characteristics too: the element changes, the role stands.

On the constitution of a single system, the statuses of roles complete the modal square ([[Metaphysics|ontological principles]]):

- a necessary role: “cannot not be” (for *A*);
- a permitted role that is filled: “can be—and is”;
- a permitted role that is empty: “can be, but is not”;
- a role that the rules do not give: “cannot be”—it is not in the field of *A*.

One last point: the question “is it the same *A*?” is incomplete until the criterion is named—what exactly is being asked. There are four criteria. The **structural** and the **functional** ask about the constitution: about the rules and about the roles and the continuity of their performance. The **material** asks about the composition: whether the elements are the same. The **conceptual** asks about the status assigned by witnesses. One object can be the same by one criterion and different by another: there is no contradiction, only different answers to different questions. A question without a criterion is an error of construction; it is analyzed in [[Ship of Theseus]]. The ship was repaired plank by plank until every plank had been replaced; a second ship was assembled from the planks removed. Which of the two is the same ship?

| Criterion | The repaired ship | The reassembled ship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structural—by the rules | the same | the same |
| Functional—by the roles | the same | different |
| Material—by the elements | different | the same |
| Conceptual—by status among witnesses | the same | disputed |

The same entry discusses the process that maintains identity: the object takes part in it, and the identity of the process itself is held by its constitution, not by its states—the elements change, the rule stands.

## Violations

In reality the law is unbreakable; it is broken in thinking, and most often this goes unnoticed. The word stays the same while the concept under it changes: one word carries different meanings for different people, and for one person at different moments of a conversation. In the [[Fallacy Catalog|fallacy catalog]] this is a single mechanism—[[2.2 Domain 2 Clarification#Meaning Drift|meaning drift]]; its forms differ.

*Concept substitution.* At the start of a dispute “freedom” means the absence of coercion. By the end it means the opportunity for self-realization. The conclusion seems logical, but it is built on a substitution: the word is the same, the concept is different.

*[[Equivocation]].* “Science has proved that man is an animal. Animals have no rights. Therefore man has no rights.” The word “animal” is used in different senses in the first and second sentences. The Law of Identity is violated.

*Gradual shift.* In a long course of reasoning a concept gradually shifts, unnoticed by the speaker. By the end it means not quite what it meant at the beginning. The conclusion rests on a distortion.

## Summary

1. **The Law of Identity: *A = A***—everything is identical to itself. Its property is stability; it is one of the two primary laws.
2. **It makes differentiation possible:** something is itself and therefore differs from what is other. In knowing, three things stand on it: recognition, definition, and communication.
3. **The law is the structure that allows content to exist:** what exactly *A* is—that is the task of substantive inquiry.
4. **The law allows for change.** The essence of *A* is its constitution: the rules and the necessary roles. The manifestation changes; the constitution stands.
5. **“Is it the same *A*?” is a question with a criterion:** structural, functional, material, conceptual.
6. **In reality the law is unbreakable.** In thinking it is violated by meaning drift: concept substitution, equivocation, gradual shift.

A stable *A* raises the next question: what can *A* *not* be? The answer is the second primary law, [[Non-Contradiction]].
