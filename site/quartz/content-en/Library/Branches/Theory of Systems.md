The structure of every system is Rules, Roles, and Elements. From this triad the arrangement of any whole is derived: from an atom to a piece of reasoning. The triad is the core of the whole theory.

## Etymology

**System.** Greek σύστημα (systēma), “that which is put together; a whole made of parts,” ← συνίστημι (synistēmi), “to set together, to compose”: σύν (syn), “together,” + ἵστημι (histēmi), “to set, to stand.” A system is that which *stands together*: composed in such a way that it holds. The Stoics defined the cosmos as “a system of heaven and earth”;[^1] the theorists of music gave the name to a scale composed of intervals; it came into the modern languages through Latin. The Latin **constitution** carries the same image: *constitutio* ← *con-*, “together,” + *statuere*, “to set up,” ← *stare*, “to stand”: that which is set up together; this is why the essence of a system is called its constitution below.

**Three components—three words.** **Rule** ← Old French *reule* ← Latin *regula*—a straight stick, a measure, from *regere*, “to direct, to rule.” The Russian word, *pravilo*, says the same by another root: Proto-Slavic \*pravъ, “straight, right”—the root of *pravda*, “truth” ([[Truth and Absolute Truth]]). **Role** ← French *rôle* ← Medieval Latin *rotulus*, “a roll”: the rolled sheet with an actor’s lines—a role is a prescribed part, that which is assigned to the one who occupies it. **Element** ← Latin *elementum*, “first principle, element; a letter of the alphabet”: that of which something is composed—and the Romans called the letters *elementa*. The words keep the order of derivation: the rule is the measure, the role is the part assigned by the measure, the element is that which fills it.

## The Triad: Rules · Roles · Elements

A system is an organized multiplicity. To exist as a determinate whole it needs three components—and each is derived from the laws of [[Logic|logic]]:

- **Rules**—*how* the assignment of roles is arranged: the operations, relations, and constraints that determine which elements carry which roles. This corresponds to the Law of [[Order]]: the rules give the structure that makes a system a system and not a collection.
- **Roles**—*what* an element is significant *for*. An element without a role—without a reason to be distinguished—is, within the system, indistinguishable from the non-existent: to exist significantly in a system is to carry a role. This corresponds to the Law of [[Sufficient Reason]].
- **Elements**—*what* exists in the system. A system without elements is empty: not a system but only its possibility. This corresponds to the Law of [[Identity]]: every element is identical to itself and distinct from the others.

| Component | Question | Law | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rules | How is it arranged? | Order | Operations and constraints |
| Roles | What is it significant for? | Sufficient Reason | Assignment and grounding |
| Elements | What exists? | Identity | The substrate of the system |

The asymmetry of ranks in the triad: **Rules** determine **Roles**, roles distinguish **Elements**, elements carry the system. An example is an optimization problem: the rules lay down how the status of maximum is assigned to a definite position—comparisons, order, convergence (*how*); the role “maximum” singles out the significant point (*what for*); the elements are the points of the domain (*what*).

## The Generating Order and the Representation Theorem

Building a system is a road, and it runs in one order. First stands the **ground**: what the system is for—its goal; the Law of Sufficient Reason is always the foundation, and without a ground there is not a single assignment: the role is not assigned but merely *named*. From the goal the **rules** are born: they state concretely which roles with which status are needed—which are necessary, which permitted. The rules assign the **roles**—the places of the system; the roles are filled by **elements**: in them the system is manifest. So the structure arises: the triad read in the order of building—**Goal → Rules → Roles → Elements**. And the road does not end at the elements: every element that fills a role is itself a system with its own goal and rules, and the system once built is ready to stand as an element in a larger one: the building itself places the system in a **hierarchy**—to name a system is to name its tier as well.

The conclusion is the **representation theorem: system = ground ⊕ structure ⊕ level**. The triple (Rules, Roles, Elements) does not by itself determine a system—this is proved ([[Formalization]]); the sufficient ground and the place in the hierarchy are components of the same standing, derived by the very course of the building. The asymmetry of ranks (above) is proved as well: the Rules are not determined by the pair (Roles, Elements)—and it is the Rules that decide which systems come together at all. Sufficient Reason enters a system twice: as the goal—the ground of the whole system—and as the role—the ground of each element; Order enters as the rules within and as the level without.

### The Goal of a System: Task and Efficiency

**The task of a system is its goal**—what is sought when the system is formed. Formation unfolds in three questions: how to define the task of the system · how to define the rules that answer to the task · how to establish the roles and the mechanisms for choosing elements for the greatest efficiency; the order is the same—from the goal come the rules, and the rules determine the roles and the selection of elements. Efficiency is the measure of the result of formation: a rightly built system is the efficient work of the elements through right assignment to roles according to the established rules; in this lies its ethical meaning ([[Ethics]]).

> [!example]- Case: chess—a system built from its goal
> The goal is the game of chess itself: a contest of two wills under equal rules. From the goal come the rules: the board, the initial position, the moves, the outcomes. The rules specify the roles: the **operators of the system**—the player of White and the player of Black—and the **piece roles**, all of them necessary: without the full set a game cannot begin. The **field** is the “where” and the “how” of the roles: the squares of the board and the side field of captured pieces—every filled role has its place in the common field of the system. The roles are filled by elements, and the manifestation is free: at the board a human or a program, the board wooden or on a screen, the pieces of any material—the constitution is the same, and chess is the same. **A single game is an instance of chess** (one game—one playing of chess); by its outcome the rules assign statuses to the operators: a win to one—or a draw to both. A piece lives by the statuses “on the field” / “off the field”: a capture does not empty the role—a captured queen remains a queen, having changed its status and place; and the promotion of a pawn is not a change of its essence: the pawn goes “off the field,” and another piece stands on its square—**the pawn remains a pawn**. The frame here is two-layered: in the legality of moves the system is hard—an impossible move cannot be made; in the choice among legal moves the operators stand in a soft position: a right and a wrong move exist but are not marked in advance—here live both oversights and mastery.

> [!example]- Case: the orchestra—a system built from its goal
> The goal is to perform music. From the goal come the rules: tuning, the discipline of the ensemble, and the scores; **a part is not a role but a prescription of the rules** for a role. The rules specify the roles: the **operators**—the conductor and the musicians—and the **instrumental roles**: violins, oboe, timpani; the instruments are roles, not things. The **field** is the “where” and the “how”: the stage and the seating; every filled role has its place in the common field, from the conductor’s podium to the last desk. The roles are filled by elements: particular people and particular instruments—the membership changes, and the orchestra remains itself as long as the constitution stands. **A composition is an instance**: one work—one performance. The contrast with chess lies in the frame of the membership: in chess the set of piece roles is fixed by the game itself; in the orchestra the necessity of the instrumental roles is set by the score of the instance—for this composition the oboe is necessary, for that one it is not provided for: the constitution by roles is fixed in chess and varies with the composition in the orchestra.

## Levels and Hierarchy

The elements of a system of level n+1 are themselves systems of level n: piece → game → tournament. The triad works *within* each level, and mixing its categories within a level amounts to violating the boundary of levels.

The hierarchy of levels is strict: no level precedes itself—in the [[Formalization|formalization]] this is a theorem (the irreflexivity of the order of levels), not an axiom. Hence: a system is never an element of itself—this is the principle of **Hierarchy**. The criterion that defines a system is established before questions of membership—the principle of **Criterion Precedence**; it speaks of the level below the system—of what can occupy its roles—and this requirement is built into the very type of a criterion. The Theory of Systems has four principles; the other two are **Intensional Identity** (the section on the constitution, below) and **Finite Actuality** (the section on processes). All four are derived from the laws ([[Logic#From Laws to Principles|from laws to principles]]).

Russell’s paradox does not even need to be ruled out here—it **does not form**: “the set of all sets that do not contain themselves” would require a criterion of the same level as the system being defined, that is, a proof of “a level below itself”—and this is refuted. The difference from the classical approach is significant: Russell’s theory of types *imposes* the hierarchy; Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory restricts the formation of sets by axioms; here the hierarchy is *derived* from the structure of distinction, and the paradoxical construction turns out to be an error of construction ([[Logic]]). The vertical, moreover, is a **tower of full embeddings** with no limiting level: the hierarchy has no completed “top storey”—Finite Actuality works on the levels themselves. Logic in this tower is not a storey but the foundation on which everything is raised: it is given before any building ([[Metaphysics]]).

## Soft and Hard System

**A soft system** is one whose rules allow a right and a wrong choice, while the [[Metaphysics|operator]] does not know in advance which is which. **A hard system** is one in which a wrong choice is impossible. Laws are broken in neither; rules—constructions composed for the goal of a system—can be broken in a soft system. Rules assign a status to any choice; a wrong choice violates the existing [[Order#In Ethics|order]].

The division carries ethics: [[Ethics|ethics]] exists only in soft systems—where a wrong choice is possible. In a hard system there is execution, not ethics: a stone is not “in the wrong.”

## Constitution and the Identity of a System

What exactly in a system holds its identity while it lives and changes? A characteristic of a system is a **role**: a critical one is a necessary role, which must be filled as long as the system exists; an optional one is a permitted role, and its filling is contingent. A manifestation is an **element** in a role. The essence of a system is its **constitution**: the rules and the necessary roles; and the constitution is not a given but **the result of composing the rules on the basis of the goal**: the generating order carries the goal through to the essence. Manifestations change freely—the identity stands, and the statuses of the roles complete the modal square on the constitution of a single system.

**The defining criterion of a system is unfolded into its constitution.** The criterion is that by which a system is distinguished from what is other: it answers *which* system this is. In a system in its full structure this question is answered by the constitution—the rules and the necessary roles—together with the goal from which it is composed. This is the principle of **Intensional Identity**: systems are identical when their defining criteria are equivalent; sameness of elements does not give identity ([[Logic#From Laws to Principles|from laws to principles]]). Two players with the same pieces on the same board can play losing chess—capturing is compulsory, and the one who gives away all the pieces wins: the elements coincide, the constitution is different—and so is the system. And the twenty games of a simultaneous exhibition are one system in twenty instances: the instances are distinguished by their lives—steps and states.

**A constitution by roles may be fixed or variable.** Fixed means always the same: in chess the set of piece roles is given by the game itself, and every game begins with the full set. Variable means depending on the goal-ground: in the orchestra the necessity of the instrumental roles is set by the score of the composition—for this one the oboe is necessary, for that one it is not provided for. Both arrangements are constitutions in the full sense: the difference comes from the goal the rules serve. A variable constitution remains one: the rule itself is constant—the necessary roles are assigned by the score.

**Identity through time is also held by the constitution.** A game on another board or in mirrored notation is the same life in another manifestation (dynamics, below); a change in the status of a necessary role is not a change of *A* but another *A*: a pawn that has reached the last rank remains a pawn—another piece stands on its square. The full analysis, with the table of the two ships, is in [[Identity]]; the impossible “change of essence” is an entry in the registry [[What Is Not and Cannot Be]].

Characteristics as roles, the modal square of the constitution, and the constitution as a condition on the process of binding are proved ([[Formalization]]).

## The Interaction of Systems

Systems do not add up like numbers—addition and subtraction live a storey below, in the system of mathematics. Systems **interact**, and the interaction has a name and a law.

**A link is the rule of interaction of systems.** While there is no link, systems stand side by side: neighbourhood, alternation—but not a whole. A link appears as a rule—and everything that goes by rules is itself the building of a system: **the interaction of systems gives birth to a new system**, in which both are represented. A game of chess on stage accompanied by an orchestra: the orchestra does not play a composition—it accompanies the unfolding of the game, rendering in music what happens on the board; the link-rule has sewn two systems into a third, reducible to neither.

**The boundaries of systems are drawn by the operator.** Where one system ends and the whole begins is a question of boundaries, and boundaries are taken by the operator who interacts with the systems—just as “the string section” is not a part of the orchestra but a construction by a rule of formation, taken by an observer.

**A tournament is a new system, not a “sum of games.”** The goal is a series of games of chess; the rules are the regulations; the roles are the participants as operators and the pairings of the rounds; the field is where it takes place. By the results of the pairings the rules assign statuses—and “the strongest” is a result, not the goal. And the tournament table is yet another, separate system: it takes the results of the games—statuses—as its inputs. This is how the storeys grow: **record-systems stand on top of the statuses of other systems**—the table over the tournament, the notation over the game.

**Systems intersect in elements.** One element can occupy roles in many systems at once: one person stands both in an orchestra and at the chessboard. The same holds for instances of one system: in a simultaneous exhibition one and the same master stands in the role of “player” in twenty games. The systems do not merge—they intersect in a common element.

The canon of interaction is proved: a linked whole is not reducible to systems standing side by side; systems intersect in a common element without merging; a record-system reads statuses only ([[Formalization]]).

## A System in Time: Dynamics

A system lives by **moves**: a move is a rule of transition between states. The rules give the **pool** of available moves; the will of the [[Metaphysics|operator]] chooses—not from the pool, however, but from its own **list**: a slice of the pool that has passed through perception. Some moves the operator does not even consider—attention was not turned to them: a potentially better move was in the pool but did not get onto the list; an oversight is a legal move outside the list. A trajectory of moves is an instance of the life of the system: a game.

**Inner time.** The clock of a system is the count of its steps: the move number. The pieces are set up—step 0: there is no step yet; it is there as a potential—“can be, but is not.” Every step taken actualizes the potential—and is irreversible.

**A state is attached to its step—and never returns.** Only a **slice** returns: the arrangement of the pieces on the board is a projection of the state, not the state itself. The rules on the repetition of a position themselves prove this: to identical boards they assign different consequences—the first occurrence of a position changes nothing, the second carries the threat of the end, the third gives the right to claim a draw, the fifth ends the game; different consequences under the rules mean different states. A metaphysical consequence: a witness lives in an eternal “now”—at the given step ([[Metaphysics#Time from Observation|time from observation]]); “the return of a past state” is a construction of the impossible ([[What Is Not and Cannot Be]]).

**The clock and zone statistics.** The clock grows—and on top of the steps stand the records of an observer. **Zone statistics** is the count of elements by the zones of the common field at each step: how many pieces are on the board, how many on the side field of captured pieces; a change in it betrays an event—a change of zone and a change of status. About the count on the board, observation of the rules yields a conclusion: it only falls or stays. **Rules give; they do not forbid**: they list the possible actions—a capture removes a piece from the board, a promotion puts a piece in the place of the departed pawn—and an action that would increase the count on the board is not on the list. “It will not grow” is a conclusion of the observer, held by the completeness of the rules: everything that happens in a system happens by the rules. Such a conclusion is a theorem about the system; a count that does not grow along a trajectory is called, in the theory of dynamical systems, a Lyapunov function. It cuts life into irreversible layers: the statistics has fallen from thirty-two to thirty-one—the layer “32” is left behind: within a layer the game may linger, but the count has one way to go—down. The spending of another irreversible count—of captures and pawn moves—is watched by the rules themselves. When the irreversible is long unspent, the fifty-move rule gives the right to a draw, and the seventy-five-move rule ends the game: there is no new distinction, and the rules stop the running.

**Completion.** The completion of an instance is a **status of a step**: checkmate and stalemate are statuses that the rules assign to a step—to a relation of the pieces on the field—closing the game; by them the operators receive the statuses of the outcome: a win to one or a draw to both. Dynamics also has a role of another kind—the **attractor**: a direction that the trajectory approaches without bound, never occupying it; the attractor is a role-limit, a face of the boundary of the theory (below).

**The same life in another manifestation.** A game replayed on another board, or written in mirrored notation, is a conjugate dynamics: a translation of one manifestation into another that preserves the course of life—what was a transition remains a transition, what completed completes. The identity of the dynamics is held by the constitution, not by the manifestation—like the [[Identity|identity]] of the system itself.

The dynamics of the core is proved: the arrow of steps is irreversible, the attractor is a role-limit, the count on the board does not grow; what returns is the slice, not the state ([[Formalization]]).

## The Whole beyond the Parts: Emergence

A system has properties—and they are of full standing: a property exists, has an essence, and is knowable; of a whole one may ask both “what is it made of” and “what is it like.” When a whole arises out of systems, some of its properties are inherited—carried over from the sides by the part→whole map. **Emergence is a property of a new whole born of the relationship of the sides: it belongs to none of the sides and is not carried over from them by the part→whole map; the whole is greater than the sides not in quantity but by a new property.**

**The kinds of the emergent are derived from the relationships.** What can stand between two systems? **Nothing**—neither a link nor belonging: the systems are side by side, and the whole is only a frame taken by an observer; every property of such a “pair” is carried over from the sides—to list the sides is to exhaust the whole: there is no emergence, and the whole is **separable**. **A link**—a rule of interaction: the interaction gives birth to a new system, and the sides in it **answer one another**—the property “the sides are in accord” belongs only to the whole: **the kind of response**. **Belonging**—the sides are not directly linked but are represented in an organizing system, and the whole has a **pervasive** trait that none of the sides has, nor any listing of them: **the kind of the pervasive property**.

> [!example]- Case: chess on stage—response
> The orchestra accompanies the game, rendering in music what happens on the board. The property “the music answers the board” belongs neither to the orchestra nor to the game—it is neither in the score nor in the notation; it lives only in the third system, born of the link. This is the kind of response: the sides are in accord, and the accord is a property of the whole.

> [!example]- Case: the tournament—a pervasive property
> The games of a tournament are not directly linked with one another—they have belonging to an organizing system. “The race of the leaders” is a property of the tournament: no single game has it, nor any listing of the games. And when the [[Metaphysics|operator]], choosing a move, glances at the standings in the table, the pervasive property enters the game otherwise than by a link between games: the operator has read the table-record—and it has gone onto the operator’s list of grounds for the choice.

> [!example]- Case: the simultaneous exhibition—where the whole is born
> One master at twenty boards. With a living master the exhibition is one system: an element cannot be present at two boards at once, and the order of the round arises as a result of this impossibility—not as a rule; the constraint lives in the very properties of the role “master.” Against a program there is no such constraint—at each step it can stand at any board: no single system arises, and the games remain apart. Intersection in a common element does not by itself give a kind—the whole is born where presence is limited.

**Emergence is measurable—and the measure is derived, not postulated.** A number is a record of an act of observation; counting is the summing of steps of distinction (below—the place of mathematics). A measure is a rule for assigning a number: not a marking of a completed set but a process of assignment ([[01 Measure as a Process of Assignment|measure as a process]]). The measure of emergence is the same act on the whole: the observer compares the properties of the whole with what is carried over from the sides and **counts the discrepancies**. Nothing to count—the whole is separable; the count is positive—a place has been shown where the whole is greater than the sides.

The subtheory of emergence is proved: the definition of a property not carried over from the sides, the kinds of the emergent, the measure as a count with a witness to the discrepancy ([[Formalization]]).

## Processes and Mathematics

**The place of mathematics.** Everything that exists exists in logic determinately—and is organized; organization is a function of logic, and organization carried out is a system. Hence mathematics, too, is a system: a language that organizes the description of the logical laws; “addition” and “subtraction” are its inner functions. And a number is not a thing of the world and not an element of systems: **a number is a record of an act of observation, information about a distinction made**—a witness has distinguished *A* and not-*A*, and this *A* is *one* *A*; a number is always “a unit of something,” and counting is the summing of steps of distinction: 1+1+1=3. Mathematics is derived from the act of distinction; in the same act the laws are recognized ([[Logic|the act of distinction]]; [[02 Counting as Length of a List of Distinctions|counting is the length of a list of distinctions]]). The categorical model of the core—morphisms, products and sums, bicompleteness, regularity—is a check of the Theory of Systems by the means of mathematics: a strict mirror, not a lawgiver. The model also assembles the tiers of a new whole—each by its own operation: Rules by a lattice of admissions (only compatible rules combine), Roles with a possible excess (emergence), Elements freely.

**A process is a mode of existence.** By the principle of **Finite Actuality** every system is finite at every moment; infinity is a quality of a process, its *unboundedness*, and it is never a quantity or a completed object. A process is potentiality; a completed object is a slice of it.

From this mathematics is built: the natural number series is a **process of generation** (there is an operation of succession, and “the set of all numbers” is a figure of speech for an unbounded process); a real number is a **process of approximations**: a sequence of rational approximations. On this ground the uncountability of the interval, the extreme value theorem, and the intermediate value theorem in approximate form are proved—without the axioms of infinity and choice ([[Formalization]]).

**Order gives the places: the Axiom of Choice is not needed.** Where classical mathematics turns to the Axiom of Choice, Order is enough here: “first,” “least,” “the point of maximum” have meaning only within an order. Assignment answers to a ground; when several positions fit one role, the ground is the place in the order—the status goes to the first that fits. This is the very definition of the status: “the point of maximum” means “the first of the points with the greatest value”; thus on a plateau the first of its points is assigned as the point of maximum. The Axiom of Choice asserts that a choice exists; Order gives the place, and there is nothing left to choose. Ambiguity therefore belongs to the system, not to the problem: it is a sign of the **non-completion of the system**—instability is the absence of constitutive order ([[Order]]).

## The Boundary of the Theory: The Role-Limit

Every theory has a boundary—what it cannot do—and a theory is obliged to show the reason. The Theory of Systems has one boundary, and it follows from its own principle: **Finite Actuality**—every system is finite at every moment.

**The role-limit.** A process has a direction—“where it is all going”: approximations to the square root of two, to a maximum, to a completed corpus. The direction is a role in the system of the process, but no step of the rules occupies it: at every step there is an element-approximation and no element-arrival. A role-limit is a role that no element occupies at any step: approach without arrival. Here too the rules give; they do not forbid: they list what can be done, and a step that would occupy this role is not on the list; “it will not be occupied” is a conclusion of the observer, held by the completeness of the rules—a theorem about the system of the same kind as “it will not grow” in dynamics.

**Where it is seen.** On this page the boundary has shown itself twice: the attractor of dynamics is a role-limit; the tower of levels is a vertical with no limiting storey. Its third face is the corpus of what is known: a process with no completed “everything” ([[Epistemology]]). Its mathematical faces—the square root of two for fractions, uncountability, the undecidability of the halting problem—live in the system of mathematics ([[Mathematics]]).

**The boundary cannot be removed.** To remove it would be to remove Finite Actuality—to cease to be the Theory of Systems. And “a process declared to have arrived”—an occupied role-limit—is a construction of the impossible ([[What Is Not and Cannot Be]]). The boundary is proved: a process that terminates gives an element; one that does not terminate gives a role-limit; approach without arrival is a theorem ([[Formalization]]).

## Bridges to the Branches

**Logic is the root, not a branch.** The trunk stands on it: organization is a function of logic, a system is organization carried out, and everything that exists in logic is organized. The root needs no bridge—it holds the trunk ([[Logic]]).

**The branches take their architecture from the trunk**—and the relation of the trunk to the branches is not a reference but a derivation; where it is proved, a bridge-theorem stands:

- **[[Metaphysics]]**—the witness, the will, perception. The eternal “now” is a conclusion of the dynamics of the trunk (a state is attached to its step); in the act of distinction both the laws and the structure of a system are recognized.
- **[[Epistemology]]**—slice ≠ fullness: what returns is the slice, not the state—derived in dynamics; field and slice, pool and list are one mechanics of seeing.
- **[[Ethics]]**—lives in soft systems: where a wrong choice is possible; an ethically rightly built system is right assignment to roles (the section on the goal of a system, above).
- **[[Architecture of Reasoning]]**—reasoning is a system; an oversight is a legal move outside the list: the domain of recognition; well-formedness is checkable: a paradoxical construction is an error of construction.
- **[[Mathematics]]**—a system of records of acts of observation (above—the place of mathematics); its processes carry the analysis of the trunk, its categorical model the check of the core.

Properties and observables are, moreover, of full standing in the theory itself: existence → essence → knowability—the branches take them from the trunk.

The bridge to the Architecture of Reasoning is proved: the well-formedness of reasoning is checkable—the constructions of Russell, the Liar, and Grelling turn out to be errors of construction ([[Formalization]]).

## Summary

1. **A system stands together**: σύστημα—composed in such a way that it holds; the words of the triad carry the order of derivation—rule (the measure) → role (the assigned part) → element (that which fills it).
2. **The triad is derived from the laws of logic** (Rules—Order, Roles—Sufficient Reason, Elements—Identity)—the three necessary components of any determinate system; the asymmetry of ranks: rules determine roles, roles distinguish elements.
3. **The generating order is Goal → Rules → Roles → Elements**; the **representation theorem**: system = ground ⊕ structure ⊕ level—the triple by itself does not determine a system. Sufficient Reason enters a system twice—as the goal and as the role; Order—as the rules and as the level. The task of a system is its goal; efficiency is the measure of the result of formation.
4. **The hierarchy is derived, not imposed**: the irreflexivity of the order of levels is a theorem; Russell’s paradox does not form; the tower of levels has no limiting storey, and Logic is its foundation.
5. **Four principles**—Hierarchy, Criterion Precedence, Intensional Identity, Finite Actuality—are derived from the laws ([[Logic#From Laws to Principles|from laws to principles]]).
6. **Soft and hard system**: ethics lives only where a wrong choice is possible; in a hard system there is execution.
7. **The constitution**—the rules and the necessary roles—holds the identity of a system: manifestation and membership change freely; a change of essence is another *A*. The defining criterion of a system is unfolded into its constitution: sameness of elements does not give identity.
8. **The canon of interaction**: systems do not sum—a link-rule gives birth to a new system; the boundaries are drawn by the operator; systems intersect in elements; record-systems stand on top of statuses.
9. **Dynamics**: a move is a rule of transition; a state is attached to its step and does not return—only the slice returns; the clock of a system is the count of its steps; “it will not grow” is an observer’s theorem over the completeness of the rules.
10. **Emergence**: a property of the whole outside the part→whole map; the kinds are derived from the relationships (nothing → the separable; link → response; belonging → the pervasive); the measure is a count of discrepancies.
11. **Mathematics is a system of records of acts of observation**: a number is a record of a distinction; on processes—uncountability, the extreme value, the intermediate value in approximate form, without the axioms of infinity and choice; the place in the order instead of the Axiom of Choice.
12. **The boundary of the theory is the role-limit**: approach without arrival; the unoccupiability is not a decree but a theorem; the boundary cannot be removed: to remove it would be to remove Finite Actuality.
