Mathematics is the language for describing logical laws. This page covers where the word comes from and what mathematics is, how the mathematics of processes is built, what the axioms of set theory cost, and where the finitization boundary runs; alongside are the volume “Mathematics” and selected results.

## Etymology

**Mathematics** ← Greek μαθηματική (mathēmatikē; ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē), “knowledge,” is understood) ← μάθημα (mathēma) “what is learned; knowledge” ← μανθάνω (manthanō) “to learn, to come to know”; Latin *mathematica*; English *mathematics* takes its plural form after Greek τὰ μαθηματικά (ta mathēmatika). By its root mathematics is knowledge acquired by learning: it is found out, not invented. The root also carries the thesis of the branch: the laws that mathematics describes exist before the description—they are discovered.

## Definition

**Mathematics is the language for describing logical laws:** the laws exist, and they are discovered, not invented ([[Truth and Absolute Truth]]).

Systems as such are not subject to mathematical laws but have their own—they stand above mathematics, in pure logic. Mathematics itself is a system; “addition” and “subtraction” are its functions ([[Theory of Systems#Processes and Mathematics|“Processes and Mathematics”]]).

**A number is the result of the witness’s act of observation, information about a distinction made:** the witness has distinguished *A* and not-*A*, and this *A* is one *A*. Every number is a “unit of something,” and counting is the summing of steps of distinction: 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 ([[Theory of Systems#Processes and Mathematics|“Processes and Mathematics”]]; [[Excluded Middle]]). At least in the mathematics of this corpus everything consists of units: the unit—the unit of distinction—is both the smallest and the largest. Numbers not tied to what they are units of become illusory constructions without connection to reality.

## Processes and Infinity

**Logical realism is the position of the volume.** The formal systems of mathematics—set theory, type theory, the calculus of inductive constructions—are *technical implementations* of logic in particular languages. They work *because* logic already exists; they are not logic—they express it within particular frameworks.

**The beginning of the unfolding.** The first principle—something exists—is *discovered*, not postulated: whoever denies existence already presupposes an existing denier ([[Logic#The Ground|“The Ground”]]). The first principle does not enter the formal system—it is the condition of its possibility; the first thing formalized is **the act of distinction**: a positive side, a negative side, their mutual exclusion and exhaustiveness. From here the mathematics of the volume unfolds: number systems, analysis, algebra, geometry, number theory, categories, computation, functions, and probability—up to the final synthesis.

**Infinity—by the Law of Sufficient Reason.** Every construction must present a ground. There is a ground for an infinite *process*—one that unfolds and is finite at every step (the principle of Finite Actuality); infinity as a completed object has none. So too with the “set”: there is a ground for the operator’s decision to work with a list in a certain mode—“sets as a mode.” The natural number series is a process of generation, a real number a process of approximations ([[Theory of Systems#Processes and Mathematics|“Processes and Mathematics”]]).

Here the laws are called by their names; their statuses are on the page [[Logic#The Unity of the Laws|“Logic”]]: Sufficient Reason and Order are meta-laws, Identity and Non-Contradiction are primary, and Excluded Middle is derived from them. The numbers under which the same laws appear in the author’s articles are given there too, in the line below the table; the formal code uses the same numbers ([[Formalization]]).

Four cross-cutting motifs run through the whole volume: **positive ontology** (speaking of what is), **operational divisibility**, **sets as a mode**, **structural inevitability**.

## The Price of Axioms

**The current situation.** The modern foundations of mathematics are built around completed infinity. Cantor introduced actually infinite sets as ready objects and a scale of their sizes—the infinite cardinals ℵ₀, ℵ₁, … (1874–1897). Unrestricted comprehension—“for every property there is a set of all the things that have it”—accepted by the logicists (in Frege it follows from Basic Law V, 1893), led to Russell’s paradox in 1901, and after it the foundational crisis of the first third of the twentieth century. Russell arrived at the paradox while analyzing Cantor’s proof that there is no largest cardinality; Zermelo found the same paradox independently. One of the answers—alongside Russell’s theory of types (1908)—was **ZFC**: Zermelo’s axiomatization (1908), supplemented by the axiom of replacement (Fraenkel, Skolem) and the axiom of foundation (von Neumann)—a fence of axioms built over a universe of completed collections to keep the paradoxes outside.

The fence stands—but it has a price. From the axiom of choice follow the non-measurable Vitali set (1905), a well-ordering of ℝ for which the axioms of ZFC give no explicit formula, and the Banach–Tarski paradox (1924): a ball is cut into finitely many pieces—five suffice—and reassembled into two balls identical to the original. The full axiom of choice is not needed for this: a non-measurable set follows already from the Boolean prime ideal theorem, the paradox from the Hahn–Banach theorem; but without any choice at all they cannot be obtained. And the results of Gödel (1938) and Cohen (1963) showed that the continuum hypothesis is **independent** of the other axioms of ZFC, and the axiom of choice of the axioms of ZF: if ZF is consistent, each of them can be accepted or rejected without contradiction. Manageability was bought with a tower of postulates. Their price is counted by catalogs of consequences and equivalent forms of the axiom of choice (Rubin and Rubin, 1963; Jech, 1973; Howard and Rubin, 1998) and by reverse mathematics (Friedman, 1975; Simpson, 1999), which determines for each theorem which axioms are necessary to prove it.

**The move of the Theory of Systems—a change of category.** The answer here is not “ZFC without choice” but a change of the category itself. A **set** is a system given by a criterion; the criterion speaks of the level below—of what can occupy its roles ([[Theory of Systems#Levels and Hierarchy|“Levels and Hierarchy”]]). **Membership** is a relationship: an element from the level below occupies a role of the system. **Infinity** is a process: at every step only the finite is actual (Finite Actuality). **Order gives the places: the axiom of choice is not needed**—the status goes to the first that fits, the head of the list ([[Order#Order and the Assignment of Statuses|“Order”]]). In this light every strong axiom of ZFC turns out to be a gluing of a legitimate *rule* with a *completed object*: the Theory of Systems keeps the rule and reinterprets it as a theorem; the completed object is not built. The three classical paradoxes—Russell’s, Cantor’s, Burali-Forti’s—are one and the same move: the rule “all…” is taken as a ready thing; that is why, as correct systems, they **are not formed** ([[01 Set as a System Not an Object#Paradoxes as Category Errors|Part X, Chapter 1]]).

**Spelled out—over the nine axioms of ZFC.**

| ZFC axiom      | What it postulates                 | The move of the Theory of Systems                                          |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Extensionality | Equality of sets by composition    | Identity by the defining criterion (Intensional Identity): coincidence of elements does not give identity |
| Pairing        | From a, b there is {a, b}          | Trivial: pairs are primitive                                               |
| Union          | ⋃A exists                          | Trivial: finite unions                                                     |
| Infinity       | There is an inductive set (hence the completed ℕ) | ℕ is an inductive *rule*; completed infinity is not built: the rules contain no step that would give it |
| Separation     | {x ∈ A : φ} is a set               | Decidable separation; the impredicative one (Russell) is not formed        |
| Replacement    | The image of a set under a definable map is a set | Bounded process families                                    |
| Foundation     | Every nonempty set has an ∈-minimal element: no ∈-cycles and no infinite ∈-descents | The hierarchy of levels is irreflexive—a *theorem*, not a postulate |
| Choice         | Choice over an arbitrary family    | Order gives the places: the status goes to the first that fits (the head of the list); the completed choice graph is not built as an object |
| Power Set      | P(A) exists                        | The finite power 2ⁿ is an explicit list; the full P(ℕ) is not built as an object |

*Pairing and Union are trivial in the process language: the language itself gives them, without a separate postulate; Extensionality is replaced by Intensional Identity; Replacement is a reclassification; the remaining five correspond to named theorems ([[Formalization#Comparison with ZFC|“Comparison with ZFC”]]). “Trivial” here means “requires no axiom,” not “unimportant.”*

**What this yields—and what it costs.** There is one payment, named outright: the completed power object—the full P(ℕ), the continuum as a ready thing—is not built; the finite power 2ⁿ is written out explicitly. The base is two explicit assumptions of the instrument: the excluded middle in the mode of totality and witness extraction. The second is what logic calls indefinite description: choice over families also follows from it, so the price of each result is named separately ([[Formalization]]). Proved without assumptions are the countability of ℚ, the uncountability of [0,1], Cantor’s general theorem (there is no largest cardinality), and Kruskal’s theorem and Higman’s lemma in their simplest cases—families of trees and words that reduce to the order on ℕ (chains, forks, a one-letter alphabet); with the excluded middle—Dickson’s lemma for pairs; with the excluded middle and witness extraction—the Schröder–Bernstein theorem and the dichotomy “at most countable or contains a perfect subset” for closed sets of binary sequences; each of them is provable in ZF without choice as well. Metatheoretical questions about ZFC—the independence of the continuum hypothesis, the method of forcing—remain questions about ZFC: the universe of completed objects in which they are posed is not accepted here.

**The full analysis** is in [[10 Set Theory without the Axiom of Choice|Part X, “Set Theory without the Axiom of Choice”]] (six chapters), with a map of the prices of axioms and a twofold map of boundaries: “not invoked”—the question is open—and “not built”—a completed object; the formal evidence is on the page [[Formalization]].

## The Finitization Boundary

Computation is finitization: bringing something to a result by finite means. The part of the volume devoted to the boundary shows that it is arranged *uniformly*:

- **One diagonal.** The halting problem, Rice’s theorem, and Cantor’s uncountability are one and the same diagonal construction met in different guises (the common scheme is Lawvere’s fixed-point theorem; Rice’s theorem enters it through Kleene’s fixed-point theorem); the uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity is proved by a kindred argument—on the pattern of Berry’s paradox. The diagonal and the analysis of [[Paradox|paradoxes]] are one family: self-application at the boundary of levels.

- **One discriminant.** On one side is the decidable: finite memory, the lower levels of the Chomsky hierarchy (regular, context-free, and context-sensitive languages: membership of a word is decidable), type safety as the finitization of computation by a bounded supply of steps; on the other is the undecidable, fenced off by the diagonal: the top level of the same hierarchy, type 0—the recursively enumerable languages, where membership is only semi-decidable. A reduction atlas connects the problems into a single map.

- **Complexity is the price of finitization**: not only “is it possible by finite means,” but also at what price.

The finitization boundary coincides with the boundary of constructivity—this is a thread that runs through the whole volume: what is reachable by computation and what is built without classical leaps are outlined by a single boundary; in the decidable cases this is proved ([[Formalization]]). The continuation at the level of functions is function as process, the calculus of process functions, and the ladder: number, function, functional.

## Selected Results

*The machine support of each result and its price are on the page [[Formalization]].*

**Foundations without ZFC.**

- **Finite Actuality and Order—mathematics without the axioms of ZFC.** The least choice by Order is given in the model as the μ-operator of computability theory; over decidable structures, by the thesis of the volume, it carries, together with Finite Actuality, the classical body of these structures—without the axiom of choice and without completed infinity. *([[10 Set Theory without the Axiom of Choice|Part X]])*
- **Choice by Order without the axiom of choice.** The roles “first,” “least,” and “point of maximum” are given by Order; for decidable families over ℕ the whole ladder passes without the axiom of choice—finite choice, countable, dependent, and the path given by Kőnig’s lemma, when the test for an infinite branch is decidable. *([[10 Set Theory without the Axiom of Choice|Part X]])*

**Decidable boundaries.**

- **For the square root of a rational number the boundary is computable over ℚ.** A complete procedure (soundness and completeness) decides whether the number is the square of a rational number; irrationality is *derived* from its answer (for example, √(1/2) ∉ ℚ). *([[15 Computation and the Finitization Boundary|Part XV]])*
- **One diagonal.** The halting problem, Rice’s theorem, and Cantor’s uncountability are one and the same diagonal construction at the boundary of levels; the uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity is a kindred argument on the pattern of Berry’s paradox. *([[15 Computation and the Finitization Boundary|Part XV]])*
- **The uncountability of [0,1], the extreme value and intermediate value theorems—without the axioms of infinity and choice.** Uncountability and the intermediate value are proved without assumptions, the extreme value with the excluded middle; no enumeration exhausts the continuum, and analysis rests on processes over ℚ. *([[05 Topology and Analysis of Processes|Part V]])*

**Algebra, fields, Galois.**

- **The Galois correspondence for ℚ[√2, √3]:** four explicit automorphisms form the group V₄, √3 ∉ ℚ[√2] (hence the degree of the extension is 4); the algebraic closure of ℚ is an ascending process with no maximal rung. *([[11 Algebra Fields Galois|Part XI]])*

**Analysis and number theory.**

- **The zeta function is a process.** Partial sums are a Cauchy process, analytic continuation a new process; the Euler product is the analytic expression of the uniqueness of prime factorization; the Riemann hypothesis is the statement that every nontrivial zero is a fixed point of the reflection s ↦ 1 − s̄: a research line of the volume, not a solution. *([[13 Zeta Function and Number Theory|Part XIII]])*
- **Nonstandard analysis over processes.** Berkeley’s paradox is not formed: δ is a process, “o = 0” is a shadow; and a zero divisor in the ring of germs of sequences (modulo the Fréchet filter) is the trace of a missing ultrafilter. *([[19 Nonstandard Analysis over Processes|Part XIX]])*

**Discrete mathematics and combinatorics.**

- **Catalan numbers—three independent derivations.** The formula and the recurrence are obtained constructively in three ways—the cycle lemma (Dvoretzky and Motzkin, 1947), the reflection principle (Aebly and Mirimanoff, 1923; traditionally attributed to André), and decomposition by first return—and checked against one another by machine. *([[17 Discrete Mathematics Probability Information|Part XVII]])*

## Summary

1. **Mathematics is the language for describing logical laws:** the laws exist, and they are discovered, not invented; systems stand above mathematics, in pure logic, and mathematics itself is a system.
2. **A number is the result of an act of observation,** information about a distinction made; the natural series is a process of generation, a real number a process of approximation; infinity is a process, finite at every step.
3. **Logical realism:** formal systems are technical implementations of logic; they work because logic already exists.
4. **A set is a system given by a criterion;** the paradoxes of Russell, Cantor, and Burali-Forti are not formed.
5. **Order gives the places: the axiom of choice is not needed**—the status goes to the first that fits.
6. **A strong axiom of ZFC is a gluing of a rule with a completed object:** the rule is kept, the completed object is not built; the payment is the full P(ℕ) as a ready thing. The base of the instrument is the excluded middle in the mode of totality and witness extraction; the price of each result is named separately.
7. **The finitization boundary coincides with the boundary of constructivity—a line running through the whole volume, proved in the decidable cases:** the undecidable side is fenced off by one diagonal, the decidable side is drawn by one discriminant.
