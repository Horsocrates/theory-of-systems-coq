## The Fourth Path

### Where the Three Paths Traversed Looked

Part II has traversed three paths to the natural number series. The cardinal path (Chapter II.2) obtained the series from the repeatability of the act of distinction: a number is the length of a list of distinctions. The hierarchical path (Chapter II.3) obtained the same series from the Law of Order: a number is a level. The binary path (Chapter II.4) obtained it from the binarity of the act: a number is the count of binary choices. Three paths have been carried to the end, and all three arrived at one series.

Before opening the fourth and last path, it is worth noting what the three traversed paths had in common—because the fourth path will depart from this common ground.

All three paths looked at *distinctions*. The cardinal path counted them in a list; the hierarchical placed them on rungs; the binary looked closely inside a single act of distinction. What changed was *how* a path took distinction—as an element of a list, as a rung, as a binary choice—but the object of the gaze was each time distinction: a single act or a series of acts. What distinctions *form* once they have come together, none of the three paths asked.

### Where the Fourth Path Looks

The fourth path looks at the *system*.

Distinctions do not remain a scattering. Coming together, they form a *system*—a determinate whole that has its own order, its own places, its own arrangement. Part I showed this: everything determinate is an E/R/R system—a collection of elements bearing roles according to rules (Chapter I.4). The first three paths looked at distinctions as material; the fourth looks at what has *formed* from this material—at the system taken as a whole.

And it asks: how is a system arranged? Not “how many distinctions are in it” and not “on what rung are they,” but what it means *to be a system*. The answer, which will be obtained in § 5.2 from the laws $L5$ and $L4$, is this: to be a system is to be *an ordered collection of distinguishable places*. Every system has places, and these places are ordered: the first, the second, the third. From this arrangement—from the series of places of every system—the fourth path to the natural number series will unfold: number as a *position* in a system, as a place in a structure.

### How the Fourth Path Differs from the First Three

The difference of starting points is worth naming directly, for the independence of the fourth path rests on it.

The first three paths took a distinction—single or in a series—and derived number from it. The fourth path takes the *system* and derives number from its arrangement. The list of distinctions, the hierarchy of levels, the binarity of the act—none of these takes part in the fourth path as an initial premise; it starts from something else—from what it means to be a determinate whole. This is a fourth independent derivation: it does not rest on the first three and is not derived from them.

### What Lies Ahead in the Chapter

The chapter will traverse the structural path and then bring all four together. First we establish what a system is: that to be a system is to be an ordered collection of distinguishable places, and this will be established from the laws $L5$ and $L4$ (§ 5.2). Then we show that a place in a system is a number: a position (§ 5.3). Next, that the series of positions of every system is the natural number series (§ 5.4). After this the fourth path will also be traversed from the side of motion: a system does not only *have* places—it *counts*, it measures itself by number, and E/R/R as a process will show this (§ 5.5). Then all four paths will be compared: four paths, one series (§ 5.6). It will be shown that here, too, induction is not an axiom (§ 5.7). And the chapter—and with it the whole of Part II—will conclude with a synthesis: the four paths will be brought together, and the common thing owing to which they converge on one series will be named (§ 5.8).

*The structural path is the fourth and last path of ToS to the natural number series. The first three paths looked at distinctions—as a list, as rungs, as a binary choice. The fourth looks at the system that distinctions form, taken as a whole: its starting point is the arrangement of a system as a system. From this arrangement number will unfold as a position in a system.*

## Structure Is Ordered Distinguishable Places

### What Is to Be Established

The fourth path stands on one ground: on what a system is. If it is clear what it means to be a system, everything further will unfold from this; and if it is unclear, the path will not stand either. Therefore we must begin with the arrangement of a system—and establish it by derivation from the laws established in Part I rather than by description.

The statement to be grounded is this: *to be a system is to be an ordered collection of distinguishable places*. This statement has two components—“distinguishable places” and “ordered”—and each rests on its own law: the distinguishability of places is given by the law $L4$, the ordering by the law $L5$.

### The Law $L4$: Places Are Distinguishable

Let us state $L4$ as it is understood in Chapter I.3. The Law of Sufficient Reason is the statement that everything determinate has a ground for being precisely what it is. Chapter I.3 showed the special position of $L4$: it is a *meta-law*—it works over $L1$, $L2$, $L3$ as the very action of Logic in which the determinate becomes *grounded*. Applied to a system, $L4$ means: every element of a system has a ground for being in it—for having a *role*. An element without a role, without a ground for being there, is indistinguishable from what is not in the system (Chapter I.4).

Let us apply this to the places of a system. A place in a system is a place *for a role*: it is a place precisely because something on it bears a definite role. Precision is needed here about what exactly $L4$ gives. The law $L4$ requires every *occupied* place to be *role-grounded*—the element standing on it must have a ground-role; an element without a role, without a ground for being there, is indistinguishable from what is not in the system (Chapter I.4). This is what $L4$ *proves*: every occupied place has a role grounding. What $L4$ does *not* give by itself is that *different* places necessarily have *different* roles: two places can bear similar or even one and the same role. Therefore the distinguishability of places in the strict sense is not provided by $L4$ alone. $L4$ gives that every occupied place is role-grounded—it stands in the system for a reason; the *distinguishability* of places as different ones is supplied by the link of $L4$ with $L5$: even if two positions bear similar roles, they are distinguishable as *different positions of the order*. The law $L4$, by requiring a role ground for every occupied place, keeps the places of a system from being an indifferent homogeneous mass; but that each place is *this* place and not another is finally provided by $L5$—the order to which the chapter turns next.

### The Law $L5$: Places Are Ordered

Let us state $L5$ in its formulation from Chapter I.3. The Law of Order is the statement that every determinacy carries order and hierarchy; its vertical side is the hierarchy of levels, its horizontal side is sequence. Applied to a system, $L5$ means: the places of a system are *ordered*—they stand in the relation “before—after,” “above—below”—rather than being heaped together as equals.

Let us apply this to the places of a system. The places that $L4$ made distinguishable, $L5$ arranges into an *order*: among them there is a first, there is the one following it, there is the one following that. The places of a system are a *series*—an ordered sequence of them—not a scattering of distinguishable points. The law $L5$, by requiring order of every determinacy, thereby orders the places of a system.

### Structure *Is* Ordered Distinguishable Places

Let us put the two conclusions together. By $L4$, every occupied place of a system is role-grounded—the element standing on it has a ground-role. By $L5$, the places are ordered—they stand in a series, and are therefore distinguishable as different positions of the order. Role-grounded and ordered places are precisely what a system consists of.

This must be said precisely, otherwise the fourth path will be misunderstood. One might think that first there is a system—some ready whole—and then the laws $L4$ and $L5$ *bring* distinguishability and order *into* it, as furnishings are brought into a finished house. This is not so. $L4$ and $L5$ *constitute* the system; they are not *added* to it. There is no system *before* order that would then be ordered: to be an ordered collection of distinguishable places is *to be* a system, and “to be a system” means nothing beyond this. Remove the order and the distinguishability of places, and there will remain no system to which they were previously applied; nothing will remain, for the system *is* them.

Therefore it is right to say “a system *is* ordered distinguishable places,” not “a system *has* ordered distinguishable places.” Structure is the very order of distinguishable places, not a container of order. This distinction is essential for the whole path: the fourth path derives number from what a system *is*, not from something that the system *adds* to itself.

*To be a system is to be an ordered collection of distinguishable places. The role grounding of every occupied place is given by the law $L4$: the element standing on a place has a ground-role. The ordering—and thereby the distinguishability of places as different positions of the order—is given by the law $L5$: the places stand in a series. And these laws are not brought into a ready system—they constitute it: structure does not *have* ordered distinguishable places, it *is* them. This is the ground of the fourth path.*

### What Follows from This

The consequence of the arrangement of a system lies close at hand, and the rest of the chapter will be occupied with it. If a system is an ordered collection of distinguishable places, then every system has a *series of places*: the first place, the second, the third. And this series is neither brought in nor chosen: it is the very form of structuredness, that without which a system is not a system.

It remains to show that a place in this series is a natural number—that the series of places of every system is the natural number series. To this—through the concept of position—the chapter turns in the next section.

## Position as Number

### A Place in the Series Is a Position

Every system has a series of places: the places are distinguishable and ordered (§ 5.2). This is enough to take the main step of the fourth path—to show that a place in this series is a natural number.

Let us call a place in the ordered series of places a *position*. A position is neither the place itself nor what stands on the place; a position is *which in order* this place is in the series. The first place occupies the first position, the place after it the second, the next one the third. A position answers the question “at which place in order?”—and the answer is the indication of the place in the sequence.

### A Position Is a Natural Number

A position is a natural number. The first position is one, the second is two, the third is three. The series of positions of every system—the first, the second, the third, and so on—is exactly the natural number series.

ToS fixes this in the arrangement of a structured system. A system taken from the side of its places is a *structured system*—a system in which each place is assigned what stands on it. ToS sets this assignment as a function of *position*: a position is assigned the element occupying it.[^1] And the type of position in this assignment is `nat`—the type of natural numbers. A position in a system *is* a natural number.

### The Circle Removed

Here the fourth path passes a place that requires special clarity. One could object as follows. The path promised to derive the natural number—and arrived at the claim that a position “is `nat`,” a natural number. Is this not a derivation of number from number? Has it not simply been said that a natural number is a natural number—and has the whole path not turned out to be a circle?

The objection is answered, and seeing exactly how is important for the whole path.

There would be a circle if `nat` stood at the *beginning* of the path—if the fourth path *started* from the claim that a system has positions numbered by natural numbers. Then the natural number series would be a premise, and its “derivation” would be empty. But the path does not start from this. It starts from the laws $L4$ and $L5$—from the fact that every system is an ordered collection of distinguishable places (§ 5.2). This is the premise of the path, and there are no natural numbers in it: neither $L4$ nor $L5$ speaks of numbers. From this premise the path *derives*: since the places are distinguishable and ordered, the system has a series of places; since there is a series of places, there is also a position—which in order; and the series of positions is the natural number series. The natural number stands at the *end* of the derivation, not at the beginning.

That in the ToS repository the type `Position` is defined directly as `nat` is not a premise of the path, but neither is it a machine derivation of the natural number series. Precision is needed here. In Rocq the entry `Position := nat` of course *uses* the already available type `nat`—a type given by the language. Therefore the machine formalization neither *creates* the natural number series anew nor *derives* it from $L4$ and $L5$ as a theorem. It *fixes* the reading chosen by ToS: the positionality of a system has the same form as the natural number series and can therefore be written through `nat`. Two layers must be kept apart here. The ontological derivation—from $L4$ and $L5$, which contain no numbers—explains *why* such an entry is meaningful: the series of positions is, by its arrangement, the natural number series. The technical entry `Position := nat` *makes use* of this: it takes the ready `nat` and thereby makes position convenient for further proofs. To read `Position := nat` as a *premise* of the path would be to confuse a convenient entry with a ground; to read it as a *machine derivation* of `nat` from the system would be to attribute to the code more than it contains. The right reading lies in between: the derivation grounds, the technical entry fixes, and the formal entry comes *after* the derivation, not *instead of* it.

*A position—a place in the ordered series of places of a system—is a natural number. This is not a circle: the path starts from the laws $L4$ and $L5$, which contain no numbers, and *derives* the series of positions as the natural number series. That the formalization sets the type of position as `nat` is neither a premise of the derivation nor a machine derivation of `nat` from the system, but a technical entry that makes use of the ready `nat`; the derivation explains why such an entry is meaningful.*

### What the Position Has Given

The fourth path has obtained its number. On the cardinal path a number was the length of a list, on the hierarchical a level, on the binary the count of bits; on the structural path a number is a *position*—a place in the ordered series of places of a system.

And it is worth noting what makes this number distinctive among the four. The first three paths counted something *outside* a ready system: acts of distinction, the rungs of their hierarchy, binary choices. The fourth path found number *inside* the system—as its own arrangement: every system already carries a series of positions within itself, for it is ordered distinguishable places. On the structural path number does not come to the system from outside—it is the very form in which a system is a system.

It remains to show that the series of positions is the whole natural number series—with a beginning, with an operation of succession, without an end. This is the subject of the next section.

## The Series of Positions Is the Natural Number Series

### From Position to Series

A position is a natural number (§ 5.3). This was established for a single position: a given place in the series corresponds to a given number. Now we must show that the structural path gives *the whole series*, not separate numbers—that the series of positions of every system is the natural number series in full, with a beginning, with an operation of succession, without an end.

The natural number series is arranged from two things: there is an initial element, and there is an operation of succession that generates the next number from each number (Chapter II.2, § 2.5; Chapter II.3, § 3.4). Let us show that the series of positions carries exactly this arrangement.

### The First Position Is the Beginning

The series of positions has a beginning—the *first* position. More precisely: every *nonempty* system whose places form an ordered series has a first place—and the first position corresponds to it.

This follows from the arrangement of the system. The places of a nonempty system are ordered (§ 5.2): they stand in the relation “before—after.” And since a nonempty series of places is ordered, among its places there is a *first*—the one before which there are no places in the system. Were the series of places without a first place, every place would be preceded by another, and another, without limit—but then the system would have no beginning either, nothing with which it begins as an ordered whole. An ordered nonempty collection of places has a first place; the first position corresponds to the first place.

A caveat about the formal side is needed here. In the current Rocq code the type `Position` is set as `nat`, and the field `ss_assignment` is a *partial* function: it may return `None` for every position (an empty system) or be occupied only on a sparse set of positions. Therefore “the first position” does not follow from `StructuredSystem` automatically—it requires that the system be nonempty and that its occupied positions form an ordered initial series. The present chapter speaks precisely of such a—*normalized*—representation of a system: one in which the occupied places go in a row, the first, the second, the third. The more general partial function `Position -> option Element` of the current code does not impose normalization; it can be added as a separate condition.[^2]

And the first position is *one*, not zero. Two layers must be separated here—exactly as in Chapter II.3 when `level_depth` and `level_to_nat` were separated. Technically, since `Position` is set as `nat` in the Rocq code, *zero-based* indexing is possible: the first technical index is $0$. This is an index numbering, convenient for the formal record. The ToS positional *number* that interests the present chapter is *shifted*: the number $1$ corresponds to the first existing position, $2$ to the second, and so on. The connection of the two numberings is simple: the ToS number of a position is the technical index plus one—which can be written by the function `pos_number p := S p` (then `pos_number 0 = 1`, `pos_number 1 = 2`).[^3] Ontologically, however, the first position is one because the first place of a system is an *existing* place, an occupied one: something on it bears a role. A “zeroth position” in the ToS sense would mean a place that does not exist—the absence of a place; but the first place of a system is an existing place, and therefore one corresponds to it. Here the structural path converges with the three traversed: just as the first act of distinction, the base level, and the first bit are one, not zero (§ 1.2; § 3.3.5; § 4.4.4), so the first position of a system is one. The primacy of one is confirmed from a fourth and last side.

### The Next Position Is the Operation of Succession

The series of positions has an operation of succession—the passage to the *next* position.

This too follows from the arrangement of the system, but it requires precision regarding $P4$. The places are ordered: after every place except the *last* there stands a next place—the one that comes after it in the order “before—after.” The passage from a place to the one following it is the passage from a position to the next position: from the first to the second, from the second to the third. And this passage increases the number of the position by exactly one—the next position is the position greater by one.

Three things must be distinguished here, otherwise what has been said would come into contradiction with the principle $P4$ (Finite Actuality, Chapter II.2, § 2.7). In a *concrete finite* system the passage to the next position is defined *up to the last* actual position: a finite series of places has a last place, and after it there is no next place in this system. In the *open scheme* of extensible systems the next position can always be *conceived*—the series of places can be continued by adding another place. And in the natural number series *as a form*, every number has a next one. Strictly speaking, the operation of succession belongs to the *general form* of the positional series—the form that a concrete finite system actualizes only up to its last place—rather than to each completed finite system as an actual passage. This is the same potential understanding of the series as on the three previous paths: the series of positions is unbounded *potentially*, as the continuability of the scheme, not as a completed infinite system.

The passage to the next position, then, is the operation of succession. What on the cardinal path was the appending of a distinction to a list, on the hierarchical path the stacking of a level, and on the binary path the adding of a bit, is on the structural path *the passage to the next place of the positional series*. Four operations, and all four are one operation of succession `S`, taken in four forms. On the structural path `S` is the passage to the next position—an operation that belongs to the form of the positional series.

### The Series of Positions Is the Natural Number Series

Let us put both components together. The series of positions has a beginning—the first position, to which one corresponds. The series of positions has an operation of succession—the passage to the next position, which increases the number by one. An initial element plus an operation of succession is precisely the arrangement of the natural number series (§ 5.4.1). The same precision is needed here as above. The *form* of the series of positions is the natural number series: positionality as such—a beginning and the passage to the next—has exactly the arrangement of $ℕ$. But a *concrete* system actualizes not this whole series but only a fragment of it: the occupied places of a finite system form a finite initial segment $1, 2, …, n$, and in the general case the partial function `ss_assignment` may also set another, sparse assignment. Therefore the exact formula is this: the *positional form* of a system is the natural number series—the type of positions has the arrangement of $ℕ$; a concrete system, by contrast, actualizes a finite initial fragment of this form or some other partial assignment of positions. Not “the series of positions of every system is the whole of $ℕ$,” but “the form of positionality is $ℕ$, and every system actualizes a fragment of it.”

This can also be presented by generation—the generation of the *form* itself. We begin with the first position—one corresponds to it. We pass to the next position—two. We pass on again—three. Each passage to the next place gives the next position, the next natural number; the positional form, unfolded by the passage from place to place, has the arrangement of the whole natural number series:

$$
\text{pos.}_1 \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{next}\;}\;
\text{pos.}_2 \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{next}\;}\;
\text{pos.}_3 \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{next}\;}\;
\cdots
$$

$$
1 \;\xrightarrow{\;+1\;}\; 2 \;\xrightarrow{\;+1\;}\;
3 \;\xrightarrow{\;+1\;}\; \cdots
$$

Two lines, one motion. The unfolding of the positional form and counting along the natural number series are one and the same generation.

ToS fixes the ordering of positions formally. The law $L5$ holds in a structured system: one and the same element stands on a single position, and the positions are arranged in order.[^4] The series of positions is a series precisely because it carries order; and an ordered form with a beginning and an operation of succession is the natural number series.

*The positional form of every system is the natural number series. It has a beginning—the first position, to which one corresponds; it has an operation of succession—the passage to the next place, which increases the number by one. A concrete system actualizes a fragment of this form. This is the fourth manifestation of one structure of the natural number series—an initial element and an operation of succession.*

### The Fourth Manifestation of One Structure

Here the structural path arrives at the same place as the three previous paths.

The natural number series is arranged from an initial element and an operation of succession. All four paths gave exactly this arrangement, differing only in what the initial element and the operation of succession turned out to be on each. On the cardinal path: the first distinction in the list and the appending of a distinction. On the hierarchical: the base level and stacking. On the binary: the first bit and the adding of a bit. On the structural: the first position and the passage to the next place. Four paths—four manifestations of one structure.

The structural path builds neither a *different* series nor a series arranged *differently*. It shows the same structure of the natural number series—an initial element plus an operation of succession—in its fourth and last embodiment. In the E/R/R analysis (§ 5.8.2) this is one role structure of number on a fourth and last bearer.

The natural number series has been generated a fourth time—by the unfolding of the series of positions. It remains to traverse the structural path from the side of motion as well—to see the system that counts—and then to compare all four paths. The chapter turns to the first of these in the next section.

## The System That Counts Itself

### Two Sides of the Structural Path

So far the structural path has taken the system *at rest*: as a ready whole that has ordered distinguishable places, and it has counted these places—the positions (§ 5.2–5.4). This is one side of the path, the static one. But an E/R/R system has another side as well. Part I showed that E/R/R is a *process*, not a frozen triple: the rules determine the roles, and the roles are distributed among the elements (Chapter I.4). A system can be taken in action as well as at rest.

Taken in action, the system reveals one more relation to number, and it deserves to be named separately. A system does not only *have* places that can be counted from outside. A system *counts by itself*: its own action is, among other things, counting. Number turns out to be not only *what* is counted in a system but also *that by which* the system measures itself.

### The E/R/R Process Produces a Measure

Let us take the E/R/R system as a process. ToS describes its direction strictly: *rules—roles—elements*. First there are the rules of the system; the rules determine the *roles*—the places in the system that are to be occupied, each with a requirement that the occupant must meet; and only then are *elements* selected for the roles—those that satisfy the requirement of the role. What is primary is the role, not the element: an element enters the system insofar as there is a role for which it fits, not on its own account. This is the work of the law $L4$: an element has a *ground* for being in the system, and this ground is its correspondence to the requirement of the role.[^5]

Number takes part in this selection at every step. To determine whether an element meets the requirement of a role, the system *measures* its correspondence—assigns to it a measure of how far it satisfies the requirement. This measure is a number: to an element corresponds its *weight*, expressed by a natural number, and the weight is composed of partial evaluations of correspondence, which are numerical too. It is by weight that it is decided whether the element takes the role or not. And where a role admits many and one must be chosen—the first among those that fit—the resolution also proceeds by number: by the order of the candidate positions.[^6] Without number this selection could not proceed: the system could neither measure an element’s correspondence to a requirement, nor compare two correspondences, nor choose one of those that fit.

### Number as the Self-Measure of the System

What has been said can be reduced to one thing. An E/R/R system, in acting, *measures*—and the measure it uses is a natural number. Number is the *self-measure* of the system: that by which the system measures the correspondence of an element to the requirement of a role. To be a system in action—to select for roles the elements that meet their requirements—the system is compelled to count.

And it is worth seeing in what sense the static and the dynamic sides converge in *one* natural number series—and in what sense they do not. In § 5.3–5.4 number was a *place* in the system—a position in the series of places; here number is a *measure of correspondence*, the weight by which an element comes to occupy a place. Position and weight are *not* literally one and the same value: position answers the question “*where*?”—on which place of the series the element stands; weight answers the question “*how far does it correspond*?”—what the measure of the element’s right to take the role is. These are two different system functions, and formally they are two different uses of `nat`: `Position` in one file, `ent_weight` in another. The unity of position and weight lies in the fact that both functions of the system use *one and the same numerical form*—the natural number series—not in their being one value. One syntax of the natural number—two system roles: place and measure. The static side (the system has a series of place-roles) and the dynamic side (the system measures correspondence in order to fill these places) converge in one *form* of number, not in one *number*—in the same natural number series, read once as the order of places and once as the measure of correspondence.

*An E/R/R system taken in action does not only have places that can be counted—it counts by itself: in selecting elements for roles, it measures the correspondence of each element to the requirement of the role, and this measure is a natural number. Number is the self-measure of the system. Position and weight are two system functions (place and measure), not one value; they are united in using one numerical form—one natural number series.*

### What the Structural Path Needs the Dynamic Side For

The dynamic side adds something essential to the structural path, and it is worth naming exactly what.

The static side (§ 5.2–5.4) showed that every system *has* a series of positions. This could leave the impression that number is something external for a system: the series of places exists, but the system itself seems indifferent to it, and someone from outside counts the places. The dynamic side removes this impression. The system is not indifferent to number: it *counts by itself*, number is its own action. The natural number series does not lie beside the system as something that could be applied to it—it works *inside* the system as that by which the system arranges itself.

With this the structural path completes Part II in substance. The first three paths showed that the natural number series can be *obtained* from distinction—by different roads. The fourth path showed more: that the natural number series is not only obtainable but also *works*—that every system carries it within itself and acts by it. Number has turned out to be the form in which a system is and acts, not a result that the system produces and leaves behind.

The structural path has been traversed—both from the side of rest and from the side of motion. It remains to compare all four paths and to bring them together. The chapter turns to the comparison in the next section.

## Comparison with the Three Paths Traversed

### How the Four Paths Differ

Four paths to the natural number series have been traversed: the cardinal in Chapter II.2, the hierarchical in Chapter II.3, the binary in Chapter II.4, the structural in § 5.1–5.5. The present section sets them side by side—in order to see what coincided and what this means, without choosing the best or checking one by another. We should begin with how the four paths differ.

They differ in their starting point—in what they look at, and by what operation they generate the series.

The cardinal path looks at distinctions *as a list* and asks how many there are. A number is the length of the list; the operation of succession is the appending of a distinction to the list.

The hierarchical path looks at distinctions *as rungs* and asks how high the hierarchy is. A number is a level; the operation of succession is the stacking of a level over a level.

The binary path looks *inside* a single act of distinction and asks how it is arranged. A number is the count of binary choices; the operation of succession is the adding of a bit to the record.

The structural path looks at the *system* that distinctions form and asks how this whole is arranged. A number is a position in the system; the operation of succession is the passage to the next place.

The difference of starting points is essential—but it must be named precisely. The four paths are not independent in the sense of not using the common frame of ToS: all four work within the same laws $L1$–$L5$ and the principle $P4$, and all four rest on the primacy of one (Chapter II.1) and on the E/R/R ontology of Part I. Moreover, the structural path rests directly on $L4$ and $L5$ and on positions as `nat`—that is, it also uses the motif of order, which it shares with the hierarchical path. The independence of the four paths is an independence of the *semantic source of number*: each path takes number from its own source—the cardinal from the length of a list of distinctions, the hierarchical from the depth of a level, the binary from the length of a binary record, the structural from a position in a system. A list, a hierarchy, the binarity of an act, the arrangement of a system are four different things, and no path takes *its source* of number from another. Four derivations independent in source and united by the frame; precisely this makes their coincidence significant.

### Where the Four Paths Coincided

For all the difference of starting points, the result of the four paths is *one*, and the coincidence is exact—in the very arrangement of the series.

Each path gave a series with a beginning—one—and an operation of succession generating the next number from each number. The cardinal: one is the first distinction, succession is the appending of a distinction. The hierarchical: one is the base level, succession is stacking. The binary: one is the first bit, succession is the adding of a bit. The structural: one is the first position, succession is the passage to the next place. The beginnings of all four paths are one and the same number one; the operations of succession of all four are one and the same operation `S`, taken in four forms. And the infinity in all four cases is one—potential, by $P4$.

The four paths diverge in their starting point and in what the operation of succession appears as—and converge completely in the result. Four different derivations led to an *identical* series: to one and the same $1, 2, 3, …$.

### Four Paths, One Series

The coincidence of four independent paths brings to completion what Part II traverses them for.

Chapters II.3 and II.4 already noted that the coincidence of independent paths is an argument for the structural inevitability of the natural number series—that $ℕ$ was discovered, not invented; and with each new path the argument grew stronger. The fourth path strengthens it for the last time. Were there two or three paths, one could still suppose—though with a stretch—some hidden kinship of the derivations, a common undeclared premise, owing to which the coincidence of their result is not so surprising. Four paths, laid from four different places—from the list, from the hierarchy, from the binarity of the act, from the arrangement of the system—exhaust such a supposition. Four roads lead to one and the same series $ℕ$, and there is no point from which all four would have started as from a common source: they diverge at the very beginning and converge only in the result. When four independent roads lead to one place, this place is that to which a road guided by the derivation of ToS is *compelled* to come, wherever it began—not a crossroads of chance trails.

*Four independent paths—the cardinal, the hierarchical, the binary, and the structural—give an identical natural number series: the same number one at the beginning, one operation of succession in four forms, one potential infinity by $P4$. The coincidence of four paths brings the argument for the structural inevitability of $ℕ$ to completion: four roads from four different places converge at one point, and this point is that to which the derivation of ToS is compelled to come.*

### The Last Question of Part II

The comparison of the four paths brings Part II to its last question.

Four paths give one series. Chapter II.4 (§ 4.6.4) already named this question as groundwork: *why* do such different derivations arrive at one series? What makes the list of distinctions, the hierarchy of levels, the binary record, and the series of positions *four forms of one thing*? To say that all four give the structure “an initial element plus an operation of succession” does not yet explain why each of the paths arrives at this structure—it is a description of the coincidence, not its cause.

Now that all four paths have been traversed, the question can and must be resolved. Behind the coincidence stands something common—that owing to which four independent roads converge at one point. To name this common thing and thereby close Part II is the task of the synthesis, to which the chapter turns in the concluding section. First, however, we must show that on the structural path, too, induction is not an axiom: this completes the traversal of the fourth path, and the chapter turns to it before the synthesis.

## Induction on Positions

### Induction on the Fourth Path as Well

The three paths traversed showed that induction in ToS is not an axiom. The cardinal path grounded it in the repeatability of the act: the base is the first act, the step is one more act (Chapter II.2, § 2.6). The hierarchical grounded it in the arrangement of the hierarchy: the base is the ground, the step is stacking (Chapter II.3, § 3.7). The binary grounded it in the growth of the binary record: the base is the first bit, the step is the adding of a bit (Chapter II.4, § 4.7). The structural path comes to the series by a fourth road—through the series of positions of a system—and induction must be grounded here anew, according to the arrangement of this path.

### Induction on Positions

The principle of induction for the natural number series was stated in Chapter II.2 (§ 2.6.2): for a property to hold for every number, it suffices that it hold for the first number (the base) and that its holding for $n$ imply its holding for $n + 1$ (the step).

Since on the structural path a number is a position in a system (§ 5.3), the same principle can be read on positions. *Induction on positions:* for a property to hold for every position of a system, two things suffice—that it hold for the first position (the base) and that its holding for a position imply its holding for the position following it (the step). The base and the step together entail that the property holds for every position.

### The Base Is the First Position, the Step Is the Passage to the Next

Why induction on positions is legitimate can be seen from how the series of positions is arranged. The answer is of the same nature as on the three previous paths; only the components are different.

The series of positions is composed of two things, and it has no others (§ 5.4). The first: there is a *first* position—the one before which there are no places in the system. The second: every position other than the first is a position *following* some position—obtained by passing to the next place. Let us compare this with induction. The base of induction: the property holds for the first position; the first component of the series: there is a first position. The step of induction: holding for a position implies holding for the next; the second component: every position is obtained by passing to the next. *The base of induction corresponds to the first position; the step of induction corresponds to the passage to the next position.*

Hence its legitimacy. Every position of a system *either* is the first *or* is obtained from the first by finitely many passages to the next (§ 5.4). A property that holds for the first position (the base) and is preserved by the passage to the next (the step) then holds along every finite chain of passages—that is, for every position. Induction on positions holds sway over all positions because every position consists of the first position and the passages to the next, not by some special power of the principle.

*Induction on positions is not an axiom. Its base corresponds to the first position of a system; its step, to the passage to the next position. Induction is a verbal record of how the series of positions is composed—of the first position and passages; and it holds sway over all positions because every position consists of them.*

It is worth clarifying the formal side. Since in the current code `Position := nat`, induction on positions *technically coincides* with standard induction on `nat`: it is ordinary `nat`-induction applied to the type of positions, not a new principle machine-derived from `StructuredSystem`. The ontological sense of the section lies in the fact that ToS *explains* why the positional structure of a system has precisely this inductive form—the first position and the passage to the next—not in Rocq generating a *new* induction principle here. The formal layer makes use of the ready `nat`-induction; the ontological layer grounds its applicability to positions.

A caveat about numbering is also needed—the same as in § 5.4.2. At the *technical* level positions are indexed from zero (`Position := nat`, the first index is $0$), and the standard position $0$ is the base of `nat`-induction. At the *ToS* level the positional number is shifted: `pos_number p = S p`, and the first position carries the number $1$. Induction on the technical index (with base $0$) and induction on the positive positional number (with base $1$) express *one and the same* structure—a base plus the passage to the next; the only difference is the number from which counting starts. When the present section says “the base is the first position,” the ToS number is meant; technical `nat`-induction gives exactly the same result, starting the count from zero.

### The Same Induction, a Fourth Grounding

Induction on positions and induction on numbers are one principle. Since the series of positions is the natural number series (§ 5.4), induction on positions and induction on numbers cannot be two principles: the principle of induction is a principle *for the natural number series*, and the series is one.

But the grounding of induction on the four paths is fourfold—and that is the whole point. The cardinal path derived induction from the repeatability of the act; the hierarchical, from the arrangement of the hierarchy; the binary, from the growth of the binary record; the structural, from the arrangement of the series of positions. One principle of induction receives four independent groundings on the four paths. And this is the same as what happened with the natural number series itself (§ 5.6): one principle, not four; four independent paths to one principle, not four retellings of one argument. The coincidence testifies here just as it testified for the series: induction is not an arbitrarily accepted axiom—it is that to which ToS comes in tracing the arrangement of the series, by whatever road one approaches this arrangement.

### Infinity

About infinity on the structural path we need speak only briefly—and it will be, for the fourth time, the same as what has been said three times.

The *positional form* of a system is potentially infinite: it can be continued—for every place of the positional series a next place can be conceived. But this is the potential infinity of the *form*, not a completed collection of all positions at once, and not an infinite actual system. By the principle $P4$—the principle of Finite Actuality (Chapter II.2, § 2.7)—an *actual* system at a given stage has only a finite number of occupied positions, a finite fragment of the positional series. The form has no *last* position—but a concrete system is finite, and in it there is a last occupied position. The openness of the series of positions is a property of the form, not a property of every actual system; the form can be continued, while a concrete system is finite at every stage.

And here, as on the three previous paths, induction and potential infinity agree. Induction on positions holds sway over *every* position precisely because the series of positions is not completed: induction does not survey all positions at once—there are potentially infinitely many of them—but gives the property for the first and carries it over by the passage to the next, thereby covering any position that the series will reach. The structural path adds nothing new to the infinity of the natural number series; it confirms from a fourth and last side that the series is infinite potentially, by $P4$.

The structural path has been traversed completely: the natural number series has been obtained as the series of positions of a system, gone through both at rest and in motion, and compared with the three previous paths, and induction on it has been grounded. It remains to bring all four paths together and to sum up Part II. This is the subject of the concluding section.

## Synthesis and Summary of Part II

### What the Chapter Has Covered

The chapter has traversed the fourth and last path of ToS to the natural number series—the structural path.

The path began with a new view of distinction. The first three paths looked at distinctions—as a list, as rungs, as a binary choice; the fourth turned to what distinctions *form* once they have come together—to the system taken as a whole (§ 5.1). Then it was established what a system is: by the law $L4$ the places of a system are distinguishable—each has its own role; by the law $L5$ the places are ordered—they stand in a series; and these laws are not brought into a ready system but constitute it, so that structure does not *have* ordered distinguishable places but *is* them (§ 5.2). Hence the core of the path: a place in the ordered series of places is a position, and a position is a natural number; and this is not a circle, since the path starts from the laws $L4$ and $L5$, which contain no numbers, while
`Position := nat` is a technical entry that makes use of the ready
`nat`—neither a premise of the derivation nor a machine derivation of $ℕ$ from the system (§ 5.3). The series of positions was traversed as the natural number series: the first position is one, the passage to the next place is the operation of succession—the fourth manifestation of one structure (§ 5.4). Then the structural path was also traversed from the side of motion: an E/R/R system does not only have places—in acting, it measures the correspondence of elements to the requirements of roles, and this measure is a number; number is the self-measure of the system (§ 5.5). Finally, the four paths were compared: different in their starting point, they give an identical series (§ 5.6), and induction on the structural path proved to be a record of the arrangement of the series of positions, not an axiom (§ 5.7).

Thus the structural path has been traversed: a natural number is a position in a system, and the whole natural number series is the series of positions that every system carries within itself by the laws $L4$ and $L5$.

### E/R/R Analysis: The Number-Position as a System

Of the four paths, the structural one is the closest to E/R/R (Part I): a position *is* a role—a place in a system—and the chapter has traversed both of its sides: the static (§ 5.2–5.4: places are distinguishable and ordered, a position is a number) and the dynamic (§ 5.5: an E/R/R system, in acting, measures correspondence; number is the self-measure). The headers of the supporting files give the labeling: *elements—$L1$, roles—$L4$, rules—$L5$* (`Roles.v`), and the E/R/R process runs in the direction Rules $\to$ Roles $\to$ Elements (`ERRProcess.v`, § 5.5).[^7] A caveat on status: the “system” here is a substantive interpretation (`StructuredSystem` with a partial assignment of places); `ss_L5_valid` fixes only one side of uniqueness, the first position requires nonemptiness (§ 5.3–5.4), and `process_entity` currently assigns only `Candidate`/`Invalid` (§ 5.5).

*Rules* (the laws $L5$, $L4$).
The constitution of the system is two laws: $L5$ orders the places (they stand in a series), $L4$ grounds the roles (an occupied place has a ground—the role that its occupant meets). Their linkage gives the series: the first position and the passage to the next—beginning and succession. To the statics, dynamics is added (§ 5.5): E/R/R is a *process*—the rules check (the agreement gate), roles are assigned according to the check (status), and elements are selected for the roles (by weight). $L1$–$L5$ all act; $L4$ and $L5$ are the distinctive ones.

*Roles—the significance of positions* (the law $L4$). The role here is literally a position: the place “where?” in the ordered series, and number is this position-role. The dynamic side gives a second role layer—*weight*: the measure “how far does it correspond?” Position and weight are two system functions, not one value, but one numerical form (§ 5.5.3). Number acts in both roles: both as place and as measure.

*Elements—the bearers* (the law $L1$ and the principle $P4$). The elements are the occupants of places (the assignment `ss_assignment` is partial: a place may stay empty). By $L1$, each is identical to itself. By $P4$, a concrete system actualizes a *fragment* of the positional form—a finite number of occupied places—whereas the form of the series of positions itself is potentially infinite (§ 5.7).

Let us gather the analysis into a table.

| Component | What it determines | E/R/R |
|---|---|---|
| $L5$ (the order of places) + $L4$ (the groundedness of a role) | the constitution of the system | Rule |
| the E/R/R process: gate $\to$ weight $\to$ status | the dynamics of selection | Rule |
| position: the place “where?”; number = position | the role of a place | Role |
| weight: the measure “how far does it correspond?” | role-self-measure | Role |
| occupied places (`ss_assignment`, partial) | bearers (a fragment, $P4$) | Element |
| laws $L1$–$L5$ (here $L4$, $L5$) | universal layer | Rule (univ.) |

*Well-formedness.* The labeling is unambiguous: the occupants of places are elements, position and weight are roles, $L4$/$L5$ and the E/R/R process are rules. $P1$ is observed: places are positions *inside* the system, the order is strict (no place precedes itself), and the system is not a position within itself. And here the analysis turns into a *diagnosis*—a concluding one at that. The fourth path makes *explicit* what the three previous paths showed implicitly: number is always a *role*—a count, a level, a number of bits, a position—not a self-standing element-object hanging outside every system. To take number for such an object is the same confusion of categories as taking a set or a point for a self-standing object (on this, Part I). Number *is a position in a structure*, a role; the structural path names this directly.

What the analysis yields. It leads to the synthesis of Part II (§ 5.8.3): since number is a role set by beginning and succession, the four paths are *one* role structure (one syntax) on four bearers (four semantics, § 5.8.4). The structural path is the E/R/R reading of number: it takes number directly as a position-role and shows that by the same numerical form the system *counts* itself as well (§ 5.5). In this way E/R/R links all four paths and closes Part II; the further extensions (integers, rationals) Part III will build as new systems of roles over the positions of number.

### Synthesis: Why Four Paths Give One Series

Four paths have been traversed. Each went from its own place and by its own road—and all four arrived at one and the same natural number series. Part II left a question open: *why*? What makes the list of distinctions, the hierarchy of levels, the binary record, and the series of positions four forms of one thing? Now that all the paths have been traversed, this question can be answered.

The answer is this: four paths give one series because all four start from *one thing*—the act of distinction—and the natural number series is the *form* of this act.

Let us trace this. The cardinal path took the act of distinction and counted how many acts are in a list. The hierarchical took the act and looked at how acts are stacked over one another. The binary took the act and looked closely at its binary arrangement. The structural took the system—but a system of ToS is nothing other than distinctions that have come together, the act of distinction unfolded into a whole. All four paths, however much they differ, take *one and the same thing*—the act of distinction—and only look at it from different sides: from the side of repeatability, from the side of hierarchy, from the side of binarity, from the side of the whole that has formed.

And the natural number series arises on all four paths because it is *the form of the act of distinction itself*—how the act of distinction is *arranged*, regardless of the side from which one looks at it. The act of distinction is repeatable—and repeatability gives a series: the first act, the second, the third. The act of distinction stacks—and stacking gives a series: the first level, the second, the third. The act of distinction is binary—and the binary record gives a series: the first bit, the second, the third. Distinctions come together into a system—and the system gives a series: the first position, the second, the third. Everywhere one and the same: *beginning and succession*, one and an operation that gives the next number from every number. This arrangement—beginning and succession—is the natural number series; and it belongs to the act of distinction as such, not to any one side of it. That is why every path that proceeds from distinction arrives at the natural number series: it arrives at the form of what it came from.

*Four paths give one natural number series because all four start from the act of distinction, and the natural number series is the form of this very act—how the act of distinction is arranged. Repeatability, hierarchy, binarity, composition into a system are four sides of one act; and from every side one and the same arrangement shows through: beginning and succession. This arrangement is $ℕ$.*

### One Syntax, Several Semantics

What has been said can also be expressed thus. The natural number series has one *syntax* and several *semantics*.

The syntax is the arrangement of the series: an initial element and an operation of succession that gives the next element from each element. This syntax is *one*: all four paths gave exactly it, without discrepancies. The semantics is *what* the initial element and the operation turn out to be on a given path: what they *mean*. And there are *several* semantics. On the cardinal path one means the first distinction, and succession the appending of a distinction. On the hierarchical, one means the base level, succession stacking. On the binary, one means the first bit, succession the adding of a bit. On the structural, one means the first position, succession the passage to the next place. Four semantics—four readings of one and the same syntax.

Herein lies the resolution of the question of the four paths. The paths neither build four different series nor build one series in four ways: they give one series—one syntax—and confer four semantics on it. The natural number series appears as a pure form—the form of beginning and succession—which distinction, from whichever side one takes it, is *compelled* to generate, and which admits of being read in different ways while remaining the same form.

### Summary of Part II

Part II set itself the task of showing that the natural number series is a *structural inevitability*—that $ℕ$ is that to which the derivation of ToS is compelled to come, rather than something invented, postulated, or chosen among possible constructions. At the level of ontological derivation this task has been accomplished.

It was accomplished as follows. Chapter II.1 established the primacy of one: the natural number series begins with one—the number of the first act of distinction—and zero is a mark of absence, not the first number. Then four chapters traversed four independent paths to the natural number series: the cardinal—number as the length of a list of distinctions (Chapter II.2); the hierarchical—number as a level (Chapter II.3); the binary—number as the count of binary choices (Chapter II.4); the structural—number as a position in a system (the present chapter). Four paths, starting from four different places and not taking the *source* of number from one another, arrived at one and the same series. And the present section has named why: the natural number series is the form of the act of distinction itself, and every path that proceeds from distinction arrives at this form.

This gives an ontological proof of what was sought. At the level of the derivation of ToS, the natural number series is not one of the possible constructions that ToS has preferred to others. It is what comes forward inevitably as soon as the act of distinction is posited—for it is the form of this act. The four paths are four pieces of evidence for one inevitability: number is born together with distinction and is inseparable from it. This is an ontological proof, an argument of derivation, not a single machine-checked chain of theorems: the formal anchors of the individual steps have been analyzed in the corresponding chapters and their accompanying remarks, while here the *argument* for the structural inevitability of $ℕ$ has been brought together.

### Transition to Part III

Part II has traversed the natural number series to the end: it has established its primacy, its arrangement, its inevitability. Let us agree on notation: in this volume the symbol $ℕ$ denotes the *positive* natural number series $1, 2, 3, …$—the series of ToS, which begins with one (Chapter II.1). This differs from the widespread convention according to which $0 \in ℕ$; the standard type `nat` of the Rocq repository, for its part, includes $0$ as a constructor, and wherever it is discussed this is specified separately. In the notation adopted here, $ℕ = \{1, 2, 3,
…\}$, and it is precisely this series that Part II has traversed. But the natural number series is not the whole of number. It is its *beginning*.

In the natural number series the larger cannot be wholly subtracted from the smaller—and this shortfall will compel the extension of the series to the integers. In the integers one cannot divide without remainder—and this shortfall will compel going on to the rational numbers. ToS will traverse each extension just as it traversed the natural number series: as a derivation—as what has already been achieved compels—not as an adopted convention. Part III is devoted to this—to the integers and the rational numbers, and then to the interval and the real numbers.

The natural number series has been traversed. Number begins with it; and with it ToS begins the count of its path through mathematics.
