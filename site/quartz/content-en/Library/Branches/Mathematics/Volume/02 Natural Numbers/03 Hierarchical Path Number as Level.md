## The Second Path

### One Series, Several Paths

Chapter II.2 traversed the cardinal path to the natural number series: a number is the length of a list of performed distinctions, and the whole series is generated by the repeatability of the first act. The path was carried through to the end—from one to any number, with induction and potential infinity grounded along the way.

One might consider the matter settled at this point: the series $1, 2, 3, …$ has been obtained, and its arrangement has been clarified. But ToS arrives at the natural number series more than once. Chapter II.1 (§ 1.1) already indicated that there are four paths to $ℕ$, and the cardinal path is only the first. The present chapter opens the second.

Why a second path, if the series has already been built? Because *the same* series, obtained from a *different* derivation, reveals in the natural number series an inevitability that is structural rather than accidental. If $ℕ$ arose in ToS in only one way, one could suspect that it is an artifact of that one way—a peculiarity of cardinal counting and nothing more. But when several independent derivational roads lead to one series, the series turns out to be that to which ToS is compelled to come from different sides, rather than the consequence of a particular device. The multiplicity of paths is evidence, not redundancy.

### How the Second Path Differs from the First

The cardinal and the hierarchical paths lead to one series but start from different things. The difference lies in what each path takes as its *basis*.

The cardinal path (Chapter II.2) starts from the *repeatability of the act*. Its starting point is the act of distinction, which can be performed and, once performed, performed again. A number there is the imprint of the performed repetitions gathered into a list; the natural number series is generated by the fact that one more distinction can be appended to a list of distinctions.

The hierarchical path starts from something else: from the *Law of Order*, $L5$, and the vertical arrangement that this law sets for every system. Its starting point is the *hierarchy of levels*, not the repeatability of the act: systems are arranged as rungs, some levels closer to the ground and others farther from it, rather than mixed together. On this path a number will arise as a *level*—as a rung in the hierarchy, not as the length of a list—and the natural number series as the series of these rungs.

The difference of starting points is essential. The cardinal path says nothing about levels; the hierarchical path says nothing about lists. These are *different* derivations: they do not rest on each other, and neither is derived from the other. And precisely for this reason the coincidence of their result—the fact that both arrive at the series $1, 2, 3, …$—is significant: these are two roads that have converged independently, not one argument retold twice.

### What Lies Ahead in the Chapter

The chapter will traverse the hierarchical path step by step. First we establish what the path rests on: the law $L5$ and the hierarchy of levels it gives (§ 3.2). Then we show that the hierarchy has a ground, that it is finite from below, and why the series of levels therefore begins, and begins with one (§ 3.3). Next comes the core of the path: a level is a number, because the arrangement of levels exactly reproduces the arrangement of the natural number series (§ 3.4). Then we show that ascent through the levels generates the series without bound upward (§ 3.5). After this the hierarchical path will be compared with the cardinal one: two paths, one series (§ 3.6). It will be shown that here, too, induction grows out of the arrangement of the hierarchy rather than being an axiom (§ 3.7). And the chapter will close with the transition to the third path (§ 3.8).

*The hierarchical path is the second of the paths by which ToS reaches the natural number series. It starts from the Law of Order $L5$ and the hierarchy of levels it gives, not from the repeatability of the act, as the cardinal path does. On this path a number is a level of the hierarchy; the natural number series is the series of levels. The coincidence of the results of two independent paths shows that $ℕ$ is a structural inevitability rather than the artifact of a single device.*

## The Law $L5$ and the Hierarchy of Levels

### What the Path Rests On

The hierarchical path starts from the law $L5$ and the hierarchy of levels it gives. Both have already been introduced in Part I: $L5$ in Chapter I.3, the hierarchy of levels in Chapter I.4. The present section briefly collects what the path will rest on, with references to the places where it was established, rather than rediscovering them.

### The Law $L5$: The Condition of the Possibility of Hierarchy

Let us state $L5$ in its own formulation from Chapter I.3, without substituting a paraphrase for it. The laws $L1$–$L4$ concern the structure of *a single* act of distinction: the identity of its sides, their exclusivity, exhaustiveness, and groundedness. $L5$, the Law of Order, differs from them in the level at which it acts: it makes possible the very structure of the *relations between* acts of distinction—their sequence, their ordering, their hierarchy. Ontologically, $L5$ is *the structural condition of the very possibility of hierarchy*: every determinate existence unfolds on determinate levels, and these levels stand in a relation of order—one level precedes another.

Chapter I.3 distinguished two sides in the action of $L5$. *Horizontally*, $L5$ manifests itself in the ordering of elements on one level—in their standing in order relative to one another. *Vertically*, it manifests itself in the hierarchy of levels: the elements of a system of level $n + 1$ are themselves systems of level $n$. The cardinal path (Chapter II.2) rested on the horizontal side of $L5$—on the order of acts in a list. The hierarchical path rests on the *vertical* side—on the hierarchy of levels. One law, two sides; the two paths take different ones.

### The Hierarchy of Levels

What the hierarchy of levels is was established by Chapter I.4 (§ 4.7). Systems of different complexity are not arranged pell-mell, as equals. They form a hierarchy in which a more complex system has more fundamental systems as its elements: the elements of a system of level $n + 1$ lie on more fundamental levels. In the nearest and most graphic case these are systems of level $n$, exactly one rung lower; but the formal idea is broader: all that is required is the relation “lower in level,” not necessarily exactly one step down. This is the vertical arrangement set by $L5$.

The hierarchy is not an additional postulate. Chapter I.4 derived it from what had already been established—from the joint work of $L5$ and the principle $P1$: $L5$ places the organizer above the organized, and $P1$ requires the level of the organized to be strictly below the level of the organizer. Applying this: the elements of a given system are themselves organized, that is, they are themselves systems—but one level lower. The hierarchy of levels is a structural consequence of $L5$ and $P1$ rather than a separate assumption.

This arrangement has a feature worth noting in advance, since it will be needed later. The hierarchy of levels is not *one* fixed ladder. $L5$ sets the *form* of hierarchicality—the relation “a level lower / a level higher”—and this form applies *recursively*, at different scales. There is the *external* hierarchy, the hierarchy of large domains: its ground is Logic, above it is mathematics, and above mathematics are more derivative domains. And there are *internal* hierarchies, hierarchies of levels within a single domain: within mathematics, for example, the natural numbers serve as the ground, above them stand systems of numbers, and above those, systems of such systems. The external and the internal hierarchies are one and the same arrangement of levels applied at different scales, not different arrangements. That every hierarchy, whatever its scale, has *one and the same* form is essential for the hierarchical path: it is precisely this form, and not any particular hierarchy, that the path will take as its basis.

A caveat is needed here so that what follows is not misread. One should distinguish the *global* type of levels, in which the base level, `L1`, is Logic, the ground of everything, from *domain-relative* hierarchies, in which one object or another plays the role of a *local* ground. When we say that within mathematics the natural numbers serve as the ground, this does *not* mean renaming the global `L1`: the natural numbers do not “become Logic.” What is meant is the application of *the same form* of hierarchy within a single domain—a local ladder with its own ground, which in the global hierarchy itself occupies some level above `L1`. The hierarchical path takes as its basis precisely the *form* of hierarchy, indifferent to scale; which object serves as the ground—global Logic or the local numbers—depends on which hierarchy is under consideration.

### The Type of Levels and the Order Relation

Since $L5$ *gives* the hierarchy itself—makes it possible as a structure—the formalization of $L5$ requires a separate structure in which one can speak of levels and the relations between them. ToS introduces such a structure: the type of *levels*, `Level`.

`Level` represents the levels of the hierarchy: there is the level $L_1$, and there are levels above it. A strict order operator is defined on levels—written $\ll$—and the notation $l_1 \ll l_2$ reads: *$l_1$ is closer to the ground than $l_2$*, that is, $l_1$ is more fundamental and $l_2$ more derivative. The semantics of this order is set by $L5$: it is the very order of levels that $L5$ makes possible.

The operator $\ll$ has properties proved in ToS as theorems that formalize $L5$. It is *irreflexive*: no level is closer to the ground than itself—formally, $\neg (l \ll l)$. This is a direct expression of the principle $P1$: nothing precedes itself, otherwise the hierarchy would lose its meaning. It is *transitive*: if $l_1$ is closer to the ground than $l_2$, and $l_2$ than $l_3$, then $l_1$ is closer to the ground than $l_3$. Irreflexivity and transitivity together make $\ll$ a *strict order* relation—what turns a collection of levels into a *hierarchy*.

*The law $L5$ is the condition of the very possibility of hierarchy: the levels of existence stand in a relation of order. The vertical side of $L5$ is the hierarchy of levels, in which the elements of a system of level $n + 1$ lie on more fundamental levels (in the nearest case, of level $n$); it is a consequence of $L5$ and $P1$, not a separate postulate. $L5$ is formalized by the type of levels `Level` with the strict order operator $\ll$ (“closer to the ground”), irreflexive and transitive. It is on this vertical structure that the hierarchical path rests.*

The hierarchy of levels is given. But the path to the natural number series needs one more thing: that the hierarchy have a *beginning*—a ground below which there are no levels. That the hierarchy is finite from below, and why, is the subject of the next section.

## The Hierarchy Is Finite from Below

### What the Hierarchy Needs a Beginning For

The hierarchy of levels is given (§ 3.2): levels stand in the strict order relation $\ll$, some closer to the ground, others farther. But for the path to the natural number series this is not yet enough. The natural number series has a *beginning*: one (Chapter II.1). If the hierarchical path is to lead to the series of levels, the series of levels must also have a beginning—a *base* level below which there is nothing.

The question, then, is this: does the hierarchy have a bottom? Does it not go down without end, so that for every level there would be a still more fundamental one, and another, and so on without limit? If the hierarchy went down infinitely, it would have no beginning; then the series of levels would have no beginning either, and the hierarchical path would not lead to the natural number series, which begins with one. The present section shows that the hierarchy of ToS is *finite from below*: it has a ground.

### The Base Level

Every hierarchy of levels has a least level—the ground, below which there are no levels. ToS calls it the base level.

It is important here not to narrow the concept of the ground. “Ground” is a *relative* concept: the ground is the bottom of a *given* hierarchy, that below which there are no levels *in it*. What exactly occupies the base level depends on which hierarchy is under consideration. For the external hierarchy of large domains (§ 3.2.3), the ground is Logic: the layer on which the laws $L1$–$L5$ themselves work as the conditions of all determinacy; Logic is that with which any system is possible at all, rather than one of the systems inside the hierarchy, and therefore it is at the ground. For the internal hierarchy of mathematics the ground is different: the natural numbers, below which there are no systems in *this* hierarchy. Hierarchies have different grounds; what they have in common is that the ground *exists*.

And this common feature is what the hierarchical path rests on. The path is tied neither to the external hierarchy nor to any internal one; it takes the *form* of hierarchy as such. And as regards the ground, this form is the same in all hierarchies: there is a base level, least in the order $\ll$, below which there are no levels. It is this level, irrespective of what populates it, that we discuss from here on.

In the type of levels `Level`, the base level is a separate, primary element: `L1`. All other levels are obtained from it by stacking; it itself is obtained from nothing—it is not stacked over anything else; it is that with which stacking begins.

### Finiteness from Below Is an Arrangement, Not a Postulate

That the hierarchy is finite from below is a consequence of the arrangement of the type of levels.

The type `Level` is arranged as follows. It has the base level `L1`, and there is a single way to obtain a new level: to *stack* it over one already present. ToS denotes the stacking operation by `LS` (*next level*): from a level $l$ it gives the level `LS` $l$, which stands one rung farther from the ground. There are no other ways to obtain a level: every level *either* is the base `L1` *or* is stacked by the operation `LS` over some level.

From this, finiteness from below follows by itself. Take any *concrete* level and begin to descend—to pass from a level to the one over which it is stacked. Each step down removes one stacking `LS`. But the stackings by which a given level is obtained are *finite* in number: the level is obtained from `L1` by finitely many applications of `LS`, otherwise it would not be a level of the type `Level`. Hence the descent from a given level *comes up against* `L1` after finitely many steps—and there is nowhere further to go: `L1` is stacked over nothing, and there is nothing more to remove. For *each concrete* level the descent downward is finite: it removes finitely many stackings and comes up against the ground.

It is worth stating at once the exact boundary of this claim. What has been said concerns descent *from any concrete level*: such a descent is finite because the number of stackings by which the level is obtained is finite. This is *not* the same as asserting a general theorem on the nonexistence of *any* infinitely descending sequence of levels—a theorem of the form “there is no function $f : \texttt{nat} \to \texttt{Level}$ with $f(n{+}1) \ll f(n)$ for all
$n$.” Such a theorem is not used in the hierarchical path and is not proved in the cited files; it would require a separate formalization (resting on the decrease of `level_depth` along $\ll$, see below).[^1]
The chapter needs only a weaker and directly grounded claim: for each *presented* level the descent is finite and comes up against the ground.

This can also be seen from the side of the operator $\ll$. The statement $l \ll \texttt{L1}$—“$l$ is closer to the ground than the base level”—is *false* for every $l$: nothing is closer to the ground than the ground itself. Below `L1` the order operator points to nothing—not because there is an “empty level” there, but because there *is no level* there. Descent along $\ll$ breaks off at `L1`, since below `L1` the order operator has nothing to match. Formally this is supported by properties of the operator $\ll$ proved in `TheoryOfSystems_Core_ERR.v`: the lemma `level_lt_depth` (depth strictly decreases down the order) and the lemma `level_lt_irrefl` (no level is below itself). The direct minimality of the ground and the upward openness of the hierarchy could conveniently be expressed by two more short lemmas—$\neg (l \ll \texttt{L1})$ for every $l$ (there are no levels below the ground) and $l \ll \texttt{LS} l$ (above every level there is a next one); both are trivially provable from the definition of $\ll$, and adding them to the main file would make the chapter’s reliance on the code direct.[^2]

*Every hierarchy of levels is finite from below: it has a base level—the ground—below which there are no levels. This is the arrangement of the type of levels, not a postulate: every level either is the base or is stacked over another in finitely many steps, and therefore descent from any presented level comes up against the ground after finitely many steps. Infinite descent from a given level is impossible—but this is a claim about each concrete level, not a general theorem about arbitrary descending sequences.*

### Why the Hierarchy Has No “Negative Bottom”

The hierarchy is finite from below—but does this not mean that it simply “comes up against” the base level, while thought is entitled to ask: what is *beneath* the ground? Should the hierarchy not be continued downward—to a “level below the ground,” to something still more original?

This question has no object, and ToS explains why through positive ontology (Chapter I.7). By positive ontology, everything that exists ontologically exists *positively*: there are no “negative objects,” and nonbeing is not a special kind of being. A level is something posited—it exists positively or does not exist at all. A “level below the ground” would be a level that is stacked over nothing and is not itself the ground—that is, posited by nothing and not posited as the original either. There is no such level because there is nothing to *posit* there, not because ToS excludes it by special decree: below the base level of a given hierarchy there is nothing from which one more level could be posited. “Beneath the ground” it is not *empty*—there is simply *no place* there for a level.

In other words, finiteness from below is not a halt before something not yet traversed. It is not a boundary behind which something is hidden. The base level is the bottom of the hierarchy in the full sense—*the one below which there are no levels*, not “the lowest of those traversed so far.”

### Why the Series Begins—and Begins with One

Now we can say what finiteness from below gives the path to the natural number series.

It gives, first, a *beginning*. Since the hierarchy has a base level, the series of levels—if, as § 3.4 will show, levels are numbers—has a first term. The natural number series does not hang without a beginning; it begins where the hierarchy begins—at the ground.

It gives, second, a beginning precisely *with one*, not with zero. Here the hierarchical path converges with what Chapter II.1 established for the cardinal one. ToS assigns to a level its *depth*—the distance from the ground, counted in rungs. The depth of the base level `L1` is *one*: the ground is the *first* level, not a “zeroth level”—it is itself a level, existing and posited, not the absence of a level. The echo of Chapter II.1 is exact here: just as the first performed act of distinction is one, not zero (§ 1.2), so the first level of the hierarchy—the ground—is one, not zero. Zero in the hierarchy would mark the *absence* of a level; but the ground is an existing level, and therefore one corresponds to it.

*The finiteness of the hierarchy from below gives the path to the natural number series a beginning: the series of levels has a first term—the ground. And it gives a beginning precisely with one: the depth of the base level is one, because the ground is an existing level, not the absence of a level. Here the hierarchical path converges with the cardinal one: the primacy of one is confirmed from a second, independent side.*

The hierarchy has a ground, and the series of levels has a beginning. It remains to show the main thing: that the series of levels *is* the natural number series—that a level is a number. This is the subject of the next section.

## Level as Number: `LS` Is `S`

### What Remained to Be Shown

The hierarchy of levels has a ground (§ 3.3) and grows by stacking. This is enough to take the main step of the hierarchical path: to show that the series of levels *is* the natural number series—that a level is a number.

This step is not an equation by resemblance. It will be shown rigorously: the type of levels `Level` and the type of natural numbers are arranged *in one and the same way*, and therefore the series of levels and the natural number series are one and the same structure, presented twice.

### How the Natural Number Series Is Arranged

First, briefly, how the natural number series is arranged. The cardinal path (Chapter II.2) showed this in substance: the series begins with one and grows by passing to the next number. This arrangement has exactly two components. The first: there is an *initial* number, one. The second: there is the operation of *succession*—the passage from a number to the one following it; ToS denotes it by `S` (from the Latin *successor*, “the one who follows”). Every natural number *either* is one *or* is obtained from some number by applying `S`. There are no other numbers, and there is no other way to obtain them.

This is how the natural number series is arranged: an initial element plus an operation of succession that generates the next element from each element. This is the *arrangement* of the series, not a description of it from outside: the series *is* what is generated by one and the repeated application of `S`.

### How the Hierarchy of Levels Is Arranged

Now, how the hierarchy of levels is arranged; § 3.3 has already shown this, and here we need only set it alongside. The hierarchy has exactly two components. The first: there is a *base* level—the ground, `L1`. The second: there is the operation of *stacking*—the passage from a level to the next one, which stands one rung farther from the ground; ToS denotes it by `LS` (*next level*, level successor). Every level *either* is the base `L1` *or* is obtained from some level by applying `LS`. There are no other levels, and there is no other way to obtain them.

Let us set the two arrangements side by side. The natural number series: the initial element `1`, the operation of succession `S`. The hierarchy of levels: the initial element `L1`, the stacking operation `LS`. *This is one and the same arrangement.* The base level corresponds to the initial number; the stacking operation corresponds to the operation of succession. In both cases: one primary element plus one operation that generates the next element from each element, and everything is generated from the primary element by repeating that operation.

### `LS` Is `S`

The coincidence of the arrangements is neither accidental nor approximate. ToS introduces the type of levels `Level` as an *inductive* type—a type every object of which *either* is the base element *or* is obtained by applying a single operation to an already built object of the same type. Formally, `Level` is written as follows:[^3]

```coq
Inductive Level : Set :=
  | L1 : Level
  | LS : Level -> Level.
```

The first line: `L1` is a level—the base one, obtained from nothing. The second: `LS` is an operation that gives, from every level, a level—the one following it. And that is *all*: the type `Level` is exhausted by the base level and stacking.

But the type of natural numbers is arranged in exactly the same way—as an inductive type with a base element and one operation of succession. Precision is needed here. Strictly speaking, `Level` and the type `nat` are *not one and the same* type in Rocq: they are two different inductive declarations, and `LS` is not literally the same constructor as `S`—they act on different types. But both types have *one and the same inductive form*: a base element and one successor constructor. Therefore they are *structurally isomorphic*—and through the isomorphism of `Level` with `nat` the constructor `LS` goes over *exactly* into `S`. In this sense, and only in this sense, `LS` is `S`: one operation of succession taken on two isomorphic types, not the identity of two Coq objects. The heading “`LS` is `S`” should be read thus: `LS` is `S` transferred to the type of levels through the isomorphism.

Hence the result of the hierarchical path. Since `Level` and the type of natural numbers have one and the same inductive form—a base element plus an operation of succession—they are *structurally isomorphic*: two realizations of one inductive scheme, not one type in Rocq. The series of levels $\texttt{L1}, \texttt{LS} \texttt{L1},
\texttt{LS} (\texttt{LS} \texttt{L1}), …$ and the natural number series $1, 2, 3, …$ are one inductive scheme in two presentations—neither two independent series nor literally one Coq type. In this sense the natural number $n$ *is* a level: the level that stands $n - 1$ stackings away from the ground.

*The type of levels `Level` and the type of natural numbers are two inductive types of one form—a base element plus an operation of succession—rather than one and the same type in Rocq. They are structurally isomorphic. Under the isomorphism, `LS` (the stacking of a level) goes over exactly into `S` (the succession of a number); in this sense `LS` is `S` taken on the type of levels. The series of levels and the natural number series are two realizations of one inductive scheme; a number is a level of the hierarchy.*

### The Depth of a Level as a Bridge to Number

The identity of the arrangements can also be presented explicitly, by a correspondence that ToS introduces specially. This is the *depth* function of a level.

The depth of a level is the number of stackings separating it from the ground, counting the ground as the first level. ToS defines it as follows: the depth of the base level `L1` is one; the depth of the level `LS` $l$ is the depth of $l$ increased by one. Formally:

```coq
Fixpoint level_depth (l : Level) : nat :=
  match l with
  | L1    => 1
  | LS l' => S (level_depth l')
  end.
```

The depth function assigns a natural number to each level and thereby serves as a *bridge* between the type of levels and the natural number series. This assignment is the role of the level in the E/R/R analysis (§ 3.8.2): the depth is the number of the level.
Two of its features are essential. The first: the depth of the base level is *one*, not zero—the ground is an existing level, and the first number corresponds to it, not a mark of absence (§ 3.3.5). The second: each stacking `LS` increases the depth by exactly one—a step along the hierarchy corresponds exactly to a step along the natural number series. The second feature is fixed by a simple lemma:

```coq
Lemma level_depth_LS : forall l,
  level_depth (LS l) = S (level_depth l).
Proof. reflexivity. Qed.
```

The lemma is almost trivial—it is proved by a single `reflexivity`—but it is substantive: it shows that `LS` on levels and `S` on numbers agree elementwise through `level_depth`.

Depth does not *add* numbers to the hierarchy from outside; it only *reads* the already existing arrangement: since `Level` is arranged like the natural number series, each level *already* has its number, and `level_depth` presents this number. The bridge does not join two banks—it shows that there is one bank.

That the hierarchy of levels is isomorphic to the natural number series, and that the properties which the standard exposition introduces by axioms of counting turn out to be properties of the inductive type of levels, ToS also establishes formally—by a separate group of proved theorems. But precision is needed here, otherwise the formal side will be read as stronger than it is; let us examine it in three steps.

**Two layers of level numbering.** 
The repository has *two* functions that assign a number to a level, and they give *different* results for the ground. In the main file `TheoryOfSystems_Core_ERR.v`, the function `level_depth` (the listing above) assigns to the base level `L1` the depth *one*: `level_depth L1 = 1`. In the separate file `L5_NatFromHierarchy.v`, the function `level_to_nat` assigns to the base level *zero*: there `level_to_nat LBase = 0`. These are *two different assignments*, not a contradiction, and it is necessary to distinguish them:

| Level | `level_depth` (Core) | `level_to_nat` (separate file) |
|---|---|---|
| `L1` / `LBase` | 1 | 0 |
| `LS L1` / `LSucc LBase` | 2 | 1 |
| `LS (LS L1)` / `LSucc (LSucc LBase)` | 3 | 2 |

The right column is a *technical indexing* by the standard `nat`, which starts at `0`: this is the ordinary isomorphism of the inductive type of levels with the standard `nat`, and it is *not* specific to ToS. The left column is the *ToS reading*: depth as a *positive* level, where the ground is the first existing level, not the absence of a level. The thesis of the chapter is precisely the left column: the ontological depth of a level is read from one. This is *not* a claim that the standard `nat` ceases to start at `0`; the standard indexing stays as it is. ToS only reads *depth* from one—the ground is an existing level, and the first positive number corresponds to it.

**The isomorphism of the standalone type.** 
The file `L5_NatFromHierarchy.v`[^4]
proves the isomorphism of the type of levels with the standard `nat`, and proves it rigorously. But *technically* this file uses *its own copy* of the type of levels: it declares `Inductive Level := LBase | LSucc` and does not import the main file. Therefore its theorems should be read as a formalization of *a structure of the same form*, not literally as theorems about the Core type `Level` from `TheoryOfSystems_Core_ERR.v` (where the constructors are named `L1` and `LS`). The two types are isomorphic in form—a base and one successor constructor—but they are two different Coq declarations. For full synchronization of the repository it would be desirable to move the corresponding lemmas to the main type `Level` or to make the file import the Core definition; until then the isomorphism is proved *for the standalone copy* of the structure of levels.

**What follows from this.** 
With these caveats the formal side is clear. It is proved that the inductive structure “a base plus one successor constructor” is isomorphic to the standard `nat`; under the isomorphism the step `LSucc` goes over exactly into `S`. On the hierarchical path the natural numbers are not introduced by assumption: the structure of every hierarchy of levels *already* is the structure of the natural number series, and this is *discovered*, not postulated. The thesis that *depth* is read from one is, for its part, an ontological reading (the left column of the table), consistent with `level_depth` of the main file.

A level is a number. It remains to go through this result in motion—to see how ascent through the levels *generates* the natural number series. This is the subject of the next section.

## Ascent as the Generation of the Series

### From the Identity of Arrangements to Motion

The previous section established the identity of the arrangements: the type of levels and the type of natural numbers are structurally isomorphic—two realizations of one inductive scheme (§ 3.4). This was established, so to speak, at rest—by presenting the two types and collating their components. Now the same result must be gone through in motion: we must see how the series of levels is not merely *isomorphic* to the natural number series but is *generated*—and generated by ascent through the hierarchy.

This is what the hierarchical path was heading toward. The cardinal path generated the natural number series by appending a distinction to a list (Chapter II.2); the hierarchical path generates it by *ascent*—by stacking level over level. The present section goes through this generation.

### A Step of Ascent Is the Addition of One

Ascent through the hierarchy is the application of the operation `LS`: from the level $l$ to the level `LS` $l$, which stands one rung farther from the ground. One step of ascent is one stacking.

What this step gives in numbers is shown by the depth function (§ 3.4.5). The depth of the level `LS` $l$ is the depth of $l$ increased by one. Hence one step of ascent—one application of `LS`—increases the depth by exactly one. Ascending one rung is exactly the *addition of one* to a number.

Here the hierarchical path converges with the cardinal one, and converges at an essential point. The cardinal path showed (Chapter II.2, § 2.5.2) that to append one more distinction to a list is to increase the count by one—the operation of succession is the appending of a distinction. The hierarchical path shows the same about another operation: to stack one more level over a level is to increase the depth by one—the operation of succession is stacking. One and the same operation of succession appears on the two paths with two of its sides: as the addition of a distinction and as the stacking of a level. The `S` of the cardinal path and the `LS` of the hierarchical one are `S` seen twice.

### Ascent Is Unbounded

The natural number series has no last number: every number has a next one. The hierarchical path must show the same about levels: that ascent does not come up against a *highest* level, that above every level there is a next one.

ToS establishes this directly from the arrangement of the type `Level`. The operation `LS` is defined on *every* level: whatever the level $l$, the expression `LS` $l$ is a level—the one following $l$. There is no level to which `LS` would be inapplicable; hence there is no *highest* level beyond which there would be nothing to stack. Above every level there is a next one, because above every level `LS` gives a level. Formally this is conveniently expressed by two short lemmas—that every level has a level stacked over it, and that every level is below the level stacked over it:

```coq
Lemma level_has_successor :
  forall l : Level, exists l', l' = LS l.
Proof. intro l. exists (LS l). reflexivity. Qed.

Lemma every_level_below_its_successor :
  forall l : Level, l << LS l.
Proof. intro l. simpl. left. reflexivity. Qed.
```

The first lemma says that the stacked level always exists; the second, that it really stands *higher* (the original level is closer to the ground than the level stacked over it). Both are proved immediately from the definitions of `LS` and $\ll$. Together they mean exactly this: ascent does not come up against any highest level.[^5]

The hierarchy, finite from below (§ 3.3), is *unbounded above*. And these are two sides of one arrangement of the type `Level`, not two independent properties: the base level `L1` gives the hierarchy its bottom; the everywhere-defined operation `LS` gives it unbounded ascent. A bottom and openness upward are the same as in the natural number series: one at the beginning, and no last number.

### Ascent Generates the Series

Now the generation of the series is fully visible. We begin at the ground—with the base level `L1`, to which one corresponds. We apply `LS`—we ascend one rung; the depth becomes two. We apply `LS` once more—the depth becomes three. Each step of ascent adds one; each level reached is the next natural number. Ascent, begun from the ground and continued without bound, *generates* the whole natural number series:

$$
\texttt{L1} \;\xrightarrow{\;\texttt{LS}\;}\;
\texttt{LS} \texttt{L1} \;\xrightarrow{\;\texttt{LS}\;}\;
\texttt{LS} (\texttt{LS} \texttt{L1}) \;\xrightarrow{\;\texttt{LS}\;}\;
\cdots
$$

$$
1 \;\xrightarrow{\;+1\;}\; 2 \;\xrightarrow{\;+1\;}\;
3 \;\xrightarrow{\;+1\;}\; \cdots
$$

Two lines, one motion. Ascent through the levels and counting along the natural number series are one and the same generation, written in two notations.

*Ascent through the hierarchy generates the natural number series: begun from the ground (to which one corresponds) and continued by applications of `LS` (each of which adds one to the depth), it gives level after level—number after number. The hierarchy is finite from below and unbounded above; by this very fact it is the natural number series: with a beginning and without an end.*

### The Infinity of the Series of Levels

About the infinity of the series of levels one must say the same as Chapter II.2 said about the infinity of the natural number series—and on the same grounds.

Ascent is unbounded: above every level there is a next one. But the unboundedness of ascent is not the presence of a *completed collection of all levels*. By the principle $P4$—the principle of Finite Actuality (Chapter II.2, § 2.7)—what is actual at every stage is finite; the series of levels is infinite *potentially*—as the unbounded continuability of ascent, not as a ready collection of all levels at once. There is no *highest* level—but neither is there a level-collection that would stand above all levels and contain them all at once.

Precision is needed here, otherwise the claim may be read as stronger than it is. ToS does *not* deny that levels can be spoken of as a whole in the *metalanguage*: formally, the type `Level` exists—it is an inductive type of Rocq (`Level : Set`), and one can speak of all the levels of this scheme in the metalanguage. What is denied is something else: within *the hierarchy itself* there is no level that would contain all levels as its elements. The distinction here is among three things: the type `Level` as an inductive type of the language (it exists); a level-system that would have all levels as its elements (there is none—it would violate $P1$, since it would contain itself as well); and the completed actuality of all levels at one stage (there is none, by $P4$). What ToS rules out is not the metalinguistic description of the type `Level` but the conversion of the open hierarchy into a completed object *on one of its own levels*. Metalinguistic talk about the type and intra-hierarchical completedness are different things; ToS denies the second, not the first.

Here the hierarchical path arrives at the same place as the cardinal one, and this is *one and the same* limitation, seen from a second side. The natural number series is the inexhaustible possibility of ascent, not its exhausted result. The hierarchical path adds nothing new to the infinity of the natural number series; it confirms that the series is infinite potentially, by $P4$—from whichever side one approaches it.

The natural number series has been generated a second time—by ascent through the levels. It remains to compare the two paths traversed and to see that they gave one series. This is the subject of the next section.

## Comparison with the Cardinal Path

### How the Paths Differ

The two paths to the natural number series have been traversed separately—the cardinal in Chapter II.2, the hierarchical in § 3.1–3.5. The present section sets them side by side. The comparison here is neither a choice of the better one nor a check of one path by the other: both have been carried to the end, and both are sound. They need to be set side by side in order to see *what* exactly coincided and what this coincidence means. But we should begin with how the paths differ.

The paths differ in their derivation—in what they start from and by what operation they generate the series.

The cardinal path starts from the *repeatability of the act of distinction*. A number on it is the length of a list of performed distinctions; the generating operation is the appending of one more distinction to the list. The side of the law $L5$ it rests on is the horizontal one: the order of acts in a list.

The hierarchical path starts from the *vertical arrangement* set by $L5$. A number on it is a level of the hierarchy; the generating operation is the stacking of one more level over a level—ascent. The side of $L5$ it rests on is the vertical one: the hierarchy of levels.

The difference is not superficial. The paths are independent *as derivational schemes*: one starts from a list of repeated acts and does not use the concept of level; the other starts from the hierarchy of levels and does not use the concept of list; one builds the series *outward*, laying distinctions side by side, the other *upward*, stacking levels. These are two different derivations, and neither is derived from the other. But their independence is an independence of *schemes of construction*, not independence from the common frame of ToS: both paths work within the same laws $L1$–$L5$ and the principle $P4$, and both rest on the primacy of one (Chapter II.1) and on the potential understanding of infinity. Moreover, both are rooted in the one law $L5$—but in different sides of it: the cardinal path rests on the *horizontal* side of $L5$ (the order of acts in a list), the hierarchical on the *vertical* side (the hierarchy of levels). The paths are independent in derivation and united by the frame; precisely this makes their coincidence significant.

### Where the Paths Coincided

For all the difference in derivation, the result of the two paths is *one*. And this coincidence is exact rather than approximate—a coincidence in the very arrangement.

The cardinal path gave a series with a beginning—one—and the operation of succession `S`: the appending of a distinction. The hierarchical path gave a series with a beginning—the base level, to which one corresponds—and the operation of succession `LS`: the stacking of a level. But `S` and `LS` are, as § 3.4 showed, one and the same operation of succession taken on two types; and the number one of the cardinal path and the number one of the hierarchical path are one and the same first number. Both paths gave *the natural number series*—one series, not two similar ones: with the same number one at its beginning, the same operation of succession, and the same potential infinity by $P4$.

The paths diverge in their starting point and in their generating operation—and converge completely in the result. Different derivations led to an *identical* series.

The difference and the coincidence of the two paths are conveniently summarized in a table:

| Path | Basis | Operation | Number |
|---|---|---|---|
| cardinal (Ch. II.2) | the list of performed distinctions | append one more distinction | the length of the list |
| hierarchical (this chapter) | the hierarchy of levels | stack one more level | the depth of the level |

The two left columns show how the paths *differ*—in basis and in operation; the right column shows that the result in both cases is one and the same natural number, read once as a length and once as a depth.

### Why Two Paths Do Not Give Two Dimensions

Here we should examine a tempting thought that may arise and that, left unchecked, would lead us astray.

The thought is this. The two paths are independent; the cardinal builds the series outward, the hierarchical upward. Do they not thereby set *two axes*—a horizontal and a vertical one—and do two axes not set a *plane*? Does geometry not arise from the two paths to number—a plane whose points are pairs “a number on one axis, a number on the other”?

The thought does not go through, and it is important to see why. Two axes set a plane only when they are *independent* in their result—when the point $(3, 5)$ differs from the point $(5, 3)$, when the number on one axis and the number on the other can differ. But the cardinal and the hierarchical paths are not like that. They are independent in *derivation* but not in result: they give *one and the same* series, not two different series—this is exactly what § 3.6.2 established. Their “independence” is an independence of roads, not of results.

Two paths that give one and the same series are not two axes of a plane. They are two descriptions of *one line*. If we are to speak of axes at all, these are two axes on which the number is always the same—and such axes do not unfold into a plane; they *coincide*. The hierarchical path does not *add* a second dimension to the cardinal one; it traverses the same dimension *once more*, by another road.

*The two paths to the natural number series set neither two dimensions nor a plane. They are independent in derivation but give an identical result—one and the same series. They are two descriptions of one line, not two axes. The hierarchical path adds no dimension—it traverses the same one again.*

How new dimensions arise from numbers—how structures richer than the natural number series are built—is the subject of later parts of the work, and they will be *derived*, each by its own derivation, rather than extracted from the presence of two paths. Here, by contrast, what matters is the opposite: that two independent paths gave *one*. In terms of E/R/R, a path is a role-mode of access to the series, not a separate series-object (analysis in § 3.8.2).

### What the Coincidence of the Paths Means

The coincidence of two independent paths is precisely what made the second path worth traversing.

Were the natural number series reachable in ToS by only one road, there would remain room for doubt: is $ℕ$ not an artifact of that road—a peculiarity of cardinal counting and nothing more? One path shows that the series *can* be built this way; it does not show that the series could not have been otherwise. The second path removes the doubt. The natural number series has been obtained from another starting point, by another operation, resting on another side of $L5$—and *the same* series has been obtained. Hence the series is not tied to a particular device: ToS comes to it from different sides, and each time comes to the same series.

This is an argument for the *structural inevitability* of the natural number series. Not “ToS is able to build $ℕ$” (one path would have shown that too), but “$ℕ$ is that to which the derivation of ToS is compelled to come, from whichever side one approaches it.” The coincidence of independent paths testifies to the subject matter: to the fact that the natural number series was *discovered*, not invented.

*The coincidence of two independent paths is an argument for the structural inevitability of the natural number series. One path would have shown that $ℕ$ can be built; two independent paths giving the same series show that $ℕ$ is that to which the derivation of ToS is compelled to come from any side. The natural number series was discovered, not invented.*

The two paths have been compared. ToS, however, comes to the natural number series four times, not twice (§ 3.1); two more paths lie ahead. But first it is worth returning to a question postponed in § 3.4—induction: on the hierarchical path, too, it is not an axiom. This is the subject of the next section.

## Induction on Levels

### The Postponed Question

In § 3.4 it was noted that the proof of the identity of `Level` and the natural number series rests on the inductive arrangement of the type of levels—and that we would have to return to induction. We return now.

Chapter II.2 showed on the cardinal path that induction in ToS is not an axiom: the base corresponds to the first act of distinction, the step to the repeatability of the act, and the principle of induction is a verbal record of how the series is built (§ 2.6). The hierarchical path comes to the series differently—through ascent through the levels rather than through the repeatability of the act. Hence induction must be grounded anew here as well: we must show that on the hierarchical path, too, it grows out of the arrangement rather than being introduced as a postulate.

### Induction on Levels

The principle of induction for the natural number series was stated in Chapter II.2 (§ 2.6.2): for a property to hold for every number, it suffices that it hold for the first number (the base) and that its holding for $n$ imply its holding for $n + 1$ (the step).

Since the series of levels is the natural number series (§ 3.4), the same principle can be read on levels as well. *Induction on levels:* for a property to hold for every level of the hierarchy, two things suffice—that it hold for the base level (the base), and that its holding for a level $l$ imply its holding for the stacked level `LS` $l$ (the step). The base and the step together entail that the property holds for every level.

### The Base Is the Ground, the Step Is Stacking

Why induction on levels is legitimate can be seen from how the hierarchy is arranged. And the answer here is of the same nature as in Chapter II.2; only the components are different.

The hierarchy is composed of two things, and it has no others (§ 3.3, § 3.4). The first: there is a *base* level—the ground. The second: every level other than the ground is *stacked* over another by the operation `LS`. Let us compare this with induction. The base of induction: the property holds for the base level; the first component of the hierarchy: there is a base level. The step of induction: holding for $l$ implies holding for `LS` $l$; the second component: every level is stacked by the operation `LS`. *The base of induction corresponds to the ground; the step of induction corresponds to stacking.*

Hence its legitimacy. Every level of the hierarchy *either* is the ground *or* is stacked over another level by finitely many applications of `LS` (§ 3.3.3). A property that holds for the ground (the base) and is preserved by stacking (the step) then holds along every finite chain of stackings—that is, for every level. Induction on levels holds sway over the whole hierarchy because the hierarchy consists entirely of the ground and the stackings over it, not by some special power of the principle.

*Induction on levels is not an axiom. The base of induction corresponds to the ground of the hierarchy, the step of induction to the stacking operation `LS`. Induction on levels is a verbal record of how the hierarchy is composed—of the ground and stackings; and it holds sway over the whole hierarchy because the hierarchy consists entirely of them.*

It is worth clarifying how this relates to the formal side. In Rocq, induction on `Level` is not introduced as a separate axiom: the principle `Level_ind` is *automatically generated* by the very inductive declaration of the type `Level`—this is what Rocq does with every inductive type. That is, at the formal layer induction on levels is given together with the type, not postulated over and above it. Ontologically this corresponds exactly to the thesis of ToS: the base and the step of induction are not imposed on the hierarchy from outside—they *express* its arrangement, the structure of the ground and stacking. The formal automaticity of `Level_ind` and the ontological fact that induction is nowhere to be found as a separate postulate are two sides of one thing: induction on levels is contained in how `Level` is built.

### The Same Induction, a Different Grounding

It is worth saying precisely how induction on levels relates to the induction of the cardinal path (§ 2.6).

The induction is *the same* in form. Since the series of levels is isomorphic to the natural number series, induction on levels and induction on numbers have one and the same form—a base plus a preserved step. But the same precision is needed here as in § 3.4 regarding `LS` and `S`. Strictly speaking, `Level_ind` (the induction principle for the type of levels) and `nat_ind` (the induction principle for `nat`) are *not* literally one and the same Coq object: they are different induction principles, since they belong to different inductive types. Rocq *generates* each of them *automatically* from the declaration of its type. Through the isomorphism of `Level` with `nat` these two principles have one and the same form and carry over into each other—but what is at issue is the *structural coincidence* of the inductive scheme, not the literal equality of two Coq objects. `Level_ind` is `nat_ind` transferred to the type of levels through the isomorphism—exactly as `LS` is `S` transferred to levels (§ 3.4.4).

But the *grounding* of induction differs on the two paths—and that is the whole point. The cardinal path grounded induction in the repeatability of the act: the base is the first act, the step is one more act (§ 2.6.3). The hierarchical path grounds it in the arrangement of the hierarchy: the base is the ground, the step is stacking. One and the same principle of induction receives two independent groundings on the two paths. And this is the same as what happened with the natural number series itself (§ 3.6): one principle, not two; two independent paths to one principle, not two groundings of one and the same argument. The coincidence testifies here just as it testified there: induction is not an arbitrarily accepted axiom—it is that to which ToS is compelled to come in tracing the arrangement of the series, from whichever side one approaches this arrangement.

*Induction on levels and induction on numbers are one principle, because the series is one. But its grounding differs on the two paths: the cardinal path derives induction from the repeatability of the act, the hierarchical path from the arrangement of the hierarchy. One principle, two independent groundings—and this, like the coincidence of the paths themselves, is an argument that induction is something discovered, not a postulate.*

The hierarchical path has been traversed completely: the natural number series has been obtained as the series of levels and compared with the cardinal one, and induction on it has been grounded. It remains to sum up the chapter and pass to the third path. This is the subject of the concluding section.

## Summary

### What the Chapter Has Covered

The chapter has traversed the second of the four paths of ToS to the natural number series—the hierarchical path.

The path began with the observation that ToS comes to the natural number series more than once: several independent derivational roads lead to one series, and their multiplicity is evidence, not redundancy (§ 3.1). The hierarchical path rested on the law $L5$ and the hierarchy of levels it gives—on the vertical side of $L5$, whereas the cardinal path rested on the horizontal side (§ 3.2). It was shown that every hierarchy of levels is finite from below: it has a ground, and finiteness from below is the arrangement of the type of levels, not a postulate; hence the series of levels has a beginning, and a beginning precisely with one, since the depth of the base level is one (§ 3.3). Then came the core of the path: the type of levels `Level` and the type of natural numbers are arranged in one and the same way—a base element plus an operation of succession—and therefore `LS` (the stacking of a level) is `S` (the succession of a number), and the series of levels is the natural number series; a number is a level (§ 3.4). This identity was gone through in motion: ascent through the hierarchy, begun from the ground and continued without bound, generates the natural number series, and the series of levels is infinite potentially, by $P4$ (§ 3.5). The two paths—the cardinal and the hierarchical—were compared: different in derivation, they give an identical series, and this coincidence is an argument for the structural inevitability of $ℕ$; moreover, two independent paths do not set two dimensions but are two descriptions of one line (§ 3.6). Finally, on the hierarchical path, too, induction proved to be no axiom: its base corresponds to the ground of the hierarchy, its step to stacking (§ 3.7).

Thus the hierarchical path has been traversed: a natural number is a level of the hierarchy, and the whole natural number series is generated by ascent from the ground along the rungs set by the law $L5$.

### E/R/R Analysis: The Number-Level as a System

The path traversed has an E/R/R arrangement (Part I), and its analysis consolidates the result of the chapter. The header of the separate anchor file gives the labeling directly: *elements—levels; role—encoding the hierarchy; rules—the isomorphism with `nat` and the irreflexivity of the order*.[^6] We conduct the analysis in the ontological order Rules $\to$ Roles $\to$ Elements. A caveat on status: the “system” here is a substantive interpretation (the number-level); its formal support is the inductive type `Level` and the proved isomorphism with `nat`, and the strict machine isomorphism is established on a *separate copy* of the type of levels, isomorphic in form to the Core type (§ 3.4).

*Rules* (the law $L5$). The constitution of the system is the law $L5$: it is what makes the hierarchy of levels possible, establishing that the system *builds up* levels (§ 3.2). Adjoining it are three rules proved in the code. The first is the isomorphism with `nat`: `LS` goes over exactly into `S` (§ 3.4), and the series of levels is the natural number series. The second is the *irreflexivity* of the order (`hierarchy_irrefl`: no level is below itself) together with finiteness from below: the hierarchy has a ground, and the series begins with one (§ 3.3). The third is induction on levels: the base corresponds to the ground, the step to stacking (§ 3.7). And the most general constitution is $P4$: ascent is a process, and the series of levels is potentially infinite, not completed (§ 3.5).

*Roles—the significance of positions* (the law $L4$). The role of a level is its *depth*—the number of stackings separating it from the ground. The function `level_depth` assigns this number to each level and thereby serves as the bridge level $\to$ number (§ 3.4.5): the role of a level *is* its number. The role is constituted by a rule: depth is defined by the induction of base and stacking—the same rules that set the hierarchy. There is also a second role layer: the *paths* to the series themselves—the cardinal and the hierarchical—are two role-modes of access to one series, not two different series (on this, § 3.8.3).

*Elements—the bearers* (the law $L1$ and the principle $P4$). The elements are the levels themselves: the base `L1` and the stacked `LS` $l$. By $L1$, each level is identical to itself (equality of levels is decidable). By $P4$, every level is reached in finitely many stackings from the ground—each bearer is finite, although the series of bearers is potentially infinite.

Let us gather the analysis into a table.

| Component | What it determines | E/R/R |
|---|---|---|
| $L5$; the isomorphism with `nat` (`LS` is `S`) | the constitution of the system | Rule |
| irreflexivity + finiteness from below | the beginning of the series (with one) | Rule |
| induction on levels; ascent ($P4$) | the generation of the series | Rule |
| depth `level_depth` (the bridge to number) | the role of a level | Role |
| levels `L1`, `LS` $l$ | bearers (finite, $P4$) | Element |
| laws $L1$–$L5$ (here $L5$) | universal layer | Rule (univ.) |

*Well-formedness.* The labeling is unambiguous: levels are elements, depth is the role, $L5$ with the isomorphism and irreflexivity are rules; no layer stands in for another. The principle $P1$ (no self-membership) is satisfied here in an especially graphic form—as the proved *irreflexivity*: no level is below itself (`hierarchy_irrefl`), the hierarchy ascends strictly, without loops, and the system does not contain itself in the role of its own level. And here the analysis turns into a *diagnosis*. The temptation to see in the two paths—the cardinal and the hierarchical—two *different* numbers (or two dimensions of number) is a confusion of categories: a path is a *role*, a mode of access, not an *element*-object; the two modes lead to one and the same series (§ 3.8.3). Likewise, one should not confuse a *level* (an element) with its *depth* (a role-number): “`LS` is `S`” asserts the coincidence of the *rule* of succession under the isomorphism, not the identity of two Coq objects (§ 3.4).

What the analysis yields. It explains *why* the two independent paths converged on one series: a number is a *role*—a position set by the base and succession—and both paths assign this role by one and the same rules ($L1$–$L5$), only on different bearers (lists of distinctions there, levels here). The coincidence is not accidental: one system of roles in two realizations. In this way Part II also prepares the remaining paths: each next path is a new bearer of the same role structure of number, and the next one, the binary path, will add one more (§ 3.8.4).

### Two Paths, One Series

Part II has now traversed two paths to the natural number series, and it is worth naming what has thereby been achieved.

The cardinal path (Chapter II.2) derived $ℕ$ from the repeatability of the act of distinction: number as the length of a list of distinctions. The hierarchical path (the present chapter) derived $ℕ$ from the Law of Order: number as a level of the hierarchy. The derivations are independent—neither uses the premises of the other—and the result is identical: one and the same natural number series, with the same number one at its beginning, the same operation of succession, and the same potential infinity.

This coincidence is the gain of the two chapters. The natural number series has appeared as that to which the derivation of ToS comes *in different ways and each time to the same thing*, not as a construction tied to a single device. Each subsequent path will strengthen this argument.

### Transition to the Third Path

ToS counts four paths to the natural number series (§ 3.1). Two have been traversed; two remain.

The third path is the *binary*, or informational, path. It starts from the fact that every act of distinction is a choice between *two*—the positing of $A$ against $\neg A$, and nothing third: by its very arrangement the act of distinction is binary. From this binarity—from the fact that the unit of distinction is one “yes or no” choice—an approach of its own to the natural number series will unfold: number as the length of a binary record, as the count of binary choice. This is the path on which the natural number reveals its kinship with the concept of information.

The next chapter is devoted to the third path.
