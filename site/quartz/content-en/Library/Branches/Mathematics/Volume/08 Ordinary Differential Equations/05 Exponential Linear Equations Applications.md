## Where the Chapter Stands

### Closing Part VIII

Part VIII has traveled the path from ontology to guarantees: Chapter 8.1 redefined the solution of the Cauchy problem as a process of approximations, Chapter 8.2 presented Euler’s method on a grid, Chapter 8.3 proved that the Picard operator is a contraction on the space of grid functions (the contracting mechanism and the geometric drawing-together of the iterates), and Chapter 8.4 analyzed stability and Grönwall. The present, final chapter does three things: it shows the process on *canonical* equations (the exponential, linear equations, systems), carries the matter through to *applications* (price dynamics), and *synthesizes* the whole Part, marking the transition to partial differential equations (Part IX).

All the examples obey the single ontology built in the preceding chapters: the solution is a process of rational approximations; the right-hand side is the law of velocity; the completed functions $e^t$, $\sin$, $\cos$ are role-limits, not pregiven transcendentals. The chapter introduces no new apparatus; it shows that the language built so far *works* on concrete equations and applications.

### What This Chapter Claims—and What It Does Not

The proved core is concrete processes over ℚ: the exponential trajectory of $y' = y$ as the ladder $(1+h)^n y_0$, the decay $y' = -\alpha y$, the general linear equation $y' = L y$, the vector step for systems and the harmonic oscillator $y'' = -y$, and discrete price dynamics.[^1] All the stages are finite rational numbers, checkable by exact arithmetic.

What the chapter does *not* do: it does not present $e^t$, $\sin$, $\cos$ as completed transcendentals, does not prove the exact conservation of the oscillator’s energy, and does not build global-in-time solutions. These objects are role-limits, and $\varepsilon$-convergence to them inherits L3 (as in Chapters 8.3–8.4).

## The Exponential as a Process

The canonical example is the equation $y' = y$, $y(0) = 1$, whose solution is $y(t) = e^t$. Euler’s method for it is especially transparent: since the slope equals the value, each step multiplies by $(1+h)$, and over $n$ steps we get $(1+h)^n$.[^2] Remarkably, this process coincides with the homogeneous Grönwall iteration of Chapter 8.4: exponential growth and the Grönwall estimate are literally one and the same recursion.[^3]

Reaching the moment $t=1$ in $N$ steps of size $1/N$, we obtain the ladder $(1+1/N)^N$, the $e$-process analyzed in Chapter 8.2: the rational stages $2, 9/4, 64/27, …$, bounded below by two for all $N$ and beginning the classical process of approximation to $e$ (the completed $e$ is a role-limit).[^4] For an arbitrary time it is analogous: $e^t$ is the role-limit of the process $(1+t/N)^N$, whose stages are rational numbers.[^5]

*$e$ and $e^t$ are not completed transcendentals but role-limits of rational processes: the ladders $(1+1/N)^N$ and $(1+t/N)^N$ generated by the equation $y'=y$. To ask “what does $e^t$ equal” is to ask “what process sets it”; the answer is the Euler trajectory of the growth equation. The stage is actual; $e^t$ is a direction.*

## Linear Equations: Growth and Decay

The equation $y' = L y$, the general linear homogeneous equation, behaves like a scaled exponential: the Euler step multiplies by $(1+Lh)$, and the sign of $L$ decides the fate of the process. For $L > 0$ there is growth (a factor greater than one); for $L < 0$, decay.

Decay—$y' = -\alpha y$ with $\alpha > 0$—is especially important for applications (cooling, radioactive decay, relaxation).[^6] Here the factor of the step is $1 - \alpha h$, and for $0 \le 1 - \alpha h \le 1$ the process does not grow but decreases: exactly the case of damped Grönwall from Chapter 8.4, $u_n \le u_0$. The solution $y(t) = y_0 e^{-\alpha t}$ is again the role-limit of the rational process $y_0 (1-\alpha h)^n$, and the completed decreasing exponential is not built as an object.

*A linear equation sets a role-rate: the constant $L$ (or $-\alpha$) is the rate at which the process multiplies itself at each step. Growth and decay are two regimes of one rule; in both the solution is a process of factors, and the completed exponential is a role-limit.*

## Systems and the Harmonic Oscillator

So far we have dealt with a single equation. But many problems are *systems*: a pair (or an $n$-tuple) of quantities evolving jointly. The vector Euler step carries everything said over to pairs: the state $(y, v)$ is updated componentwise, $(y, v) \mapsto (y + h f_1, v + h f_2)$.[^7]

The vector form makes it possible to reduce a *higher-order equation* to a first-order system: $y'' = g(t, y, y')$ becomes the pair $y' = v$, $v' = g(t, y, v)$; introducing the velocity as an independent role enlarges the state space and lowers the order.[^8] The classical example is the harmonic oscillator $y'' = -y$, that is, the pair $y' = v$, $v' = -y$. Its exact solution is $(\cos t, \sin t)$, and this trajectory is the role-limit of the vector Euler process; $\sin$ and $\cos$ as completed transcendentals are not Elements but role-limits, exactly like $e$.

The oscillator also lays bare the *honest* side of the numerical method: explicit Euler does not conserve energy. The quantity $E = y^2 + v^2$, classically constant, *drifts upward* on the grid: in two steps with $y_0=1, v_0=0, h=1/10$ it grows from $1$ to $1.0201$.[^9] This is not a defect of the exposition but a *property* of the explicit method, honestly computed: exact conservation of energy belongs to the continuous flow of the oscillator (or to specially structure-preserving, symplectic, schemes), whereas explicit Euler on a finite grid gives a systematic rational drift.

*A system is a joint vector process; the velocity in the reduction of order is an independent role, not an auxiliary symbol. The energy of the oscillator is a role-invariant of the continuous flow (and of structure-preserving schemes); its drift on the explicit grid is an honest rational Element. $\cos$ and $\sin$ are role-limits of the vector process, as $e$ is the role-limit of the scalar one.*

## Applications: Price Dynamics

The language of ODE processes goes beyond pure mathematics. In applied fields the equation of change often sets *discrete* dynamics from the very start—and then the process form is not an approximation but the model proper. A simple example is price dynamics: the price is updated at each step by an external impact (an inflow of demand, news, a flow of orders),

$$
p_{n+1} = p_n + f_n,
$$

and the whole trajectory is a process over the list of impacts.[^10] Concretely: the price $100$ under the impacts $1, -\tfrac12,
\tfrac34, -\tfrac14, \tfrac12$ passes through the stages $100, 101, \tfrac{201}{2}, \tfrac{405}{4},
101$ and arrives at $\tfrac{203}{2} = 101.5$, an exact rational trajectory.[^11] The file also introduces a stability check through a Lipschitz coefficient: an analogous Lipschitz condition (a contraction coefficient less than one), akin to the condition $LT<1$ of Picard contraction (Chapter 8.3), read as a sign of stable dynamics.

*In applied dynamics the process is not an approximation to a completed curve but the model itself: the price is a role-process updated by impacts. Stability is governed by a Lipschitz condition of the same type as Picard convergence: a single E/R/R mechanism works in both the pure and the applied problem.*

## The Synthesis of Part VIII and the Transition to Partial Differential Equations

### The Unified E/R/R Structure of the Part

All the chapters of Part VIII described one system from different angles: *the solution of a differential equation as a process*. Let us gather its E/R/R structure, common to the exponential, linear equations, systems, and applications; the analysis is an ontological comprehension of the ground covered, not new formal content. The abbreviation sets the epistemic order (Elements $\to$ Roles $\to$ Rules); we proceed in the generating one.

**Rules ($\leftrightarrow$ L5).** At the foundation are the equation itself, $y' = f(t,y)$ (the law of velocity), and the rule for generating approximations (the Euler step or the Picard operator). Above them is the guarantee: under the Lipschitz condition the iteration contracts (Chapter 8.3), whence the Cauchy convergence of the process and the control of divergence by Grönwall (Chapter 8.4). The universal layer is L1–L5, with L3 entering only where a role-limit is read as a completed object.

**Roles ($\leftrightarrow$ L4).** The right-hand side $f$ is the role-law-of-velocity; the approximation $y_n$ is the role-stage; the fixed point of the operator is the role-solution; the Lipschitz constant is the role-rate; and the completed $e^t$, $\sin$, $\cos$ and the solution on an infinite interval are role-limits.

**Elements ($\leftrightarrow$ L1${}+{}$P4).** At each stage there are
finite rational data: the values $y_n$, the nodes $t_n$, the factors $(1+Lh)^n$, the index $n$. Under P4 completed solution functions and transcendentals are not elements: the finite stages of processes are actual.

| **Component** | **What it determines** | **E/R/R** |
|---|---|---|
| equation $y'=f$; Euler step / Picard operator; contraction $\Rightarrow$ Cauchy, Grönwall | HOW the solution process is generated and controlled | Rules ($L5$) |
| law of velocity $f$; stage $y_n$; solution; rate $L$; $e^t,\sin,\cos$ as a limit | WHAT FOR the bearers are significant: roles in the process | Roles ($L4$) |
| $y_n, t_n, (1+Lh)^n \inℚ$; index $n\inℕ$ | WHAT exists at each stage (finite, P4) | Elements ($L1$, P4) |

### What Is Proved and Where the Boundary Lies

Let us draw up the balance of the Part. *Proved over ℚ without axioms*: Euler’s method and its concrete trajectories; the Picard operator as a contraction on grid functions ($ℚ^{N+1}$, the sup metric) with the geometric gap between the iterates; the uniqueness of the solution on the grid through a finite maximum; stepwise and geometric control of divergence; homogeneous Grönwall and decay; the exponential, linear equations, the oscillator, and price dynamics as concrete rational processes. *The boundary* (L3 / role-limit): the $\varepsilon$-convergence of iterations and factors to a completed limit; completed solution functions, $e^t$, $\sin$, $\cos$ as objects; global-in-time continuation, blow-up, the maximal interval. This boundary is not a gap but an honest demarcation: what is a process over ℚ is not passed off as a completed object.

### The Transition to Partial Differential Equations

Part VIII solved ordinary equations: evolution in time. The next step is partial differential equations (Part IX): the evolution of a function that depends on both time and space. The process ontology carries over without any change of principle: space is discretized by a grid of nodes, time by steps, and the solution is a process on a *space-time* grid. The heat equation will become a process of smoothing, the wave equation a process of propagation, and the integral and derivatives finite differences over ℚ. What in Part VIII was a ladder of numbers $(1+1/N)^N$ will in Part IX become a ladder of *grid functions*; but the ontology—the solution is a process, not a completed object—remains the same.

*The solution of a differential equation—ordinary or partial—is a process of approximations whose stages are finite and rational, and the completed solution function is a role-limit. This is the outcome of Part VIII: equations of change are solved not by extracting a ready object from a function space but by unfolding a Cauchy process, guaranteed by contraction and controlled by Grönwall.*
