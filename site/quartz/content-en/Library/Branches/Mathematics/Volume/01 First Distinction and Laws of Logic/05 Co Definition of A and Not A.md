## Distinction versus Separation

### Where We Have Come From

By the beginning of the present chapter, the chain traced in Chapters 1–4
has reached its final link. From the pre-formal first principle
$A = \exists$, the act of distinction was singled out as the first
formalizable; this act received the record `Distinction` with four fields
(Chapter 2); from the structure of the record the laws $L1$–$L5$ were
derived (Chapter 3); after the laws, the principles $P1$–$P4$ and the
structure $E/R/R$ were introduced as the next ontological layer, and in
Chapter 4 its metaphysical application to the Absolute as well.

*Architecturally*, these links form the chain

$$
A = \exists \;\to\; `Distinction` \;\to\;
  L1\text{–}L5 \;\to\; P1\text{–}P4 \;\to\; E/R/R.
$$

At the same time, as was noted in Chapters 3 and 4, in the *current*
formalization the links of this chain are represented by different files
and are not always brought together into one single theorem: the
derivation of $L1$–$L5$ from `Distinction` and the derivation of
$P1$–$P4$ from the laws are formal theorems, whereas the bridge
$P1$–$P4 \to E/R/R$ is an architectural interpretation, and the
application of $E/R/R$ to the Absolute is a metaphysical step, not a
machine-checked one. The present chapter does not move the chain further.
It returns to its *first link*—the record `Distinction`—and unfolds what
was fixed in its arrangement but not fully disclosed in substance.
Namely: the positive and negative sides of the act of distinction are not
two separate things that are afterward somehow related. They co-arise in
the act itself, and this co-arising is a *central* property of the
structure, not an auxiliary remark about `Distinction`.

\sloppypar
In § 2.6 of the present work this was designated as the impossibility of
building `Distinction` having only one of the sides. At that point the
observation played a *minimalist* role: it entered the general check of
the minimality of the record—every field is necessary. Now, after the
whole derivational chain has been unfolded, the same structural feature
acquires another meaning: it is the substantive expression of the fact
that *negation* in the Theory of Systems is an *aspect of the primary
act*, not an operation on something already ready-made. This is the main
position of the present chapter, and unfolding it requires first fixing
how co-definition differs from all other kinds of connection between
sides.

Let us begin by contrasting two stances: the separation of the
ready-made and distinction in a single act.

### Two Stances: Separation and Distinction

In the standard stance toward working with opposites, the world consists
of *ready-made* objects. There exists an object $A$—a particle, a
statement, a concept. An *operation* of negation can be performed on this
object. The operation yields another object—$\neg A$—which by
construction is “what is not $A$.” Structurally, this stance has three
steps:

1. An object $A$ is given—by itself, without $\neg A$.
2. The operation of negation is applied: $\neg(\cdot)$.
3. A second object $\neg A$ is obtained—by itself, as the result of applying the operation.

*Time* in these three steps has no physical meaning; what is at issue is
*logical order*: first there is $A$, then $\neg A$ as something
derivative. Let us call this stance **the separation of the ready-made**:
work with two objects, one of which is primary while the second is
obtained from the first by an operation.

In the stance unfolded in ToS, the picture is different. There is no
primary object $A$ to which an operation yielding $\neg A$ is afterward
applied. There is an *act of distinction* in which both sides—what is
singled out and that against which it is singled out—arise *jointly*.
Structurally, this stance has a single step:

1. An act of distinction is performed; in this act $A$ and $\neg A$ are given simultaneously as two sides of one arrangement.

Let us call this stance **distinction in a single act**: work with one
structure that has two sides as its *aspects*, not as two independent
objects connected with each other in a derivative way.

*The separation of the ready-made and distinction in a single act are not
two descriptions of one operation. They are two different ontological
stances regarding what negation is and what the status of its sides is.*

In the first stance, negation is an *action* on an object; in the second,
it is a *structural condition* for an object to be designated as $A$ at
all. In the first, $A$ can be thought separately from $\neg A$; in the
second, such separate thinking is impossible, since without indicating
what $A$ *is not*, $A$ itself has not yet been singled out.

### An Objection: Is This Not One and the Same?

The first objection to the difference between the two stances runs as
follows. Suppose there is an operation of negation
$\neg(\cdot) : Prop \to Prop$. Applied to $A$, it yields $\neg A$. But
this very operation is *defined* so that $\neg A$ and $A$ are mutually
exclusive and jointly exhaust the space of possibilities. Does this not
give the same as a single act? Why introduce a difference between two
stances if the result is one and the same?

The difference here lies in the *ontological status* of the objects,
*not in the result*. In the stance of the separation of the ready-made,
$A$ is there *before* the operation is applied: it is thought of as a
meaningful object by itself, its properties can be established and fixed,
and only after that is the action performed that yields $\neg A$. In the
stance of distinction in a single act, $A$ *has no* meaningful status
before the act of distinction: until what $A$ is not has been singled
out, there is no $A$ itself as a determinacy either. There exists only the
pre-formal indistinction analyzed in § 2.1.

This difference has concrete consequences. If $A$ is there before
$\neg A$, one can ask: “what are the properties of $A$ considered in
isolation, without reference to $\neg A$?” This question has meaning
within the separation of the ready-made, for $A$ as a ready-made object
has properties of its own. Within distinction in a single act this
question *has no content*: to speak of $A$ without $\neg A$ is to speak of
what has not yet been singled out as a determinacy, that is, of the
pre-formal, to which formal predicates do not apply.

### Co-Definition: A Definition in the Strict Sense

For the further work to be exact, let us fix what precisely is meant by
*co-definition*.

**Co-definition** is the structural simultaneity of specification in
which neither side can be indicated before the other—neither by the
order of construction, nor by logical inference, nor by epistemic access.

This definition calls for clarification in three directions, since
co-definition is easily confused with three other kinds of connection
between objects.

**Co-definition and mutual dependence.** 
In *mutual dependence* there are two objects $X$ and $Y$, existing
independently, between which a relation is established by which each
depends on the other. For example, the pressure and the volume of a gas
in an isothermal process are connected by the relation
$PV = \mathrm{const}$: knowing one, one can determine the other. But $P$
and $V$ as physical quantities exist *by themselves*—they can be measured
separately, they have dimensions, they can figure in other equations
independently. Mutual dependence is a *relation* imposed on
already-existing objects.

Co-definition in our sense is different. The positive and negative sides
of the act of distinction have no status *by themselves* between which a
relation is afterward established. They appear as *sides of one
arrangement*, and outside this arrangement they do not exist as
determinacies. Metaphorically: $P$ and $V$ are two objects on a table tied
together with a rope; the positive and negative sides are the two ends of
one rope, and outside it there are neither ends nor sides.

**Co-definition and equivalence.** 
In classical logic, *equivalence* asserts $P \iff Q$, that is, $P$ holds
if and only if $Q$ holds. This is a symmetric relation between two
propositions that are *both* meaningful outside the equivalence. One can
ask about $P$ separately—whether it is provable, whether it is false, in
which models it is satisfied. Equivalence only fixes that these questions
receive one and the same answer for $P$ and for $Q$.

Co-definition is not equivalence. The positive and negative sides of the
act of distinction are not equivalent—on the contrary, they are mutually
exclusive (the field `exclusive`). But it is precisely through this
mutual exclusivity that they *do* determine each other: what
$\mathit{positive} D$ means is determined by its being *not*
$\mathit{negative} D$, and conversely. Equivalence is a relation between
independently meaningful propositions; co-definition is their *joint*
acquisition of meaningfulness.

**Co-definition and mutual derivability.** 
One can imagine a situation in which a statement $B$ is formally derived
from a statement $A$, and conversely. For example, in groups: “the
element $g$ commutes with all elements” and “$g$ belongs to the center of
the group” are two statements derivable from each other by the
definition of the center. Each of them *uses* concepts already introduced
in group theory, and each is meaningful by itself as a statement about
the element $g$. Derivability is a *logical* step performed on already
determined statements.

Co-definition works *one step deeper*—where formal inference cannot yet
be performed, since the very determinacies between which an inference
could be performed are not yet there. Co-definition specifies these very
determinacies; formal inference is possible only *after* co-definition
has taken place.

The upshot of the three distinctions can be written as follows. Mutual
dependence is a relation between ready-made objects. Equivalence is a
relation between ready-made propositions. Mutual derivability is a formal
step on ready-made statements. Co-definition is the *simultaneous
acquisition* of their determinacy by two sides in one structural act; all
of the above presupposes a co-definition that has already taken place and
works on its result.

### Connection with § 2.6

In § 2.6 of the present work it was shown that one cannot build
`Distinction` having only the positive side without the negative one, and
vice versa. Technically this was expressed through the structure of the
constructor `mkDistinction`, which requires all four fields at once: no
field can be presented without the others.

This conclusion was obtained in the context of discussing the
*minimality* of the record: every field is necessary, and none can be
removed without loss of structure. Co-definition then appeared as one of
four observations about the impossibility of removal, alongside the
discussion of the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive`.

Now the same structural feature is considered in another role—as a
*general ontological position* with respect to negation, not as one of
four particular observations. That the positive and the negative are
co-defined is not a particular, technical property of the record
`Distinction`; it is a *consequence* of how ToS sees the very operation
of negation: as an *aspect of the primary act*, not as an operation on a
ready-made object.

### Where in the Book Separation Works and Where Distinction Works

The distinction between the two stances should not be understood
polemically, as if the stance of the separation of the ready-made were
wrong and the stance of distinction in a single act were right. Both
stances have their own domain of application.

The separation of the ready-made is the natural stance in all situations
where objects *already* exist and the operation of negation is applied to
them as to something given. When a mathematician writes $\neg(x = y)$,
the mathematician relies on $x = y$ as an already-determined statement,
and its negation is a meaningful operation yielding another statement.
This is a working rule of classical logic, and it remains in force.

Distinction in a single act is a deeper stance, working at another level.
It answers the question: *where* does the determinacy $x = y$ as such,
which can then be subjected to negation, come from in the first place?
The answer: from an act of distinction in which $x = y$ and $\neg(x = y)$
co-arise as two sides of an arrangement; only after this co-arising can
one work with one of the sides, applying formal operations to it.

ToS does not cancel the work of classical logic at its own level; ToS
*deepens* it, pointing to the structure without which this work would be
impossible. The distinction between the stances is the distinction
between the *working level* of formal logic (the separation of the
ready-made) and the *ontological ground* of this work (distinction in a
single act). The detailed relation between these two levels is the
central content of § 5.4 of the present chapter.

Having fixed the conceptual difference between separation and
distinction and having defined co-definition in the strict sense, we can
now turn to how this difference is expressed in the record `Distinction`
itself—as a *formal* arrangement in which four fields are specified by
one constructor that requires their simultaneous presentation.

## The Formal Expression of Co-Definition

### The Record as the Simultaneous Presentation of Four Fields

In § 5.1 co-definition was fixed as the structural simultaneity of
specification: no side of a distinction can be indicated before the
other—neither by the order of construction, nor by logical inference, nor
by epistemic access. Let us now show how this simultaneity is expressed in
the record `Distinction`—as a structural arrangement of the type itself,
not as a commentary on its property.

Let us recall the record:

```coq
Record Distinction := mkDistinction {
  positive   : Prop;
  negative   : Prop;
  exclusive  : ~ (positive /\ negative);
  exhaustive : positive \/ negative
}.
```

The following is essential. `Record` in Rocq is a construction that, to
build an instance, requires the *simultaneous* specification of values
for *all* the declared fields. One cannot present two fields and leave
the other two unwritten: then CIC has no instance—there is only an
intention to build one, not carried through to the end. Only at the
moment when *all four* fields `positive`, `negative`, `exclusive`,
`exhaustive` are given concrete values is the constructor `mkDistinction`
successfully applied, producing an instance of the type `Distinction`.

This fact is a general property of records in typed languages, not a
peculiarity of Rocq. A record formally determines that an instance is a
*quadruple* of values consistent in their types. A quadruple lacking one
element is not a quadruple with the fourth missing; it is *nothing*, an
object not yet built.

### Dependent Typing: The Connection of Fields within the Record

In the record `Distinction` the four fields are not structurally on an
equal footing. The fields `positive` and `negative` are ordinary values of
type Prop. But the types of the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive`
*depend* on the already specified values of `positive` and `negative`:

- The type of the field `exclusive` is $\neg (`positive` \wedge `negative`)$. This expression has no meaning until `positive` and `negative` are specified: $\wedge$ and $\neg$ have to be applied to *something*.
- The type of the field `exhaustive` is $`positive` \vee `negative`$. The same remark holds: disjunction requires both sides as concrete propositions.

This kind of typing is a standard construction of CIC, in which the types
of later fields of a record may refer to the values of earlier fields.
Here it yields the following structural consequence: *the field
`exclusive` cannot be built apart from the fields `positive` and
`negative`*, since the very *type* of this field mentions both. The same
holds for `exhaustive`.

In substance, this means that mutual exclusivity and joint exhaustiveness
are not independent statements existing by themselves and then
“attached” to the two sides. They are *statements about the two sides*,
and without the sides they have no subject matter. Structurally this is
the same connection as that between “$x$ is greater than $y$” and $x, y$
themselves: without $x$ and $y$ a statement of comparison has no meaning.
In the record `Distinction` this connection is fixed in the type system
itself, not in comments and not in informal explanations.

### The Constructor `mkDistinction` as a Single Act

The application of the constructor `mkDistinction` is a formal *single
act* of building an instance. The signature of the constructor is:

$$
`mkDistinction` :
  \forall (p n : Prop),\;
  \neg(p \wedge n) \;\to\; (p \vee n) \;\to\; `Distinction`.
$$

The constructor takes *four* arguments, one after another, and returns an
instance of the type `Distinction`. An application of the constructor to
three arguments is not yet an instance; it is a *partially applied*
function waiting for the fourth argument. Only when all four arguments
are supplied is a ready `Distinction` obtained.

This means the following. The type system *can* contain a partially
applied constructor—for example, the expression `mkDistinction P`—but
this is *not* an instance of `Distinction`: it is a well-formed term of a
function type, waiting for the remaining arguments. What the type system
does *not* contain is an *object of the type* `Distinction` in which only
the positive side would be specified while the negative side, `exclusive`,
and `exhaustive` would not. Either there is a completed instance of
`Distinction` with all four components supplied and consistent in their
types, or there is an uncompleted application of the constructor—a
function, not an instance. An intermediate object “`Distinction` with
only one side” does not occur in CIC.

*A completed instance of `Distinction` is the formal expression of what
§ 5.1 called distinction in a single act. It is not an object with one
side to which the second is added later, but an instance for which all
four components are integrally required at once.*

It is worth stressing that the *syntactic* order of the constructor’s
arguments ($p$ before $n$, both before `exclusive`, and so on) is a
feature of the notation. Structurally, all four components are *equally
necessary* for a completed instance; the syntactic order in which they
are listed is not an ontological order in which the sides appear.

### The Case `distinction_of P`: One Proposition, Both Sides

In the record `Distinction` the sides $`positive`$ and
$`negative`$ are two independent fields. In principle they can be
specified by entirely different propositions, provided that `exclusive`
and `exhaustive` can be proved. But Rocq has a construction that, from an
arbitrary proposition $P$, builds the corresponding distinction *in one
action*:

```coq
Definition distinction_of (P : Prop) : Distinction :=
  mkDistinction
    P                               (* positive   *)
    (~ P)                           (* negative   *)
    (fun H => match H with          (* exclusive  *)
              | conj p np => np p
              end)
    (classic P).                    (* exhaustive *)
```

In this case—the most telling one for the topic of co-definition—all four
fields are produced from *one* argument $P$:

- The field `positive` is $P$.
- The field `negative` is $\neg P$—not as a “second object” that we introduced in some separate way, but as the syntactic inversion of the same $P$, of type Prop, through the logical connective $\neg$.
- The field `exclusive` is a *constructive* proof of the incompatibility of $P$ and $\neg P$—the lambda expression $\lambda H.\; \mathrm{match}\; H\; \mathrm{with}\; \langle p, np \rangle \Rightarrow np  p$, which works for any $P$ without appeal to any axioms.
- The field `exhaustive` is an application of the classical axiom `classic`: $P \vee \neg P$. This axiom is the subject of a separate discussion, to which the present work will return in a later chapter on the axiomatic layer.

What `distinction_of P` shows: to build the *canonical* distinction with
respect to $P$, one needs one proposition $P$ *plus* the principle $L3$
adopted in ToS, formally represented as the axiom `classic`. The negative
side $\neg P$ and the proof of incompatibility `exclusive` are built
without additional substantive data—directly from the arrangement of the
logical connectives. Joint exhaustiveness `exhaustive`, by contrast, is
*not* built constructively: it requires $L3$, that is, an appeal to
`classic`. The exact formula is therefore this: *one proposition $P$
specifies both sides and their incompatibility; their joint
exhaustiveness is added by the principle $L3$*.

In other words, the act of distinction formalized in `Distinction` is
launched *from one* proposition—but contains *from the very start* both
sides and both links between them. Not “there is $P$, and then we shall
add $\neg P$ and the links,” but rather: “there *is* $P$, and this means
that there are $P$, $\neg P$, their incompatibility, and their
exhaustiveness—one structural whole, described by an instance of the type
`Distinction`.”

### What “Co-Definition Is Built into the Type” Means

Summing up what has been said, we obtain the following.

In typed programming languages and in systems of formal inference such as
Rocq, what *cannot be built* is what *is not there* in the sense of the
formal system. Not “exists, but may not be built”; rather, it *does not
exist as an object of the given type*. This is neither a moral injunction nor a
methodological recommendation—it is a structural property of the type.

Applied to `Distinction`, this means: *an instance of this type with one
side and without the other does not exist*. It does not exist in Rocq,
does not exist in CIC, does not exist in any system that implements a
record with dependent fields. And this is a fact about the *type*, not an
empirical fact checkable by enumeration: the very form of `Record` with
such field types does not admit it.

*The co-definition of the positive and the negative is not an additional
property that `Distinction` *possesses*; it is the arrangement that
`Distinction` *is*.*

Between these formulations there is an essential difference. A
*property* of an object is a statement that can be proved or refuted for
the object. An *arrangement* is that without which the object ceases to
be itself. If co-definition of the sides were removed from `Distinction`
(say, by allowing instances with one side), the result would be *another
type*, not `Distinction` with fewer properties—a type that ToS does not
use, because it does not express the structure of the act of
distinction.

Having fixed what is written in the type, let us now turn to the reverse
side of the same observation: what exactly is *impossible* to build if
one tries to do without one of the sides or without one of the links.

## The Impossibility of a One-Sided `Distinction`

### From Positive Content to What Is Ruled Out

§ 5.2 fixed the *positive* content of co-definition: the record
`Distinction` is arranged so that its four fields are specified
*simultaneously*, the types of the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive`
depend on `positive` and `negative`, and the constructor `mkDistinction`
is a single application that takes all four arguments at once. This is
what is written in the type.

Let us now consider the *reverse* side of the same observation: what is
*impossible* to build if one tries to do without one of the sides or
without one of the links between them. This analysis introduces no new
structures; it only *unfolds* the consequences of what was fixed in
§ 5.2—but it is precisely through the analysis of what cannot be done
that it becomes especially clear to what extent co-definition *is* the
mode of existence of `Distinction` and *not* an additional property of
it.

### What “Impossible” Means in a Typed System

In colloquial speech, “impossible to build” may sound like a rhetorical
turn—as if no one has built it yet, or the construction is too
complicated. In the type system of CIC it is a precise technical and
theoretical statement.

When we say that *an instance of `Distinction` cannot be built without
the negative side*, we mean the following: *there exists no expression*
in the language of CIC that would have the type `Distinction` while doing
without a specified value of the field `negative`. It exists neither as a
program, nor as a proof, nor as any other form of term: type checking in
any correct implementation of CIC, including Rocq, will reject any such
expression as syntactically ill-formed.

*Impossibility in a type system is structural, not empirical,
impossibility. What does not have the correct type does not exist as an
object of the given type—regardless of effort, time, and ingenuity.*

This is an important point. In Chapter 3 the laws $L1$–$L5$ were derived
as *theorems* about an arbitrary `Distinction`—statements that *hold* on
every instance. In § 5.3 of the present chapter we pass to another kind
of statement: statements *about the very existence of instances*. These
statements work at the level of the *conditions of possibility* of being
an instance of this type, not at the level of the *properties* of
`Distinction`. Accordingly, they are established by a direct structural
analysis of the type, not by deriving a theorem.

### Impossibility without the Negative Side

Suppose we want to build an instance of `Distinction` having only the
positive side—some proposition $P$—and without introducing the negative
one. Structurally this means supplying the constructor `mkDistinction`
with one argument $P$ and trying to obtain an instance.

Supplying `mkDistinction P` yields not a `Distinction` but a *partially
applied function* waiting for the remaining arguments. To pass from this
function to an instance, one must supply it with a second proposition—the
value of the field `negative`. Without this proposition the constructor
cannot be fully applied, and no instance arises.

One may object: “what if we simply skip the field `negative`—for
example, leave it unfilled?” A typed system has no such action.
`negative` is declared as a field of type Prop; at the moment the
constructor is applied, this field *must* receive a concrete value of
type Prop. The words “leave unfilled” have no formal counterpart: either
the field has a value, or the constructor has not been applied.

One may object differently: “what if we set `negative` equal to
`True`—that is, supply an identically true statement as the negative
side?” This case is substantively instructive. The constructor
`mkDistinction`—we shall come to this in a moment—also requires proofs
of `exclusive` and `exhaustive`, with types that mention both sides. If
`positive` is $P$ and `negative` is `True`, then `exclusive` must have
the type $\neg (P \wedge `True`)$,
which is equivalent to $\neg P$. This is no longer a statement about the
arrangement of a distinction but simply the assertion that $\neg P$ is
true—which in the general case is false. Consequently, supplying `True`
as the negative side does not build a distinction for an arbitrary $P$;
it works only when $\neg P$ holds.

In substance this means: if we try to bypass the “real” negation—$\neg P$—
and slip some other statement into the role of the negative side, then
either the record is not built (the types of `exclusive` and `exhaustive`
are not provable), or it is built but *does not express the distinction
with respect to $P$*: it expresses a distinction with respect to
something else. `Distinction` itself is not obtained *in any* sense
without a substantive negative side.

### Impossibility without the Field `exclusive`

Let us pass to the two other fields. The field `exclusive` has the type
$\neg (`positive` \wedge `negative`)$—a statement about the mutual
incompatibility of the sides. What happens if one tries to build the
record without this field?

Structurally: it is impossible, because a record `Distinction` without
this field simply *does not exist* in CIC. But let us consider a more
interesting scenario: imagine that we have *defined* another type—say,
`WeakDistinction`—with three fields instead of four, without `exclusive`.
What do we lose?

By the theorem `L2_from_distinction`[^1], the field `exclusive` in the
record `Distinction` *is identically* the law $L2$ for the given
instance: $\neg (`positive` D \wedge `negative` D)$.
In other words, it is *the field of the record itself* that is the
structural realization of $L2$ on this instance, *not a separate*
statement derived from something else.

Accordingly, removing the field `exclusive` means that on the resulting
structure the law $L2$ ceases to hold *structurally*: the resulting type
admits instances in which the positive and negative sides *can hold
together*.

But if the sides of a distinction can hold together, it is no longer a
distinction. In substance: if $P$ and $\neg P$ can hold at the same
time, there is no *gap* between them that makes $P$ and $\neg P$ *two
sides* rather than one indistinct situation. A record without `exclusive`
describes *co-presence*—a structure in which both propositions are
present without distinct separation—rather than an act of distinction
with two sides. This is not Distinction; it is another, degenerate
construction.

### Impossibility without the Field `exhaustive`

The picture with the field `exhaustive` is symmetric. Its type is
$`positive` \vee `negative`$. By the theorem
`L3_from_distinction`[^2],
this field identically realizes $L3$ on the instance.

Removing the field `exhaustive` yields a type on which $L3$ does not hold
structurally: situations become possible in which *neither* the positive
*nor* the negative side holds—a “third” appears between them, a kind of
gap covered by neither side.

In substance: if neither $P$ nor $\neg P$ holds, then $P$ has *not yet
been singled out* as a determinate statement—neither in its affirmation
nor in its negation. This is the pre-formal indistinctness analyzed in § 2.1:
the situation *before* the act of distinction. A record without
`exhaustive` describes the *absence* of an act of distinction, not the
act itself; it describes a *splitting in two* without full coverage, not
a division with a boundary.

Combining the two preceding subsections: `exclusive` rules out the
*overlap* of the sides, and `exhaustive` rules out a *gap* between them.
Without both of these exclusions, the positive and negative sides are
simply two propositions that can do anything at all with each other—not
two sides of one distinction.

### The Lemma `co_constitution` and the Exact Formal Support of Co-Definition

The ToS Rocq repository[^3] contains a lemma whose name refers to
co-definition:

```coq
Lemma co_constitution : forall P : Prop,
  (P -> exists Q, Q = ~P) /\ ((~P) -> exists Q, Q = P).
```

Precision is required here as to *what exactly* this lemma proves. Its
proof in the repository—`split; intro H; eexists; reflexivity`—is
trivial, and this triviality is telling: the premises $P$ and $\neg P$
are in fact *not used* in it. The simpler statements
$\forall P,\ \exists Q,\ Q = \neg P$ and
$\forall P,\ \exists Q,\ Q = P$ are provable just as well. Therefore
`co_constitution` is *not* a strong proof of co-definition in the sense of
a dependence of the truth-values or the meaningfulness of the sides. Its
content is more modest: as soon as a proposition $P$ is given as an
object of the language, the expression $\neg P$ is already syntactically
available as a proposition. This is a *minimal syntactic trace* of
co-definition, not a full formalization of the chapter’s central thesis.

The exact formal support for the chapter’s thesis is given by *other*
statements—about the connection of the sides within a full instance of
`Distinction`. In any `Distinction` the negative side is logically
equivalent to the negation of the positive one, and vice versa:

`negative D <-> ~ positive D`  and  `positive D <-> ~ negative D`.

These equivalences are proved *from the fields* of the record: the
direction `negative D -> ~ positive D` follows from the field `exclusive`
(the incompatibility of the sides), and the converse
`~ positive D -> negative D` from the field `exhaustive`
(exhaustiveness). It is they that directly express what the chapter says:
the sides are not specified syntactically one through the other, but in a
full distinction they are logically co-defined through two rules,
`exclusive` and `exhaustive`.[^4]

It is essential that these equivalences are precisely logical
*equivalences* ($\leftrightarrow$), *not* syntactic equalities of
propositions. Formally we obtain `negative D <-> ~ positive D` but *not*
`negative D = ~ positive D`: the passage from $P \leftrightarrow Q$ to
$P = Q$ would require a separate principle (propositional
extensionality), which is not adopted in the basic axioms of ToS. This
distinction is important: the sides are co-defined logically but not
identified syntactically.

### Co-Definition and the Five Laws

Summing up the three preceding subsections, we obtain:

- Without the negative side an instance of `Distinction` is not built: the very types of the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive` require mentioning it.
- Without the field `exclusive`, $L2$ is structurally lost, and the record describes co-presence, not distinction.
- Without the field `exhaustive`, $L3$ is structurally lost, and the record describes a splitting with a gap, not distinction.

All three “withouts” lead to *another* structure that does not express
the act of distinction, not to a weakened `Distinction`. The
co-definition of the sides is *interlocked* with the very structural laws
through which `Distinction` is what it is; it is not one of the
properties of `Distinction` that could be isolated and carefully
weakened.

The following observation is especially important, and precision is
required here as to what the formalization fixes. In the file
`Distinction.v` the single theorem `five_properties_of_distinction`
asserts that for every $D : `Distinction`$ *four* properties of
distinction hold: stability ($L1$), exclusivity ($L2$), exhaustiveness
($L3$), and self-grounding ($L4$). The fifth law $L5$ does not enter this
theorem: $L5$ concerns the hierarchy of *levels* and is not a property of
an individual instance $D$. It is added in the file
`LawsFromDistinction.v`: the unifying theorem
`five_laws_from_distinction` brings in the hierarchical layer there, and
$L5$ is represented in it by the irreflexivity of the base level and does
not depend on the particular $D$ (as analyzed in Chapter 3).[^5]

This observation has an essential meaning in light of the present
section: if co-definition were not built into the structure, one could
build a `Distinction` on which some of the properties hold and others do
not. But precisely because co-definition is *inseparable* from the
arrangement of the record, any successful construction of an instance
*immediately gives* all four structural properties $L1$–$L4$; the
hierarchical $L5$ is secured by a separate layer of the formalization.

*The co-definition of the sides and the holding of the structural
properties $L1$–$L4$ are one structural circumstance considered from two
sides—from the side of the sides and from the side of the laws—not two
independent properties of `Distinction`.*

### What the Impossibility Shows

Let us pass from listing the impossibilities to answering the question:
*what does* this impossibility *show* as a whole, as a philosophical
observation about the work of ToS?

The impossibility of a one-sided `Distinction` means that *there is no
substantive stage* at which the act of distinction would have only one
side without the other. In particular:

- There is no stage “there is a positive side, but the negative one has not yet arisen.” This stage—grammatically formulable—is not structurally realized.
- There is no stage “there are two sides, but they are not yet separated.” This stage is impossible as well: the separation is the field `exclusive` itself, without which there are no two *sides*, only two arbitrary propositions.
- There is no stage “there are two sides, separated, but with something else between them.” This stage is excluded by the field `exhaustive`: there is no third.

In other words, in ToS one cannot describe a “partial” distinction in
which some part of the structure is present while another has not yet
arrived. Either the act of distinction is there, and then all four fields
and all five structural consequences are there, or it is not, and then we
have the pre-formal indistinctness to which `Distinction` as a type does not
apply.

This conclusion has an important methodological consequence, to which we
turn in § 5.4: the operation of negation in ToS does not work on
statements “taken separately.” It works only on statements already
involved in an act of distinction—that is, on the fields `positive` and
`negative` of some instance of `Distinction`. This means that negation is
an *aspect of the arrangement* that gives designations the status of
meaningful propositions, not an *operation applied afterward*.

Having fixed that doing without one of the sides or without one of the
links is structurally impossible, let us pass to a detailed discussion of
the operation of negation itself—to the distinction between its two
roles: the classical operation, acting on ready-made statements, and the
aspect of the primary act, which makes these statements possible.

## Negation: Operation or Aspect

### Posing the Distinction

The preceding sections of the present chapter showed that the positive
and negative sides of the act of distinction are co-defined in one
structural act. Let us now take the next step and consider what this
co-definition means for the very *concept* of negation. In ordinary
dealings with logic, negation is understood in *one* way—as an operation
that turns one statement into another. In ToS it turns out that negation
has *two* distinct statuses, which are not distinguished in the standard
setting but work ontologically at different levels.

These two statuses can be designated as follows:

1. **Negation as an operation**—an action that is applied to an already-given proposition and returns a new proposition.
2. **Negation as an aspect**—one of the two sides of the act of distinction, co-arising with the other side rather than obtained by applying an action.

*The claim developed in the present section: both statuses work in ToS,
but *at different levels*. The operation is secondary; the aspect is
primary. Between them there is a structural relation, not a
contradiction.*

Let us unfold both statuses in turn.

### The Classical Operation $\neg : Prop \to Prop$

In classical and intuitionistic logic, as well as in their implementation
in CIC, negation is a *syntactic operation*. Formally:

$$
\neg : Prop \to Prop, \quad \neg P \;\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\; P \to \bot.
$$

That is, the *negation* of a proposition $P$ is the statement
“$P$ implies falsehood.” Precision in the sense of Curry–Howard matters
here: a proof of $\neg P$ is a *function* that builds an element of the
empty type $\bot$ from any supposed proof of $P$, that is, derives a
contradiction. The formula $\neg P$ is the object-level statement
$P \to \bot$ inside CIC, not the meta-statement
“$P$ has no proof”: it is important not to conflate these two readings—
$\neg P$ speaks of the *presence of a function* $P \to \bot$, not of the
absence of anything. The definition is given through the arrow and the
type $\bot$ (the empty type, an identically false proposition).

This operation is worked with as follows. First some proposition $P$ is
given—for example, “the natural number $n$ is prime” (for some
particular $n$). Then $\neg$ is applied to this proposition, and a new
proposition $\neg P$ is obtained—“$n$ is not prime.” A proof that a
particular $n$—for example, $n = 4$—is not prime is the construction of a
term of type $\neg P$, that is, the construction of a function from $P$
to $\bot$—that is, of a way to derive a contradiction from the assumption
“$4$ is prime” (for example, by presenting the factorization
$4 = 2 \cdot 2$, which contradicts the definition of primality).

In this setting negation is:

- a *two-place link*: it relates *two* propositions—the original $P$ and the derived $\neg P$;
- *asymmetric*: $P$ is there *before* $\neg P$—precisely because $\neg P$ is obtained by *application* to $P$;
- *definable*: $\neg P$ is given by a formula through $\to$ and $\bot$, and requires no additional structure beyond the apparatus already available in CIC.

This operation is correct, it works, and it is used in the ToS repository
at every step. In particular, in the expression `distinction_of P` the
negative side is specified precisely as $\neg P$—an application of the
classical operation to $P$. ToS has no dispute with classical negation.

### Negation as a Role in the Record `Distinction`

But alongside the operation another status of negation is at work, one
fixed in the record `Distinction` itself. Let us consider the signature
of the record once more:

```coq
Record Distinction := mkDistinction {
  positive   : Prop;
  negative   : Prop;
  exclusive  : ~ (positive /\ negative);
  exhaustive : positive \/ negative
}.
```

In this signature the field `negative` has type Prop—that is, it is
simply a proposition, alongside the field `positive`. The field
`negative` is *not defined* as $\neg `positive`$. The signature contains
no equation of the form:

$$
`negative` \;=\; \neg `positive`.
$$

In principle, when a particular instance of `Distinction` is built, the
fields `positive` and `negative` may be entirely arbitrary
propositions—provided that proofs of `exclusive` and `exhaustive` can be
presented.

The construction `distinction_of P` is *one particular way* of building a
`Distinction`—a way in which the negative side is specified precisely as
$\neg P$. But the record `Distinction` *by itself* does not require
`negative` to be the result of applying $\neg$ to `positive`. The record
requires only that the two sides be *mutually exclusive and jointly
exhaustive*—conditions that can be satisfied in different ways.

*In the record `Distinction` the field `negative` is a *position*
syntactically independent of the field `positive`. The record specifies
the role; the way the role is filled is a separate question, settled upon
actualization, not by the record itself.*

### The Syntactic and the Logical Levels

The observation just made requires an essential clarification, without
which it may be understood too strongly. The clarification concerns two
different levels at which the relation between `positive` and `negative`
works.

**The level of syntax.** 
At the level of the syntax of CIC, the fields `positive` and `negative`
in the record `Distinction` are independent. The signature contains no
equation linking them. When a particular instance is built, *any*
proposition may be supplied for `negative`—provided that proofs of
`exclusive` and `exhaustive` can be presented. The record does not
*write* “`negative` is the negation of `positive`”; it *leaves open* what
`negative` will be in each particular case.

**The level of logic.** 
At the level of logical content, matters stand differently. From the
proofs of `exclusive` and `exhaustive`—that is, from the very
requirements of mutual exclusivity and joint exhaustiveness—it follows
that `negative` and $\neg `positive`$ are *logically equivalent*.

This statement is easy to check structurally. If `exclusive` asserts
$\neg (`positive` \wedge `negative`)$, and `exhaustive` asserts
$`positive` \vee `negative`$, then taken together they are *equivalent*
to the statement $`negative`
\leftrightarrow \neg `positive`$:

- From `negative` follows $\neg `positive`$—this is a reformulation of `exclusive` (if `positive`, then not `negative`, and contrapositively).
- From $\neg `positive`$ follows `negative`—this is a direct application of `exhaustive`: one of the sides must hold, and if not the positive one, then the negative one.

In other words, *any* proposition that, together with `positive`,
satisfies `exclusive` and `exhaustive` turns out to be logically
equivalent to $\neg `positive`$. In content, `negative` is *always* the
negation of `positive`, even if syntactically it is written differently.

**What exactly the record shows.** 
The observation that the record `Distinction` makes does not consist in
`negative` differing in content from $\neg `positive`$. In content they
coincide, and this coincidence is a direct consequence of the
requirements of mutual exclusivity and joint exhaustiveness. The
observation lies elsewhere: the *role* “negative side” is specified in
the record *independently* of the *operation* $\neg$. The record says:
“there are two positions satisfying such-and-such conditions.” The
operation $\neg$ is *one of the ways* to specify the contents of the
second position starting from the contents of the first—and in the case
of `distinction_of` this is exactly how matters stand. But the record
does *not define* the second position through the operation; the record
defines the second position through its *structural role* in the act of
distinction.

*Syntactically, `negative` is independent of $\neg `positive`$;
logically, it is equivalent to it. The record `Distinction` fixes the
syntactic level: the role “negative side” is introduced independently of
the operation $\neg$, although in content the two coincide in every
instance.*

### What This Distinction Is Needed For

A natural question arises: if at the logical level `negative` and
$\neg `positive`$ coincide, does their syntactic independence matter?
Could one simply accept that `negative` *is* $\neg `positive`$ and
simplify the record?

It does matter. The distinction between the syntactic and the logical
levels fixes that the *concept* of the negative side is not *derived*
from the concept of the positive side by applying an operation. This is
an ontological claim about the status of the two sides in the act of
distinction, not a technical remark about the syntax of CIC.

**First structural consequence.** 
If `negative` were defined syntactically as $\neg `positive`$,
the negative side would be a *derivative* construction over the positive
one—what results when an operation has been applied to `positive`. This
is precisely the stance of the separation of the ready-made analyzed in
§ 5.1: first $A$, then $\neg A$ as the result of an action.

The record `Distinction` leaves both sides in *equal* syntactic status:
neither is “the result of an operation on the other.” This agrees with
the stance of distinction in a single act: both sides co-arise as sides
of a structure, not as an object and its derivative.

**Second structural consequence.** 
In the $E/R/R$ analysis (“Connection with E/R/R: The Level of the Schema
and the Level of the Instance”), the field names `positive` and
`negative` at the level of the record schema are role slots. If
`negative` depended syntactically on `positive`, the *slot* “negative
side” would conceptually reduce to an *operation* on the contents of the
slot “positive side.” This would mix the level of role slots with the
level of their filling: a slot is the name of a position, whereas an
operation works with the contents of positions. The syntactic
independence of the fields makes it possible to keep this separation
clean.

**Third structural consequence.** 
Other structures in ToS are possible—not `Distinction`—in which a
similar picture, “two sides co-arising as sides of an arrangement,” is
found, but in which there is *no* operation through which one side would
be expressed syntactically through the other. The record `Distinction`,
with the syntactic independence of its fields, serves as a model for such
structures; if `Distinction` were written with `negative` $=$
$\neg `positive`$, it would be a *degenerate* case, not representative
of the general pattern.

### Connection with E/R/R: The Level of the Schema and the Level of the Instance

The observation just made receives an exact formulation in terms of the
$E/R/R$ analysis unfolded in Chapter 4. Here, however, it is necessary to
separate explicitly *two levels of reading* the record—otherwise an
apparent contradiction with the formalization will arise.

The point is that the words `positive` and `negative` designate *two
different things*: the *name of a field* in the record schema and the
*value of this field* in a particular instance. At the level of the
schema of the record `Distinction` as a type, the field names `positive`
and `negative` are *role slots*, the positions “positive side” and
“negative side.” At the level of a particular instance
$D : `Distinction`$, the values `positive` $D$ and `negative` $D$ are
concrete propositions, and it is they that are the *elements* of the
E/R/R analysis: the file `ERRFromDistinction.v` defines the list of
elements as `err_elements D = [ positive D; negative D ]`. Accordingly,
the resulting labeling has two levels:

| Component | Level | E/R/R category |
|---|---|---|
| `positive`, `negative` (field names of the schema) | record schema | Roles (slots) |
| `positive` $D$, `negative` $D$ (values) | instance $D$ | Elements |
| `exclusive` $D$, `exhaustive` $D$ | instance $D$ | Rules |

Both readings are legitimate and do not contradict each other: they
belong to different levels. When the present chapter says that
`positive` and `negative` are *roles*, it speaks of the level of the
schema: the name of the position (“positive side,” “negative side”) is
specified by the record `Distinction` itself, whereas the filling of the
position—a concrete proposition—appears upon actualization, and at the
level of the instance this filling is an element.

In the labeling at the level of the schema, the negative side is the
*second position in one structure*, symmetric to the first, and not the
“opposite” of the positive side taken as a ready-made object. Both
positions are on an equal footing: neither has ontological precedence
over the other. The symmetry of the record is visible directly from its
signature: the fields `positive` and `negative` are both declared in the
same way (of type Prop), with no indication that one is “original” and
the other “obtained from the original.”

From the standpoint of the generative order (Chapter 4): first the
*rules* are specified (the type `Distinction`, the requirements
`exclusive` and `exhaustive`), *then* the *roles* are distributed
(`positive` and `negative` as positions), and only *after that* are
*elements* selected for the roles (concrete propositions upon
actualization). Negation as an operation works at the level of
Elements—when there is already a proposition, another one is matched to
it through the syntactic $\neg$. Negation as an aspect works at the level
of Roles—a position is specified into which a proposition can then be
placed.

### The Relation of Operation and Aspect across the Levels of E/R/R

It is essential that these two statuses of negation—operation and
aspect—neither contradict nor compete with each other. They work at
different levels.

**The level of Elements (negation as an operation).** 
At this level there are concrete propositions—$P$, $Q$, “$n$ is prime,”
“$n$ is even,” “$a$ divides $b$,” and so on. The operation $\neg$ takes a
proposition and returns another proposition. Classical logic works at
this level, and works correctly. All theorems proving $\neg P$ for a
particular $P$ remain proved.

**The level of Roles (negation as an aspect).** 
At this level there are as yet no concrete propositions—there is the
structure of the act of distinction, in which *two positions* are singled
out. These positions are called “positive side” and “negative side,” but
their filling is a matter of actualization. At this level the negative
side is not the result of applying an operation; it is the *second
position*, coexisting with the first by the structure of the act.

**The connection of the levels.** 
When a particular act of distinction is performed *with respect to* the
proposition $P$ (that is, when `distinction_of P` is applied), the two
levels are combined: the concrete filling of the positions `positive` and
`negative` is given precisely by applying the operation $\neg$ to
$P$. In this particular case operational negation and aspectual negation
*coincide in result*—but not in the structure on which they work.

*Classical negation is not canceled. It is **embedded**
in a more general structure: the operation $\neg$ is one of the ways of
filling the role `negative`, not a definition of what this role is.*

### Consequences for Understanding Operations in the Volume

The distinction between operation and aspect has several important
consequences, to which the present work will return in later parts.

**First: the status of operations as a methodological position.** 
In standard mathematics it is customary to speak of “operations”
(addition, multiplication, negation, differentiation, projection) as if
they were *primary* objects acting on ready-made elements. ToS shows that
every such operation—if considered structurally—has an aspectual double:
what the operation *does* is, at a deeper level, the *disclosure* of an
aspect already built into the structure. Negation is the simplest and
most telling case here, since it is built into the very record of the act
of distinction.

**Second: the primacy of structure over procedure.** 
In the formalism of CIC an instance of `Distinction` exists as a *whole*
with four fields—not as the result of a step-by-step application of
operations. This agrees with the general ontological position of ToS
analyzed in Chapter 4: *Rules are primary* with respect to Roles, and
Roles with respect to Elements (the generative order). Structure
specifies the possibilities of procedures; procedures do not create
structure.

**Third: a limit on “operationalization.”** 
Not every structure can be adequately described by listing operations on
its elements. If a structure contains *co-defined* aspects—like
`positive` and `negative` in `Distinction`—then an attempt to present
these aspects as “an object and the result of an operation on it”
distorts the structure: the co-arising, inseparable from the arrangement
itself, is missed.

The distinction between operation and aspect is a particular case of a
more general regularity: what appears in formal work as a procedure is
often the *unfolding* of an already-present structure. This line will run
through the entire present work. In particular, in Part III (number
systems) and in Part XI (group structures), similar observations will be
applied to such operations as addition and the inverse element of a
group.

Having fixed the structural distinction between operational and
aspectual negation, let us consider how the co-definition of the positive
and the negative—as a structural fact of the act of distinction—is found
not only in the formal record `Distinction` but also in several different
traditions of philosophical and scientific thought, and what the
*converging testimony* of these independent findings consists in.

## Parallels and Anticipations

### Converging Parallels

The intuition of the co-definition of sides—the idea that opposites arise
as sides of one arrangement, rather than being independent entities
between which a relation is afterward established—is not new. It shows
through in several traditions of thought remote from one another in time,
language, and cultural context: in structural linguistics, in Hegelian
dialectics, in classical Chinese philosophy, in early Buddhism. These
traditions did not communicate with one another; they differ in method,
vocabulary, and subject matter. And yet each of them—by different means
and in different terms—arrives at one and the same structural
observation.

We regard this similarity of remote traditions as a *heuristic
parallel*, not as evidential support for ToS: the agreement of independent
perspectives raises the *plausibility* that the observation captures
something structural, but by itself it *proves* nothing. The formal force
of the chapter’s thesis lies in the arrangement of the record
`Distinction` analyzed in the preceding sections, not in these
parallels.

The present work adds to these parallels something of a different
nature: the *formal* tracing of co-definition in the record
`Distinction`. The dependent types of the fields `exclusive` and
`exhaustive`, which require both sides for their very meaningfulness,
give co-definition an exact form—independently of how it was seen by
Saussure, by Hegel, or in Eastern texts. This formal result is
conveniently understood precisely against the background of traditions
in which a similar structure was seen in substance but could not be
formalized—for lack of a suitable formal apparatus, not for lack of
effort.

In the present section we consider four traditions: Saussurean
structural linguistics, Jakobson’s structuralism that developed from it,
Hegelian dialectics, and the Eastern traditions (yin-yang and Nāgārjuna’s
dependent origination). Each is considered briefly—just enough to single
out the point at which it echoes co-definition and the point at which its
treatment differs from the formal apparatus of ToS. Let us stress in
advance: these are parallels and anticipations, not testimony in the
evidential sense.

### Saussure: Meaning through Difference

Of all the traditions under consideration, the most exact parallel to
co-definition is found in Ferdinand de Saussure. His *Course in General
Linguistics*[^6] formulates one of the central theses of structural
linguistics—that in language there are only differences.

The substantive content of this thesis is as follows. The meaning of a
sign—a word, a morpheme, any linguistic element—is not a property
inherent in the sign *by itself*. A sign has no meaning in isolation.
The meaning of a sign is its *position* in a system of other signs—that
by which it *differs* from its neighbors, which compete with it for a
role in the same paradigm.

**Saussure’s example.** 
The French word *mouton* and the English *sheep* designate one and the
same animal, the sheep. One might think that these two words have one and
the same meaning. But English has a separate word, *mutton*, for the meat
of the sheep, while French does not: the same *mouton* designates both
the animal and its meat. The meaning of the English *sheep* is *not
equal* to the meaning of the French *mouton*—precisely because *sheep*
stands in a system with *mutton*, whereas *mouton* stands in a system
where there is no such opposition. The meaning of *sheep* is constituted
by its *difference* from *mutton*; without this difference the meaning
would be different.

**Structural content.** 
The main thing in Saussure’s position is the thesis of the *relational*
nature of meaning, not the thesis of the arbitrariness of the sign (that
is another, separate line). A sign has no content apart from the system;
the content of a sign *is its role* in a system of differences, and this
role cannot be indicated without indicating other roles.

This is structural content directly parallel to what the record
`Distinction` shows: the positive side has no content outside the act of
distinction; its content is *its role* in the arrangement, and this role
is not singled out without indicating the negative side.

**Point of difference.** 
Here is where Saussure’s finding differs from the formal finding.
Saussure works at the level of a *general observation* about the
structure of meaning; his apparatus is descriptive, not formal. He has no
*record* that fixes co-definition as a concrete structural arrangement.
The thesis “in language there are only differences” remains a statement
about language in general, not a *formalizable* construction.

The record `Distinction` gives co-definition what the Saussurean
apparatus does not give: an *exact form*. Not a general thesis but a
concrete type in CIC, on which one can work formally—prove theorems,
derive consequences, rely on it in further constructions. This does not
mean that the formal result “goes further” than Saussure’s—it means that
one and the same structure is seen from two sides: from the side of the
analysis of meaning and from the side of formal arrangement. Both
readings are heuristically parallel and point to one and the same
structural motif.

### Jakobson’s Structuralism: Binary Oppositions

The line set by Saussure was developed in the first half of the
twentieth century in the works of Roman Jakobson and the Prague
Linguistic Circle. Jakobson showed that in phonology the values of
phonemes are expressed through *binary oppositions*: the sound “b” in
Russian differs from “p” by one feature—voicing (in everything else they
are the same)—and the whole phonological system of a language can be
described as the intersection of several such oppositions.

Structurally, this is the same picture as co-definition in
`Distinction`: the two sides of an opposition have meaning only as sides
of the opposition, not as independent entities—“voicing” has content
only in a pair with “voicelessness.” Jakobson’s material essentially
illustrates the same relational arrangement as Saussure’s, only on
phonological material; we therefore confine ourselves to this compact
indication. The point of difference from ToS is the same as with
Saussure: Jakobson works within the subject matter of linguistics,
whereas the record `Distinction` is not tied to language and concerns the
very structure of singling out a determinacy in any domain.

### Hegel: Dialectical Co-Arising

A different parallel to co-definition—more general, but also more
nebulous—shows through in Hegel. In the *Science of Logic*[^7] Hegel unfolds what is customarily called the *dialectical
movement of concepts*. A caveat is needed here. In popular reception,
Hegelian dialectics is often described through the scheme
thesis–antithesis–synthesis; but as a brief description of his own
method this scheme is disputable and simplifies Hegelian logic (Hegel
himself did not formulate this triad in this form). What matters for us
is not this scheme but a deeper motif—the *mutual determinacy of
opposites*; it is this motif that we single out in the Hegelian material.

With respect to the topic that interests us, the following observation of
Hegel’s is essential. Pure *being* (Sein) and pure *nothing* (Nichts),
taken in their absolute indeterminacy, turn out in Hegel to be identical:
without any inner determinations, “something is” and “there is nothing”
are equally empty in content. Their difference appears only in
*becoming* (Werden)—the movement from one to the other, in which each
receives determinacy *through* its opposite. Being is not there before
nothing; nothing is not there before being; both co-arise in a single
movement of becoming.

**Structural content.** 
Hegel’s idea, in its abstract form, resonates directly with
co-definition: opposites do *not* exist as ready-made objects between
which a relation is afterward established. They co-arise in one movement;
their identity is mutual determination through opposition.

**First point of difference: time.** 
Hegelian co-arising unfolds in *time*—in the *movement* of becoming. This
is a temporal structure. `Distinction` in ToS does not unfold in time:
the four fields are integrally required for one instance, built by a
single application of the constructor
`mkDistinction`. Co-definition
in ToS is structurally instantaneous, not processual.

This difference is essential. In Hegel, opposites can be thought of as
“arising from each other” in the sense of a movement of thought; in ToS,
opposites are given in a single step, and there is no “movement” in which
one precedes the other. The Hegelian picture admits an interpretation in
which temporal sequence matters; ToS excludes such an interpretation—the
structure of the act of distinction has no inner sequence.

**Second point of difference: sublation.** 
In Hegel, the movement of thesis and antithesis concludes in *sublation*
(Aufhebung)—a special transition in which the opposites are preserved and
at the same time overcome in a synthesis. In ToS there is no sublation at
all. The positive and negative sides of the act of distinction remain two
sides; they do not merge into a synthesis and do not form a third entity
above themselves. `Distinction` has exactly two sides, and the law $L3$
excludes a “third.”

**Third point of difference: formalization.** 
Hegelian dialectics, for all its influence, has never been formalized.
Attempts to formalize it—from Marx to contemporary analytic
treatments—remain interpretations that do not turn Hegelian categories
into a working formal apparatus. `Distinction` is a concrete record with
concrete rules; Hegelian co-arising remains a substantive intuition, not
a formal structure.

To sum up: the structure of co-definition is *visible* in Hegel, and
Hegel articulates it quite deeply—but he articulates it within a frame in
which time, sublation, and an unformalizable dialectic are at work. In
ToS the same structure is found within another frame—structural,
atemporal, formal. What Hegel described as a movement of thought turns
out in formal work to be *the arrangement of a structure*—but these are
two different approaches to one and the same structural fact, not an
appropriation of Hegel’s thought by formalism.

### The Eastern Tradition: Metaphorical Anticipations

Let us briefly mention two more traditions in which the intuition of
co-definition is especially distinct, but in a form far from
formalization.

**Yin-yang in classical Chinese philosophy.** 
In the *I Ching*[^8] and in later Daoist thought, the central pair *yin*
and *yang* appears as two sides of one arrangement of the world, not as
two independent principles between which an opposition has been
established. Yin without yang *has no meaning*: darkness without light is
not darkness; the feminine without the masculine is not the feminine;
the passive without the active is not the passive. Each is determined
*through* the other and does not exist apart from it.

This formulation—substantively parallel to co-definition in
`Distinction`—has in the Chinese tradition an *imagistic*, not a formal,
character. It works as a way of *looking* at the world, not as a formal
apparatus in which theorems can be proved.

**Pratītyasamutpāda in Nāgārjuna.** 
Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (second–third century
CE)[^9] unfolds the principle of
*pratītyasamutpāda*—“dependent origination.” According to this
principle, no entity exists *by itself*; everything that exists *arises*
in dependence on other existing things and has no independent identity.
Applied to opposites, this means: neither opposite is by itself; both
co-arise as sides of a mutual dependence.

Here, too, the parallel with co-definition is direct. But here, too,
there is no formalization: pratītyasamutpāda is a general philosophical
thesis about all that exists, not a concrete type in a formal system.

**What is common, what differs.** 
The Eastern traditions—yin-yang and pratītyasamutpāda—see in
co-definition a *general principle of the arrangement of what exists*.
They go beyond concrete oppositions (as in Saussure and Jakobson) and
beyond a concrete movement of concepts (as in Hegel) to a general
ontological position: *nothing exists independently; everything is
determined through another*.

ToS, in the formal part of the present volume, makes no such general
ontological claim. `Distinction` is the formalization of a concrete act of
distinction, not a claim about all that exists. The formula “nothing
exists independently; everything is determined through another” is too
broad for a technically oriented chapter, and we take it *only* as a
heuristic parallel, not as evidential support for ToS. The ontological
position of the present work—which Volume I develops and which shows
through in Chapter 4 on $E/R/R$—echoes the Eastern one in its structure:
the whole system $E/R/R$ works so that the elements of a system receive
their determinacy through Roles, and Roles through Rules; no level is
determined in isolation. The same structural regularity that the Eastern
traditions *see* in the general arrangement of what exists is traced in
ToS by formal means—neither as a borrowing of the Eastern position nor as
a proof from it, but as one more heuristic parallel to the same
structural motif.

### What Formalization Gives

All four traditions—Saussure, Jakobson, Hegel, the Eastern texts—*see*
co-definition. Each describes it by its own means and in its own terms.
Each remains at the level of a substantive intuition, a philosophical
position, or a descriptive model; none carries co-definition through to a
formal record in which it would become a *working instrument*.

Here is what the formalization of ToS adds to this picture.

**First: precision.** 
A formal record makes explicit what remained blurred in the traditions.
Saussure’s co-definition is a general thesis about meaning; co-definition
in Hegel is a movement of concepts; yin-yang is an image. `Distinction`
is a *concrete* structure with four *concrete* fields and a *concrete*
dependence among them. Precision here does not mean narrowing—on the
contrary: the exact form reveals a structure that blurred formulations
concealed.

**Second: checkability.** 
A formal record makes it possible to *prove* statements about
co-definition. The lemmas analyzed in § 5.3 and the theorems derived in
Chapter 3 are formal results that hold in CIC. Saussure’s “in language
there are only differences” is not a theorem and cannot be proved or
refuted; it is a general observation. The statements of ToS about
co-definition are formal theorems checked by Rocq.

**Third: the possibility of unfolding.** 
A formal record makes it possible to *build* further constructions on its
basis. `Distinction` is the foundation on which the laws $L1$–$L5$
(Chapter 3) and the principles $P1$–$P4$ (Chapter 4) stand; on this
basis number systems (Part III), function spaces, group structures, and
much else will be built in later parts. Saussure, Hegel, the Eastern
texts—each stopped at co-definition as an observation *about* the
structure of what exists; ToS makes co-definition a *working instrument*
on which what follows is built.

*The formalization of ToS neither cancels the substantive intuitions of
the traditions nor appropriates them. Co-definition is a structural fact
of the act of distinction, and ToS finds it just as independently as
Saussure found it in the analysis of meaning and Hegel in the analysis of
the movement of concepts. Formal work gives this one and the same
structural fact an exact, checkable, and unfoldable form—and thereby
makes available as an apparatus what without form remained a substantive
observation.*

Having fixed the place of co-definition in the tradition and the nature
of what its formal discovery in `Distinction` adds to it, let us pass to
the concluding substantive observation of the present chapter—an
observation about two methodological stances, whose distinction works
not only in formal work but also in the very approach to knowing.

## Two Methodological Stances

### Returning to the Two Stances

In § 5.1 of the present chapter, the difference between two stances
toward working with opposites was introduced—the separation of the
ready-made and distinction in a single act. This difference was
introduced as a *conceptual* frame within which the formal observations
were then unfolded: about the record `Distinction` (§ 5.2, § 5.3), about
the status of negation (§ 5.4), and about the converging testimonies in
the tradition (§ 5.5).

Now, in the final section of the chapter, the same conceptual pair is
considered from another side: *methodologically*—as a way of *working*
with determinacies in formal activity, not as a thesis about the structure
of the act of distinction. These two stances have concrete consequences
for *how* the work of a mathematician unfolds, and these consequences
deserve to be fixed separately—as a methodological observation that will
apply in all subsequent parts of the present volume, not as a moral
exhortation.

### Distinction in the Mathematician’s Work

The stance of distinction—when work is carried on within the frame “there
is a structure that has two sides co-defining each other”—manifests
itself in mathematical work as follows.

**First: introducing an object together with its complement.** 
When a mathematician introduces a new object—a class, a relation, a
structure—that against which this object is singled out is introduced at
the same time. An even number is introduced as one side of the dichotomy
even/odd, not “by itself”—this dichotomy is exact for the integers and
the natural numbers (for other structures it would have to be stipulated
separately). A prime number is introduced within the classification of
the natural numbers, not in isolation: alongside it stand the composite
numbers—and the special case of one, which is neither prime nor
composite. A group is introduced together with its homomorphisms into
other groups—that is, with that by which this particular group *differs*
from other groups with the same underlying set. A category, as we shall
see in Part VIII, is introduced at once with both aspects—objects and
arrows—which do not exist one without the other.

This simultaneity of introducing an object and its complement is not an
aesthetic feature—it is a structural consequence of the fact that the
determinacy of an object *is specified* by its place among neighboring
determinacies. An isolated definition that does not indicate how the
object differs from its neighbors is a name, not a definition.

**Second: checking roles when working with a structure.** 
In the stance of distinction, working with a structure includes
*checking* that the roles in the structure are filled consistently. These
are checks of the kind “do the elements correspond to their roles?” and
“are the rules that hold the structure together satisfied?”—the very
well-formedness check analyzed in § 4.9.3 of Chapter 4 and connected with
the $E/R/R$ analysis.

In the stance of distinction such checks are natural and regular: when a
structure is specified as an arrangement with roles and rules, it is
natural to look at whether the rules are satisfied. In the stance of the
separation of the ready-made such checks are irregular: if the objects
are “already given,” the question of their consistency with roles does
not arise—roles do not work at all.

**Third: preserving the connection between levels.** 
The generative order (Chapter 4) states: rules specify roles, roles
select elements. This sequence must not be violated. In the stance of
distinction the connection between levels *is preserved*: every concrete
object of work stays together with the rules and roles through which it
was introduced. In the stance of separation the connection *can* be lost:
an object detached from its structural ground loses determinacy—but this
loss is not immediately visible to one who uses the object as
ready-made.

### Separation and the Loss of Connection

The stance of the separation of the ready-made—when work is carried on
within the frame “there are ready-made objects on which we perform
operations”—is not an erroneous stance. It works at its own level, and
the greater part of everyday mathematical work takes place precisely
within it. When one computes a derivative, proves a theorem about limits,
or solves a differential equation, one works with objects as ready-made,
without returning each time to the structural question of how these
objects are constituted.

The problem arises from the *automatism* of this stance, not from its
use—when it is applied to situations in which the structural question is
essential but is not asked, because the working stance does not see it.

**First scenario: paradoxes.** 
[[Russells Paradox|Russell’s paradox]][^10] arises precisely in the
stance of the separation of the ready-made: “the set of all sets that do
not contain themselves” is introduced as a ready-made object, without
checking whether this record is consistent with the structure of levels
($P1$, the hierarchy). In the stance of distinction no such object arises
at all: the record fails the well-formedness check before a paradox could
form.

**Second scenario: the fragmentation of formal domains.** 
If different formal domains—algebra, analysis, topology, category
theory—develop as self-enclosed disciplines, without holding on to the
connection between their foundations, a fragmentation of mathematical
knowledge arises. Each domain becomes an independent system of ready-made
objects; connections between domains are established *later*, through
special constructions—functors, equivalences, translations. In the stance
of distinction these connections are present *from the very start*:
different domains are different actualizations of one structure $E/R/R$,
and their connection is a structural circumstance, not a later addition.

**Third scenario: ontological forgetfulness.** 
The subtlest scenario is when work in the stance of the separation of the
ready-made leads to oblivion of the pre-formal foundations on which formal
work stands. The objects of mathematics begin to seem self-sufficient—as
existing “by themselves,” without reference to the structure of the act
of distinction, to the laws of logic, to the Absolute as a meta-system.
This is precisely the loss of the ontological ground that Volume I
analyzes and that in Volume II receives a counterweight through the
rigorous methodology of the first formalizable, analyzed in Chapter 1.

*Distinction and separation are not two opposed methodologies, one of
which is right and the other not. They are two stances working at
different levels of depth. The separation of the ready-made is the
working stance of everyday formal activity; distinction in a single act
is the structural stance to which one must return whenever everyday work
runs up against foundations.*

### Between the Levels

It is useful to formulate the relation between the two stances still
more precisely. It is a correlation of the levels at which they work, not
a choice between them.

**At the level of Elements**  the stance of the separation of the
ready-made works. Here there are concrete propositions, numbers,
functions, elements of groups, points of spaces. Operations work with
them, theorems are applied to them, they are substituted into formulas.
This is the working surface of mathematics, and on it the stance of
separation is adequate.

**At the level of Roles and Rules**  the stance of distinction in a
single act works. Here the questions concern positions and the rules that
link them, not objects. Here it is essential that the positive and
negative sides are co-defined, that the elements correspond to the roles,
that the rules hold the structure together. At this level the stance of
the separation of the ready-made does not work—because there are no
objects here in the usual sense; there is an *arrangement* in which
objects receive their positions.

**The transition between levels**  occurs when work *with* objects
meets a question *about* objects. When a mathematician computes a
derivative, the work proceeds in the stance of separation. When the
mathematician asks “but what is a derivative?”, the work passes to the
level of Roles—to the question of what role the derivative plays in the
structure of analysis, what rules hold it in place, what place it
occupies in the overall arrangement. Here the stance of distinction
works.

Understood in this way, the pair distinction/separation does not oppose
two ways of doing mathematics; it distinguishes *two levels* at which
mathematics works—levels that in ToS are both preserved, both needed,
and both at work.

With this final methodological observation Chapter 5 is complete.
Chapter 6 of the present part discusses the technical axioms of Rocq on
which work with `Distinction` rests—the classical axiom `classic` and
the axiom `L4_witness`, which implements the mechanism of status transfer
through E/R/R in place of the Axiom of Choice. The theme of co-definition
unfolded in the present chapter will return in later parts each time
structures with pairs of sides are at issue—from duality in categories
(Part VIII) to inverse elements in groups (Part XI).
