Chapter 6 completed the methodological dimension of Part I: it
introduced the main formal nodes of the ToS apparatus—distinction, the
Laws, the Principles, the $E/R/R$ structure, levels, criteria, and the
technical status of the axioms of implementation. Some of these nodes
are already directly machine-checked in Rocq; others serve as an
ontological and methodological guide for the subsequent parts. The
present chapter completes Part I in its *ontological* dimension.

Throughout Chapters 1–6, ToS made a number of positional decisions,
carrying them out through the very arrangement of the apparatus rather
than announcing them separately. Infinity unfolded as a process, not as
an object. The list proved more primary than the set. Determinacy arose
in the act of distinction rather than preexisting it. These decisions
worked, but they were not *gathered* and *explicitly declared* as the
ontological positions of the volume.

The present chapter gathers them as the *ontological positions* to which
ToS returns throughout all the subsequent parts of the volume, and fixes
the *reading program* for these parts. The chapter is ontological: it
clarifies on what ontological grounds the already-built apparatus
stands, rather than introducing new formal apparatus. Some of the nodes
gathered below have direct Rocq anchors; some have partial formal
traces; some are methodological guides that will unfold in later parts.

## Non-Completion and Quantum Superposition

### The Processuality of Ontology

The processuality of ontology is *not* one of the four positions of the
volume (§ 7.3) on a par with the others. It is the background of all the
ontological positions of the volume, the condition on which all four
rest.

The processuality of ontology was in fact already unfolded in Chapter 6,
in the discussion of the principle $P4$ and of non-completion. Here it is
formulated directly, as an ontological position.

**Formulation.** 
*The ontology of ToS is one of processes, not of objects.* What exists
exists as an *unfolding*, not as a ready-made givenness. This applies to
infinity (it is a process of construction, not a completed set), to
status (it exists at a step of a process, not as a permanent property),
and to determinacy in general (it arises in the act of distinction and
does not preexist it).

This position follows from the very arrangement of the act of
distinction with which ToS begins; it is not a technical restriction
adopted for the convenience of formalization. The act of distinction is
an *act*; it is performed. Before it there is neither the distinguished
nor the determinate; with it determinacy sets in. An ontology derived
from an act cannot but be processual: at its foundation lies an act, not
an object.

**Why the processuality of ontology needs a separate declaration.** 
In Chapters 1–6 processuality served as a working principle: concrete
constructions were built on it. But a working principle and a *declared
ontological position* are not the same thing. To declare a position
means to indicate that ToS *deliberately* chooses a process ontology
where classical mathematics assumes an object ontology by default. One
widespread misunderstanding is connected with process ontology—about a
supposed conflict with quantum physics—and it is worth clearing it up at
once.

### Superposition and the Law of Excluded Middle

The process ontology of ToS rests, among other things, on the Law of
Excluded Middle ($L3$). One sometimes hears that this Law comes into
contradiction with quantum physics; it is worth showing briefly that
there is no contradiction.

**A common oversimplification.** 
Since the work of Birkhoff and von Neumann (1936), *quantum logic* has
been known—the logical structure corresponding to the lattice of closed
subspaces of a Hilbert space. This structure is sometimes described in
the words “quantum mechanics abolishes classical logic,” including the
Law of Excluded Middle. Such a description is an oversimplification.
Quantum logic weakens the *distributive law*—$A \wedge (B \vee C) = (A \wedge B) \vee (A
\wedge C)$—rather than the Law of Excluded Middle. Distributivity and
$L3$ are different laws; weakening one is not denying the other.
Moreover, the lattice of quantum logic is *orthocomplemented*: for every
subspace $A$ we have $A \vee A^{\perp} = 1$—that is, the analogue of
excluded middle is *preserved* in quantum logic. What falls is
distributivity, not $L3$.

**$L3$ holds unconditionally.** 
In ToS the Law of Excluded Middle is ontologically derived from $L1$
and $L2$ (Chapter 3): if something is identical to itself ($L1$) and
cannot at once be and not be ($L2$), then between “being $A$” and “not
being $A$” there is structurally no third outcome. $L3$ is a statement
about the *disjunction of outcomes*, and it holds *always*, for any
$A$. That in some situation it is *unknown* whether $A$ or $\neg A$
holds does not shake $L3$ in the least: $L3$ is a law about the
structure of outcomes, not about knowledge. Not knowing which of the two
outcomes holds is not a “third option.”

**Superposition is not a “third outcome.”** 
Quantum superposition—the state of a system *before* measurement—is not
a “third outcome” alongside $A$ and $\neg A$. It is not a statement
competing with “$A$” and “$\neg A$” at all. Superposition is a state in
which the corresponding act of distinction *has not yet been performed*:
the question “$A$ or $\neg A$?” has not yet been posed as an actual
distinction. Measurement *is* an act of distinction; before measurement
there is no outcome—not because there is a “third,” but because the
distinction itself, to whose outcome $L3$ refers, has not yet been made.
When the measurement is performed, the outcome is strictly one of the
possible ones, and $L3$ is *respected* by it.

What does not preexist the act is *the very fact of the distinction
having been made*, not the *outcome* of the disjunction
$A \vee \neg A$ (the disjunction is always true). Superposition neither
violates $L3$ nor competes with it; it is the ontological situation
*before* $L3$ receives the actualized distinction to whose outcome it
applies. There is no conflict with $L3$ here—only a process ontology in
which determinacy sets in with the act.

### $P4$ and Superposition: One Structural Motif in Two Domains

What has been said reveals a *common structural motif*, not an identity
of mathematics and quantum physics. In mathematics the principle $P4$
says: infinity does not preexist as a completed object; it unfolds as a
process. In physics quantum superposition points to a similar structure:
a definite value of an observable need not preexist the act of
measurement as a ready-made fact; it can be actualized in the process of
distinction.

Therefore superposition neither abolishes $L3$ nor competes with it.
$L3$ asserts the completeness of a *completed* distinction:
$A \vee \neg A$. Superposition, for its part, describes a situation in
which the corresponding distinction is not yet completed as the outcome
of a process. This points to the processual character of the
actualization of determinacy; it is no violation of excluded middle.

Let us stress the status of what has been said. It is a structural
analogy, a common motif of processuality, and *not* a proven identity of
the mathematical apparatus and the physical theory. ToS puts forward a
programmatic hypothesis: both processualities—the one ToS discovers in
the foundations of mathematics and the one physics discovers in the
quantum description—are manifestations of a single ontology of the act
of distinction, derived from one first principle, $A = \exists$. Turning
this hypothesis into a formal derivation is the subject of physics.

### The Bridge to Physics

What has been said marks out a bridge that unfolds fully beyond the
present volume.

This volume is devoted to mathematics: it builds the apparatus of ToS and
applies it to number systems, analysis, algebra, and geometry—to
mathematical content. The derivation of physical laws from the act of
distinction is the subject of physics. There the process
ontology, grounded here, unfolds into a program for deriving the laws of
physics: in particular, into a study of the unistochastic structure of
quantum theory as a structure consistent with the arrangement of
distinction[^1].

For the present volume the following is essential: the processuality of
ontology is not confined to mathematics. It is *ontological*, not
narrowly mathematical; and precisely for this reason it is the
*background* of all the ontological positions of the volume rather than
a local principle of one of its parts. In every subsequent part of
the volume where a mathematical object turns out to be a process rather
than a completed givenness, the reader meets this position again. The
full set of the ontological positions of the volume is established in
§ 7.3.

## Sets as a Mode of Consideration of a List

### The Demand for Sufficient Reason for the Existence of a Set

The next ontological position of the volume concerns the relation between
two fundamental constructions—the *list* and the *set*—and which of
them is ontologically primary.

In the classical foundations of mathematics the set is accepted as the
initial concept: a collection of elements existing as a ready-made
object, irrespective of any order or process. Everything else is built
from sets. ToS does not make this initial assumption—and it refrains for
a concrete reason.

**A set requires a ground of gathering.** 
Let us ask: on what ground are the elements of a given set *gathered
together*? The set $\{a, b, c\}$ presents three elements as one
collection—but *what* makes them one collection? In the classical
setting the answer is given in advance: a collection exists insofar as
its elements exist, and gathering requires no separate ground. From the
position of ToS such an answer is insufficient. By the Law of Sufficient
Reason ($L4$), every distinction requires a ground; and to *gather*
elements into one collection is to *distinguish* this collection as one.
Consequently, the existence of a set as one object raises a *demand for
sufficient reason*: it must be indicated on what ground precisely these
elements form one.

**A list raises no such demand.** 
A list is arranged differently. A list is elements unfolding *in the
order* given by the Law of Order ($L5$). A list *unfolds* its elements
one after another rather than *gathering* them into one. This does not
mean that a list has no ground *at all*: by $L4$ everything has a
sufficient reason, and the list is no exception. A list has a
*different* ground from a set: a *rule of unfolding in order*, not an act
of gathering. A list presents its elements as the result of a sequential
process, not as an already-gathered totality, and its sufficient reason
is given by this process-rule.

The difference is fundamental. A set, in order to exist as one object,
requires an answer to the question “on what ground is it gathered?” A
list does not raise this question: its ground is a rule of unfolding,
not gathering.

### The Primacy of the List

From what has been said follows the ontological thesis that forms the
core of this position.

**Thesis.** 
*The list is primary; the set is derived.* A list is the direct
expression of how a process unfolds according to $L5$: an ordered
sequence of elements occupying positions (Chapter 6, § 6.6). The
sufficient reason of a list is given by the rule of its unfolding—by the
very fact that the process unfolds in order, not by an act of gathering;
and the unfolding of a process is a direct consequence of the
arrangement of the act of distinction. The set, by contrast, is a
*derived* construction, not an initial one: it is obtained from a list by
an operation examined in the next subsection.

**Why the list, and not the set.** 
The list is closer to the act of distinction. The act of distinction is
performed—it has a *course*, a direction, an order: first one
distinction, then the next. The list inherits this structure directly:
it is an ordered unfolding. The set, by contrast, is abstracted from
order—and abstraction from order is an additional operation that the act
of distinction does not by itself presuppose. Therefore the list is
primary: it is what is obtained from the act of distinction
*immediately*; the set is what is obtained *after* an additional
operation.

**Connection with process ontology.** 
The primacy of the list is a manifestation of the processuality of
ontology (§ 7.1), not an independent thesis. The list is
processual: it unfolds. The set, taken as a ready-made collection, is
object-like. To assert the primacy of the list over the set is the same
as to assert the primacy of process over object. This position thus
rests on the processuality of ontology.

### The Set as a Mode of Consideration

If the list is primary and the set derived, it remains to say precisely
*what* a set is and *by what operation* it is obtained from a list.

**A set is a mode of consideration of a list.** 
A set is not a new primary object independent of the process. It is the
result of a *mode of consideration* that takes a list to its *support*:
it keeps only the values occurring in the list and forgets order and
multiplicity.

Precision is needed here. It would be wrong to say that a set is *the
same* list: one and the same values are obtained from *different*
lists—for example, $(a, b, c)$, $(c, b, a)$, and $(a, a, b, c)$ all give
one set $\{a, b, c\}$. Therefore a set is the result of *forgetting* what
these lists differ in—order and multiplicity—and not a list. This
passage is described most precisely by a two-step scheme:

$$
\text{List}(A) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Multiset}(A)
  \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Set}(A).
$$

The first passage forgets *order* (list $\to$ multiset), the second
*multiplicity* (multiset $\to$ set). A set is the result of both
forgettings—the support of a list with order and multiplicity
suppressed.

Take the list $(a, b, a, c)$. To consider it *as a set* is to apply both
forgettings to it: to abstract from the fact that $a$ comes before $b$
(order) and from the fact that $a$ occurs twice (multiplicity). In this
mode the list $(a, b, a, c)$ appears as the set $\{a, b, c\}$—but
$\{a, b, c\}$ itself is not a new object arising beside the list; it is
the support, the result of the mode of consideration applied to the list
(or to any other list with the same values).

**The mode creates no object.** 
This is essential: the passage “list $\to$ set” does not *create* a new
entity. It is a change of *view*, not the generation of an *object*.
When mathematicians work with a set, they work with a list in a certain
mode of consideration—abstracting from order and multiplicity. The set
as a “ready-made collection existing by itself” is a *result of
abstraction*, not an object; to take it for an independent initial
object is to take a mode of consideration for an object.

**In terms of E/R/R.** 
This is a special case of the general marking of the volume (Chapter 4):
a set is a *role-mode* over the elements of a list in which the rule of
succession ($L5$) is suppressed, not an *element*-object. To take a set
for an independent object is to take a role for a bearer—the same
category mistake as “number-as-object” instead of the number-role (Part II)
or “point-as-atom” instead of the point-class (Part IV). Formally this
mode—the passage *list $\to$ multiset $\to$ set* as the successive
suppression of rules—is unfolded with an E/R/R analysis in Part II (the
list as the cardinal path to number).

**Connection with Chapter 6.** 
In § 6.6 a distinction was drawn between position, element, and number:
an element occupies a position; a number is the value of an element. The
mode of consideration “as a set” abstracts precisely from the
*positional* structure (the order of positions) and from the fact that
different elements can have *one* value (multiplicity). A set is the
support of a list in which position and multiplicity are suppressed and
only *which values* occur in it is kept.

### What This View Explains

Understanding the set as a mode of consideration of a list is not a
terminological subtlety. It clarifies a number of classical difficulties
of set theory.

**[[Russells Paradox|Russell’s paradox]].** 
“The set of all sets that do not contain themselves” simply has no
source in the mode of consideration of lists: there is no list whose
unfolding would produce it. Russell’s paradox requires a completed
totality of all sets (Chapter 6, § 6.5); but the totality of all lists
is unfolded by no process, and a set produced by no list does not exist
from the position of ToS. The paradox does not form—not by decree,
but because the construction on which it is built has no source
in a list.

This list-based explanation agrees with the hierarchical blocking
already introduced and complements it rather than replacing it. From the
standpoint of the position “sets as a mode” (§ 7.3), the Russellian
totality has no process source. From the standpoint of $P2/L5$, the same
totality attempts to set a criterion at its own level, which the
hierarchy of levels does not admit: formally this is analyzed in the
file `TheoryOfSystems_Core_ERR.v`, where the theorem
`russell_paradox_blocked` establishes the impossibility of a witness
criterion at its own level through the irreflexivity of the relation
`level_lt`. The list-based and the hierarchical explanations are two
consistent descriptions of one and the same structural impossibility.

**The Axiom of Choice.** 
In classical set theory the Axiom of Choice asserts the existence of a
choice function for a family of nonempty sets. Care in formulation is
needed here. For a *finite* list or a *finite stage* of a process,
choice requires no separate axiom: the order is already given ($L5$
gives it), and so one can choose the first, the maximal, or another
canonical element by a rule—the status mechanism (Chapter 6, § 6.6)
determines such a choice by order. But this is *not* identical to the
full Axiom of Choice for an arbitrary *completed* family of sets. Full
AC requires a separate analysis through $P4$, since it concerns a
*completed* choice function over an infinite family, not a finite choice
within one stage; and in this completed form it is, as analyzed in
Chapter 6 (§ 6.5–6.6), incompatible with $P4$. Thus the mode of
consideration of lists removes the need for the axiom *where finite or
stage-wise choice is at issue*; the question of full AC it does not
close but redirects to the analysis through $P4$.

**Questions of cardinality.** 
Classical questions of cardinality—such as the continuum hypothesis—are
posed on the assumption that cardinality is a primary characteristic of
a ready-made set. From the position of ToS cardinality is not primary:
what is primary is the list and its unfolding, while cardinality is a
characteristic visible only in a mode of consideration abstracted from
order. Questions of this kind are thereby *reformulated* rather than
discarded; their detailed analysis is the subject of the part of the
volume devoted to number systems and the continuum.

Let us stress the boundary of this thesis. *No* solution of the
continuum hypothesis is asserted here—neither positive nor negative.
What is asserted is only a change in the ontological *status* of the
question: cardinality ceases to be a primary characteristic of a
ready-made totality and becomes a characteristic of a mode of
consideration. Formal work with this reformulation is the task of later
parts, and the present chapter does not prejudge it.

**Set theory as a superstructure.** 
What has been said does not abolish set theory. It indicates its
*place*: formal set theory is a *superstructure* over the more primary
level of lists, not a foundation beneath it. In the ToS repository this
is reflected directly: the formalization of set-theoretic
constructions[^2]
is located *on top of* the list apparatus, as a derived layer rather than
as a foundation.

### What This View Does Not Assert

So that the position “sets as a mode” is not read as stronger than it
is, its boundaries must be outlined.

ToS does *not* assert that set theory is false. Set theory is a precise,
powerful, and working mathematical apparatus; nothing in the position of
ToS abolishes this. ToS does *not* assert that mathematicians working
with sets are doing anything wrong: the mode of consideration “as a set”
is legitimate and productive.

*Only* the following is asserted: the ontological *status* of a set is
the status of a mode of consideration of a list, not the status of a
primary object. What changes is the place of set theory in the ontology,
not its working value: it is a superstructure, not a foundation. The
distinction is ontological, not practical. Mathematicians can go on
working with sets exactly as before; ToS only specifies *what* a set is
in doing so.

### Connection with the Ontological Positions of the Volume

The position of the present section—the primacy of the list, the set as
a mode of consideration—is connected with the rest of the ontological
core of the volume.

It continues process ontology (§ 7.1): the primacy of the list over the
set is a special case of the primacy of process over object. The list is
processual, the set as a ready-made collection is object-like; to assert
the first as primary is to carry process ontology into the domain of set
theory.

At the foundation of this position lies a triad worth fixing
explicitly:

> **List** (primary) $\;\longrightarrow\;$ **mode of
> consideration** (abstraction from order and multiplicity) $\;\longrightarrow\;$
> **set** (derived).
> 
> 
> 
> The list is unfolded by a process and is ontologically primary. The mode
> of consideration is an operation of abstraction. The set is what is visible
> in this mode—not an independent object, but a list considered
> with order and multiplicity suppressed.

This triad is the core of the position under consideration. The full
set of the ontological positions of the volume and their mutual
connections are established in § 7.3.

### Connection with Categorical Structure

What has been said admits a precise categorical formulation, which is
only indicated here; its development belongs to the part of the volume
devoted to the categorical structure of mathematics.

The passage “list $\to$ set,” that is, the mode of consideration that
abstracts from order and multiplicity, is a *forgetful functor*: a map
that *forgets* part of the structure—here the structure of order and
multiplicity—keeping only which values occur. Forgetful functors are a
standard categorical construction; characteristically, every such
functor has the direction “full $\to$ impoverished,” and in general there
is no way back—no unique recovery of the forgotten structure.

This categorical observation agrees with the ontological thesis of the
present section: a list carries *more* structure than a set; the passage
from list to set is forgetting; and what is forgotten—the order given by
$L5$, and multiplicity—cannot be uniquely recovered from the set. The
categorical language thus independently confirms: what is *richer* in
structure is primary, and what is obtained from it is obtained by
forgetting. The detailed development of this correspondence is the
subject of the corresponding part of the volume.

## The Ontological Positions of the Volume

### The Setting: From Separate Positions to Their Full Set

§ 7.1 and § 7.2 disclosed separate ontological positions: § 7.1, the
processuality of ontology; § 7.2, the primacy of the list over the set.
The present section gathers the *full set* of the ontological core of
the volume and establishes the order in which its positions are connected.

First of all, one clarification about processuality. The processuality
of ontology, unfolded in § 7.1, is not *one of* the four positions listed
below. It is a *common feature* of the whole ontological core—a feature
running through each of the four positions. Infinity is processual; the
list is processual; divisibility is carried out in a process; structures
arise in unfolding. Processuality is what pervades the whole list, not a
separate item of it. That is why it was set apart in § 7.1, separately
and earlier: it is the background on which the four positions stand.

The volume returns to these four ontological positions throughout all
the subsequent parts. They are listed below in the order of the
*deductive hierarchy*: each subsequent one rests on the preceding ones.

### Positive Ontology

**Formulation.** 
*Everything that exists ontologically exists positively.* There are no
“negative objects”; non-being is not a special kind of being existing
alongside being. What is, is as presence; what is not simply is not—it
is not a *special object* called “absence.”

**Ground.** 
Positive ontology follows directly from the arrangement of the act of
distinction. The act of distinction *posits* the distinguished—it is an
act of presence, not an act of absence. To distinguish $A$ means to
posit $A$; to distinguish $\neg A$ means to posit something other than
$A$—but, again, to *posit*, not to “present an absence.” Negation in ToS
is a *derived operation* on what is posited, not an initial object.
$\neg A$ is not “the negative object $A$”; it is the result of the
operation of distinction applied so that something other is posited.

**Technical caveat.** 
In the record `Distinction` (the file `Distinction.v`) there is the field
`negative : Prop`. Formally this does *not* mean that
`negative` is a “negative object.” This field is the
*negative side of the distinction*—a propositional aspect
connected with the positive side `positive` through the fields
`exclusive` (the incompatibility of the sides) and `exhaustive`
(exhaustiveness). In the canonical case `distinction_of` $(P)$
the field `negative` is given as $\neg P$—that is, as the result of the
operation of negation, not as an independent negative object.
Positive ontology agrees with this: what is positively posited is the
structure of distinction itself with both its sides, while negation is
an aspect of this structure, not a separate kind of being.

**Why positive ontology is primary.** 
Positive ontology stands first in the deductive hierarchy because it
formulates *the very way* in which something is in ToS. Before speaking
of the divisibility of what exists, of the modes of its consideration, of
the inevitability of structures, one must fix that what exists exists
positively. All subsequent positions already operate on what is
positively posited.

### Operational Divisibility

**Formulation.** 
*Atomicity is operational, not absolute.* An object can be indivisible
*relative to* a given level, criterion, language, or procedure of
consideration—but this does not make it an absolutely indivisible being.
What appears as an indivisible element at one level of consideration may
turn out at another level to be a system with its own elements.
Indivisibility is an *operational* characteristic: it indicates the
level at which the object does not decompose, not an absolute property of
the object.

**Ground.** 
Operational divisibility rests on the hierarchy of levels—the principle $P1$
(Chapter 4). Levels in ToS are not absolute: above every level $L$ there
is a higher level, and beneath a structure that appears as an element at
one level, its own arrangement is found at a lower level. What ToS calls
an element is always an element *at some level*; an “element as such,”
absolutely indivisible, has no place in the hierarchical structure of
levels.[^3]

**Connection with positive ontology.** 
Operational divisibility rests on positive ontology. It makes sense to
speak of divisibility only with respect to what is *posited* positively:
what is divided is what is present. Operational divisibility is a
statement about the arrangement of what positively exists—namely, that
its indivisibility is always relative to a level. Without the positivity
of what exists, operational divisibility would have no subject matter:
there would be nothing to divide.

### Sets as a Mode

**Formulation.** 
*Sets are secondary; lists are primary.* A set is not an initial object;
it is a mode of consideration of a list, abstracted from order and
multiplicity. This position is fully unfolded in § 7.2; here it merely
takes its place in the overall composition.

**Connection with the processuality of ontology, $L5$, and the preceding positions.** 
The position “sets as a mode” rests *above all* on the processuality of
ontology and the Law of Order $L5$: a list is an ordered unfolding, that
is, a process construction, and without processuality and order
($L5$) the very concept of a list would not exist. It is on them that
this position depends in the first place. The connection with positive
ontology and operational divisibility is secondary but essential: this
position rests on positive ontology in that a list is a sequence of
*positively posited* elements; on operational divisibility, in that the
passage from list to set is a *change of the mode of consideration*, that
is, an operation carried out at some operational level of work with an
already given structure. Abstraction from order and multiplicity is
operational in exactly the same sense in which divisibility is
operational. This position is thus the application of the operational
view to the pair “list / set”—but it is rooted in the processuality of
ontology and the order of $L5$.

### Structural Inevitability

**Formulation.** 
*Stable structures are regarded as recurring manifestations of the
arrangement of distinction.* Certain structures—orders, hierarchies,
equivalence relations, categorical constructions, and others—appear in
the most diverse areas of mathematics. The position of structural
inevitability proposes to read this as the recurring manifestation of one
arrangement—the arrangement of the act of distinction—rather than as a
chance coincidence or an arbitrary choice of axioms. This is essential:
structural inevitability is a *working ontological thesis* and a
*reading program* for the subsequent parts,
not an already proven universal theorem. ToS does not claim that *all*
the stable structures of mathematics have been formally derived; ToS
proposes to check, in each area of the volume, how order, distinction,
level, criterion, and role again yield familiar mathematical structures.

**Ground.** 
Structural inevitability rests on all the preceding positions, including
the processuality of ontology. If what exists is posited positively, if
it is divisible operationally, by levels, and if the modes of its
consideration are derived from primary process constructions,
then the structures that arise in this way are naturally regarded as a
trace of the arrangement of distinction rather than as an external
choice. The Law of Order ($L5$) gives ordering wherever there is
unfolding; the hierarchy of levels ($P1$) gives hierarchical structure
wherever there is distinction by levels. The position of structural
inevitability proposes to hold this observation as a guiding hypothesis
of reading—and to test it on concrete material in each part of the
volume, rather than accept it as an already proven universal fact.

**Why structural inevitability completes the hierarchy.** 
Structural inevitability stands last because it is a *programmatic
conclusion* from the three preceding ones: it formulates a *guiding
hypothesis* resting on the fact that all three preceding positions hold,
rather than a separate position. If the ontology is positive,
divisibility operational, and sets derived, then the stability and
recurrence of structures is naturally to be *expected* as a trace of the
arrangement of distinction and to be checked part by part. Structural
inevitability is the outcome of the deductive hierarchy of the positions
and, at the same time, the reading program for the rest of the volume.

### The Ontological Core of the Volume

The four positions, gathered together, form the ontological core of
the volume. They are connected by a deductive hierarchy, positive
ontology $\to$ operational divisibility $\to$ sets as a mode $\to$
structural inevitability: each subsequent position rests on the
preceding ones.

> 
> **THE ONTOLOGICAL CORE OF THE VOLUME: THE BACKGROUND AND THE FOUR POSITIONS**
> 
> 
> 
> 
> **The processuality of ontology.** What exists
> exists as unfolding, not as a ready-made givenness. The common background
> pervading all four positions below.
> 
> 
> 
> **Positive ontology.** Everything that exists
> ontologically exists positively. Non-being is not a special kind of
> being; negation is a derived operation, not an initial object.
> 
> 
> 
> **Operational divisibility.** Atomicity is
> operational, not absolute. Indivisibility indicates a level of
> consideration, not an absolute property of the object.
> 
> 
> 
> **Sets as a mode.** Sets are secondary, lists are
> primary. A set is a mode of consideration of a list (its support),
> abstracted from order and multiplicity.
> 
> 
> 
> **Structural inevitability.** A working thesis and a
> reading program: stable structures are regarded as recurring
> manifestations of the arrangement of distinction and are checked part by part.
> 
> 
> 
> *Deductive hierarchy:* positive ontology $\to$ operational
> divisibility $\to$ sets as a mode $\to$ structural inevitability, against
> the common background of the processuality of ontology. Each position
> rests on the preceding ones; processuality is the background pervading all four.

### Positions and Their Formal Status

To distinguish clearly what in the assembled core is already
machine-checked and what is a methodological guide, let us collect the
positions in a table indicating their formal anchors and status.

| Position | Formal anchor | Status |
|---|---|---|
| processuality | `Generator`/`Process` in Core, `P4CompletedInfinity.v`, the physics-track files | partially formalized |
| positive ontology | `Distinction`, fields `positive`, `negative`, `exclusive`, `exhaustive` | direct formal trace |
| operational divisibility | `Level`, `System` $L$, `Criterion` in `Core_ERR.v` | formalized as the hierarchy of levels |
| sets as a mode | lists, support, the layer `src/settheory/` | needs refinement |
| structural inevitability | the recurrence of order, equivalence, categorical constructions | methodological guide |

The table makes the difference in statuses explicit. Positive ontology
has a direct formal trace: the record `Distinction` with its fields is
machine-checked. Operational divisibility is formalized as the hierarchy
of levels (the type `Level` and related constructions). The
processuality of ontology is formalized *partially*: process
constructions and the non-completion of infinity have been checked, but
not as a single complete model. The position “sets as a mode” rests on
the list apparatus, but the exact connection between the list, the
support, and the set-theoretic layer still needs refinement. Structural
inevitability is a methodological guide, a reading program, not a
machine-checked theorem.
This heterogeneity of statuses is normal for a chapter that fixes an
*ontological core and a program*: part of the core is already formal,
part is a guide for subsequent work.

### The Completion of Part I

With the establishment of the four ontological positions, Part I—“The
First Distinction and the Laws of Logic”—is complete in both its
dimensions.

In the *formal* dimension, Chapters 1–6 set the main axis of the ToS
apparatus: $A = \exists$ as the pre-formal first principle; `Distinction`
as the first formalizable trace; the Laws $L1$–$L5$ as structural
properties of distinction and hierarchy; the Principles $P1$–$P4$ as
principles of system construction; $E/R/R$ as the ontological scheme of a
system; levels, criteria, and the technical status of the axioms of
implementation. A number of key nodes of this architecture are directly
machine-checked in Rocq; some nodes serve as an ontological and
methodological guide for the subsequent parts. The exact distribution of
what is already machine-checked and what remains a guide is collected in
the table “Positions and Their Formal Status.”

In the *ontological* dimension (Chapter 7), the core has been gathered:
the processuality of ontology as the background of all the ontological
positions of the volume, and the four positions—positive ontology,
operational divisibility, sets as a mode, structural inevitability—in
their deductive hierarchy.

Chapter 7 adds no new technical layer. It fixes the ontological core and
the *reading program* through which the subsequent parts of the volume
will consider number systems, the structure of the continuum, the
foundations of analysis, algebraic and geometric structures, the
categorical organization of mathematics, and the bridge to physics. In
each of these areas the ontological positions are to be checked anew: where a
number system turns out to be a process rather than a completed object;
where the atomicity of an object proves relative; where a set turns out
to be a mode of consideration; and where one and the same structure
arises in seemingly unrelated areas. Part I has given the ground and the
program; the volume continues with their application.
