## From “Is” to “Distinguished”

### The Indistinctness of Existence before Distinction

Chapter 1 established the primary fact $A = \exists$, discovered the boundary of its formalization, and pointed to the act of distinction as the first formalizable. Now, at the beginning of Chapter 2, we are to *unfold* this pointer: to consider the act of distinction no longer methodologically (as the point to which the present work passes after acknowledging the boundary) but *in substance*—what exactly takes place in it, what its structure is, and why it is fit to serve as the ground of mathematical work.

Let us begin with how $A = \exists$ and the act of distinction are related.

The statement $A = \exists$ says that something *is*, and it says nothing more. It is a maximally substantive minimal statement: it fixes the fact of existence and leaves everything else entirely *indeterminate*. What exactly is? How much of it is there? What is it like? What is its inner structure? All these questions lie beyond $A = \exists$. What exists, as it is given in $A = \exists$, is *indistinct*: it is, but in this “is” nothing determinate has yet been singled out.

In the terms of the metaphysics of Volume I, this ontological state has a precise description. Before the first act of distinction (what Volume I calls Light—the act of Logic distinguishing the dormant status of the Source), there are Logic and the Source as *meta-level entities*—primary facts established in Volume I—but there are no *events*, no distinguishable inner structures, no sequence. This is not “emptiness”: Logic and the Source exist as primary entities; but this is existence that is *not yet differentiated*. The act of Light, as the first event of distinction, actualizes the already-present relation of Logic and the Source: Logic distinguishes the dormant status of the Source, and this distinction becomes the ground for the activation of the witnessing property of the Source—the Source passes from the dormant mode into the active one. From this moment the *determinacy* of structure appears: the division into the actualized and the non-actualized, into what is singled out by distinction and what it is not.

The present work does not unfold this metaphysics; its full exposition belongs to Volume I. Mathematically, however, it is important for us to fix the structural fact that follows from it: *determinacy arises through distinction*. Before distinction, what exists has in principle no structural characteristics on which mathematical work could rely. With distinction the first structural characteristic appears: the division into sides.

### The First Determinate Statement: About the Boundary

If $A = \exists$ is indistinctness, then the first *determinate* statement must be a statement *about a boundary*: that something is *this* and is not *that*.

This formulation is exact. A boundary is the only object that introduces determinacy without presupposing it. An object, a property, a relation presuppose the determinacy of what is already given: to speak of an *object*, one must know its type; to speak of a *property*, one must have its bearer; to speak of a *relation*, one must have the objects it connects. In § 1.4 these possibilities were not treated as candidates for the first formalizable (the work there went differently—by tracing the conditions that make $A = \exists$ possible), but here we can use them as a *contrast*: each of them requires something already given and therefore cannot be the *first* determining gesture.

A boundary, by contrast, requires nothing prior except that within which it is drawn. To draw a boundary, it is enough to have the indistinct “is” already asserted by $A = \exists$ and the work of Logic, which is capable of distinguishing. What exists, within which the boundary is drawn, *already is*—no act of distinction cancels this fact. But the *sides* as determinate sides—what is *this* resting on what is *not that*—appear together with the act itself. Distinction *actualizes* a structural relation that before it existed only as a possibility, not as determinacy.

This agrees with how the metaphysics of Volume I describes the first act of Light. The Source exists before Light as well—but in the dormant mode, in which its witnessing property is not activated. The act of Light *does not create* the Source; it *actualizes* its witnessing property, carrying the dormant mode over into the active one. So too in derivative acts of distinction: the material within which the boundary is drawn exists before this act as well—but as determinate *sides* of a division, as the structural relation “this resting on not that,” it first appears in the act itself. Distinction is ontologically productive by *actualizing determinacy* in what exists, not by *creating* what exists.

In the later chapter on the co-definition of the positive and the negative, this principle will be unfolded in full. Here it is enough for us to fix it as the condition under which the act of distinction can play the role of the first determining gesture: it *does not presuppose* an already divided existent; it *actualizes* the structure of division within what exists.

### Converging Testimonies in the Tradition

The idea that *distinction* (rather than an object, a relation, or a property) is the structural origin of determinacy has a long tradition in philosophy and mathematics. ToS does not base itself on this tradition—its grounding belongs to Volume I—but it is useful to point to converging independent testimonies: they show that the position we have discovered was not invented by us from scratch but recurs in the tradition at different stages of its development.

**Spencer-Brown.**  In *Laws of Form* (1969)[^1], George Spencer-Brown begins with a single operation: “*Draw a distinction*.” From this gesture, conveyed by a single graphic sign, he unfolds the whole of mathematical logic, including the propositional calculus and fragments of arithmetic. For Spencer-Brown, distinction is the *utterly simple* mathematical action on which everything else stands, and there is nothing structurally simpler that would precede it.

Spencer-Brown’s approach, for all its originality, shares a structural skeleton with ToS: distinction as the primary action from which everything further is derived. The differences in detail are substantial (Spencer-Brown works in a graphic notation, and his calculus does not cover quantification or richer constructions), but the *methodological gesture*—to begin with one act of distinction—is shared.

**Saussure and structural linguistics.**  In the *Course in General Linguistics*[^2], Ferdinand de Saussure formulates one of the central theses of the twentieth century: *the meaning of a sign arises not through a direct relation to what it signifies but through its differentiation from other signs*. “In language there are only differences without positive terms.”

This position is a linguistic formulation of the same structural fact. Saussure shows that for a sign to *mean*, there must be a system of other signs from which it differs. Without a differential structure, no sign has content. And again: difference here is not *found* between ready-made signs; it *makes* them signs.

**Heidegger: the ontological difference.**  In *Being and Time* (1927) and in *Identity and Difference*[^3] (1957), Martin Heidegger introduces the *ontological difference*—the difference between *being* (*Sein*) and *beings* (*Seiendes*). This is a structurally primary difference, without which the very posing of the question of being is impossible.

Heidegger’s approach is far from the mathematical one, yet the same intuition is clearly at work in it: a *primary distinction* is not a choice between existing alternatives; it *opens* the possibility for there to be alternatives at all. The difference between being and beings is not a description of two separated entities but the condition of the very possibility of speaking of beings as beings.

**Derrida: *différance*.**  In *Différance*[^4] (1968), Jacques Derrida introduces a neologism that unites two senses of the French verb *différer*: “to differ” and “to defer.” *Différance* is a structure in which difference *produces* identity rather than following from it.

For all the specificity of Derrida’s approach—for him difference is *ontologically, radically primary*, and the identity of objects turns out to be its own effect—the structural move coincides in its general direction with what is at work in the act of distinction: the positive side has identity (the law $L1$) only by resting on its distinction from the negative side. ToS does not follow Derrida in his more radical conclusions about the absence of a stable ontology, but it records the same structural observation: distinction *actualizes* the identities of the sides; it does not presuppose them as given in advance.

**The metaphysics of Volume I.**  Finally, the testimony that matters most for us. In Volume I, on metaphysics, the act of distinction occupies a central place: Light, as the act of Logic distinguishing the dormant status of the Source, is the *first event* in the ontology. Before this act, Logic and the Source exist as meta-level entities, but without events—the Source abides in the dormant mode, and its witnessing property is not activated. The act of Light does not *create* the Source; it *actualizes* its witnessing property, carrying the dormant mode over into the active one. And it is precisely through this first event that time itself becomes possible as the structure of the sequence of acts. ToS rests on this result: the act of distinction is not one of many possible starts but the structurally primary point at which the possibility of determinacy first passes into actuality.

*Accordingly, mathematics as a language of description* begins its work from this first act: mathematics records the structure of acts of distinction found in reality; it does not create a separate world of “mathematical entities.” The position fixed in the Methodological Introduction—mathematics is a language for describing the logical structure of reality—receives, in the work of Volume I and in the analysis of the act of distinction, its first mathematical application: `Distinction`, as a formal record in the language of Rocq, is a record of the logical structure of real acts of distinction, not a self-standing mathematical entity.

This convergence—from Spencer-Brown, working in mathematical logic, through Saussure’s structural linguistics and Heidegger’s ontology, to Derrida’s poststructuralism and the metaphysics of Volume I—is not a proof of the position of ToS. Proof and grounding belong to the present volume’s own line: the derivation of the laws and principles from $A = \exists$. But this convergence shows that the idea of the primacy of distinction is not an arbitrary invention of the author: it arises again and again in different traditions—mathematical, linguistic, philosophical—as a stable structure of thought that is reached independently from different sides. It is testimony to the aptness of the initial idea, not a substitute for its grounding.

Having established that the act of distinction is the object through which the present work passes from the pre-formal $A = \exists$ to formalizable mathematical work, we can now consider its structure *in substance*: what exactly takes place in it, which four moments are combined in it, and how they are connected with one another. The next section is devoted to this.

## The Structure of the Act of Distinction

### Four Moments of One Act

When an act of distinction takes place, four moments manifest themselves in it *simultaneously*:

- **The positive side**—what *is* in the sense of the division being made.
- **The negative side**—what the positive *is not*.
- **Mutual exclusion**—the impossibility of the positive and the negative coinciding at once.
- **Joint exhaustiveness**—the absence of a third: the positive and the negative together cover everything possible in this distinction.

These four moments are *not four independent statements* but *four aspects of one act*. This point requires special attention, since it sets the act of distinction apart from any sum of independent statements that one might suppose.

When we say that the moments are aspects, we mean the following. It is impossible to produce the positive side without at the same time producing the negative one (*this* is *this* as distinct from what?). It is impossible to produce both sides without at the same time fixing their mutual exclusion (otherwise they would merge, and there would be no division). It is impossible to produce a division without at the same time asserting its exhaustiveness (otherwise there would remain room for a “third,” and the original “is” would not be divided). Each moment *requires* the others; none can be produced in isolation from the rest.

In this sense the act of distinction is a *synchronous unity* of four moments. They are distinguishable for analysis—we can speak of each separately, as we are doing now—but ontologically they are *simultaneous* and *mutually require one another*. The entry `Record Distinction`, which we shall introduce in “Formalization in : The Entry Record Distinction,” will reflect this unity as *one structure with four fields*, not as a set of independent statements.

Let us now consider each of the four moments in substance.

### The Positive Side: What “Is” Means in Distinction

The positive side is what *is* in the sense of the division being made. This formulation needs clarification, since the word “is” works here in a different sense than in $A = \exists$.

In $A = \exists$, “exists” is the *primary fact of existence in general*, prior to any distinction. In the act of distinction, “is” refers to one of the sides of the division: the positive side *is* in the sense of being *singled out* by the act, resting on the fact that it *is not* the negative side. Here “is” is a *determinate* “is,” structurally different from “is” as such.

In the terms of the metaphysics of Volume I, the positive side is the *actualized* pole of the distinction. Distinction, as we saw in “From ‘Is’ to ‘Distinguished,’” actualizes a structural relation that existed as a possibility. The positive side is one of the two poles of this actualized relation—the one to which the act of singling out itself refers.

The Law of Identity $L1$ applies to the positive side: it is identical to itself throughout the whole theory. This is an *ontological* application of the Law of Identity, not a formal rule: the positive side is a *determinate* side, and as such it is stably identical to itself in all contexts where this distinction is in force. Without this self-identity the positive side could not take part in further structures—statements about it would not be stable, and references to it would lose their determinacy.

An illustrative example. When a mathematician asserts “the number $n$ is even,” an act of distinction is performed in which the positive side is the statement “$n$ is even.” This positive side is *identical* to itself: at any point of the further reasoning, turning to the statement “$n$ is even,” the mathematician means one and the same thing. If the positive side were not identical to itself, the statement itself would become indeterminate.

### The Negative Side: “Not This” as a Full Participant

The negative side is what the positive *is not*. Here, too, an important clarification is needed, since the everyday understanding of negation can be misleading.

The negative side *is not* “nothing,” “emptiness,” or “absence.” It is the *other side* of the division, with its own content, its own determinacy, and its own ontological status, equal to that of the positive side. In the act of distinction the negative side is *just as actualized* as the positive one; the only difference is that the act’s *singling out* is directed at the positive side, while the negative side stands as that by resting on which the positive is determined.

In other words, the act of distinction *does not single out* the positive from an indeterminate background; it *divides* what exists into two determinate poles, one of which is called positive (the singled-out one) and the other negative (that by resting on which the singling out is determined). Both poles *equally are*, as actualized sides of the division.

This point is essential. In standard mathematical work, negation is sometimes understood as the *complement with respect to the universe*: “not $P$” means “everything in the universe except $P$.” This is a *set-theoretic* understanding of negation, in which the negative is defined through the positive and the universe. The act of distinction works *prior to* this understanding: in it the negative side is not defined as a complement of something earlier; it *co-arises* with the positive side in one act.

Let us take the earlier example. The statement “the number $n$ is even” is the positive side. The negative side—“the number $n$ is not even”—is just as determinate and contentful. It is not “everything else”; it is a *concrete* statement with its own structure and its own meaning. Without this determinacy the act of distinction would be incomplete.

The full unfolding of the principle of the co-arising of the positive and the negative is the subject of the later chapter on the co-definition of the positive and the negative. Here it is important for us to fix the structural fact: the negative side is a full participant in the act, not its “remainder” or “background.”

### Mutual Exclusion: What Is Impossible in One Respect

The third moment of the act of distinction is the assertion of an *impossibility*: the positive and the negative sides cannot hold at once in one respect.

This is the work of the Law of Non-Contradiction $L2$ within the act itself. Without mutual exclusion the division itself would fall apart: if the positive could at the same time be the negative, the boundary between them would be fictitious, and the whole act would reduce to the assertion that *something is* (without any determinate *what*).

Formally, mutual exclusion is written as follows:

$$
\neg(\text{positive} \wedge \text{negative}).
$$

In natural language: “it is impossible for the positive and the negative to hold at once.” This formulation is an inner part of the act of distinction, not an external commentary on it. When an act of distinction takes place, this impossibility is *asserted by the act itself*, not postulated separately.

**A subtlety: “in one respect.”**  The Law of Non-Contradiction and the mutual exclusion corresponding to it work *relative to a concrete act of distinction*, not *in general*. This is an important clarification, since without it one could arrive at the mistaken conclusion that one and the same entity cannot be the positive side in one distinction and the negative side in another.

Meanwhile, this is an entirely ordinary situation. Take the number $4$. In the distinction “even / odd” it stands on the positive side (even). In the distinction “less than 10 / not less than 10” it is again on the positive side. In the distinction “equal to 5 / not equal to 5” it is on the negative side. *In one respect* it occupies a definite place; *in different respects* it can occupy different ones. There is no contradiction here, since what is at issue are *different* acts of distinction, and mutual exclusion works within each of them.

Aristotle, in *Metaphysics* IV.3, formulates the Law of Non-Contradiction with precisely this qualification: “*It is impossible for the same attribute at once to belong and not to belong to the same thing and in the same relation*”[^5]. The words “in the same relation” are the key ones. They show that the Law of Non-Contradiction leaves room for different distinctions of one object in different contexts; what it excludes is only simultaneous membership in both sides of *one* distinction.

### Joint Exhaustiveness: That There Is No Third between the Sides

The fourth moment of the act of distinction is the assertion of the *completeness* of the division: the positive and the negative sides together cover everything possible in this distinction. Between them there is no “third”—nothing that would be neither positive nor negative.

This is the work of the Law of Excluded Middle within the act itself. Formally:

$$
\text{positive} \vee \text{negative}.
$$

“Either the positive holds or the negative holds; there is no third.”

Care is needed in the formulation here. In the metaphysics of Volume I, as was pointed out in the Methodological Introduction, the Law of Excluded Middle is *derived* from Identity and Non-Contradiction. If something *is* (Identity) and cannot at once be and not be (Non-Contradiction), then between “is” and “is not” *there is no third*—nothing that would be neither the one nor the other. This is a consequence of the joint work of the first two laws, not a self-standing third law.

In the formal system Rocq, however, in which the present work is carried out, the Law of Excluded Middle is *not derivable* as a theorem: for an arbitrary proposition $P$ there is no constructive proof of $P \vee \neg P$. For this reason it receives in the formalization the status of a self-standing $L3$—technically a separate field of the record `Distinction` and a separate axiom `classic`, on which the construction `distinction_of` rests (“The Construction distinction_of: Building a Distinction from a Proposition”).

This is a particular case of the difference between the ontological and the formal numbering of the laws, discussed in the Methodological Introduction. Ontologically, joint exhaustiveness *follows* from mutual exclusion together with the identity of the sides; formally, it is *introduced* as a separate field and rests on an axiom. In substance this is one and the same statement; its status within the formal system is technically separate.

For the act of distinction itself, what matters is that exhaustiveness is an inner moment, not an external condition. When an act of distinction takes place, this completeness is *asserted by the act itself*: the act divides what exists into two sides, leaving no room for a third. If such room remained, the division would be incomplete, and the act would not be an act of *distinction* in the full sense.

### Over Everything: The Work of Sufficient Reason

The four moments—the positive side, the negative side, mutual exclusion, joint exhaustiveness—form the structure of the act of distinction. But they do not work by themselves. Over the whole act works the Law of Sufficient Reason—the meta-structure in which the four moments are *grounded* as moments of the act being performed.

Let us unfold this claim. Sufficient Reason, as was established in the Methodological Introduction and in (see the corresponding chapter) of Chapter 1, is not one of the laws alongside the others. Sufficient Reason is *the very action of Logic*, in which grounding exists as a structure. Applied to the act of distinction, this means that each of the four moments has a *ground*, and the act itself as a whole has a ground for taking place as it does and not otherwise.

Concretely:

- The positive side has content because there is a ground for asserting it—a substantive cause for singling out precisely this side and not another. Without such a ground the singling out would be arbitrary, and the act would cease to be an act of *distinction*, turning into a random cut.
- The negative side has meaning because there is a ground for the negation—a substantive cause for taking precisely *this* as what the positive *is not*. Without such a ground the negative side would be empty or arbitrarily filled.
- Mutual exclusion rests on a ground: *why exactly can these two sides not coincide*? The answer must lie in the very structure of the sides, not in an arbitrary condition imposed from outside.
- Joint exhaustiveness rests on a ground: *why exactly do these two sides together cover everything possible*? The answer lies in the nature of the division itself—in its being drawn *along a definite boundary* that leaves no third.

These four grounds are not four independent facts; they are the *joint manifestation* of the one meta-structure of Sufficient Reason applied to the four moments of one act. Sufficient Reason *does not enter* the record of the act as one more field; Sufficient Reason is that *through which* the four fields have force.

In the formal system Rocq this claim shows itself in a specific way. Sufficient Reason is not written as a separate field or axiom; its work shows itself in the fact that building a concrete instance of `Distinction` requires presenting *justifications* for all four fields. One cannot write a `Distinction` with arbitrary field values without presenting their joint consistency; the compiler will reject such an entry. In this sense Rocq as a formal system *builds* the work of Sufficient Reason into its foundation: every definition must be grounded (these are the types), every theorem must be proved (these are the proof terms), every construction must be checkable (this is the kernel of the system). Sufficient Reason works as an *organizing principle* of the formal system, not as one of its statements.

This move—understanding Sufficient Reason as a meta-structure that shows itself in the organization of the work rather than as a separate axiom—will be at work throughout the present volume. Each time we require a constructive building, an explicit criterion, an algorithmically checkable condition, we shall be applying Sufficient Reason—implicitly, but systematically.

Here, however, a distinction must be drawn at once, without which later references to Sufficient Reason and to the law $L4$ may get mixed up. In the present volume we shall distinguish *three* levels at which one and the same ontological principle of sufficient reason appears.

*The first: ontological Sufficient Reason.* This is the very action of Logic that demands a ground—the meta-structure described above. It is neither a field of the record `Distinction` nor a theorem of Rocq; it is that *through which* the record and the theorems have force.

*The second: the law $L4$ in the `Distinction` layer.* This is the self-grounding of distinction, formally expressible in Rocq as the statement `positive D -> ~ negative D`: given the positive side, the negative side is negated. The ground of this negation is given by the very fact of the positive side together with exclusivity—the field `exclusive`. Chapter 3 will derive $L4$ precisely in this form, as a theorem about the record.

*The third: witnessing in the axiomatic layer.* This is a separate technical trace of the same principle: the requirement that individual existence have a *definite witness*. This level is the subject of a later chapter; here we only note that it differs both from ontological Sufficient Reason and from the law $L4$ in the `Distinction` layer.

These three levels should not be confused: ontological Sufficient Reason is a principle, $L4$ is a theorem about the record, witnessing is a technical device of the axiomatic layer. All three are manifestations of one principle of sufficient reason, but they work at different levels of the formalization.

### Four Moments and Five Laws

Let us take interim stock of the structure of the act of distinction from the standpoint of the laws of Logic.

| **Moment of the act** | **Corresponding law** | **Formalization** |
|---|---|---|
| The positive side | $L1$ (Identity) | `positive : Prop` |
| The negative side | $L1$ (Identity) | `negative : Prop` |
| Mutual exclusion | $L2$ (Non-Contradiction) | `exclusive` |
| Joint exhaustiveness | $L3$ (Excluded Middle) | `exhaustive` |
| The grounding of the act | Sufficient Reason (meta-law) | built into Rocq |

The Law of Identity $L1$ works twice—once for each of the two sides. The Law of Non-Contradiction $L2$ works once, providing mutual exclusion. The Law of Excluded Middle $L3$ works once, providing joint exhaustiveness. Sufficient Reason works as a meta-structure that shows itself in the very possibility of a grounded building of the act.

The Law of Order $L5$ does not show itself explicitly in this structure; its work concerns the *hierarchy* of acts of distinction, not the structure of a single act. It will be introduced in the later chapter on the structure of levels—as the self-standing structure `Level`, which formalizes the order of levels.

Thus all the laws of Logic manifest themselves in the act of distinction—some as substantive requirements on the structure of a single act, others as organizing principles of work with acts as a whole. This agrees with what was discovered in § 1.4 of Chapter 1: the act of distinction is *the simplest manifestation of the work of the laws of Logic accessible to a formal record*.

With the structure of the act of distinction established—four moments working as aspects of one act, under the meta-structure of Sufficient Reason—we can now turn to the formal record. The next section will present `Record Distinction` in Rocq and the connection of its four fields with the four moments analyzed above.

## Formalization in Rocq: The Entry `Record Distinction`

### The Record

Having established the substantive structure of the act of distinction—four moments working as aspects of one act under the meta-structure of Sufficient Reason—we pass to its formal record in the system Rocq. This is the first formal object of the present volume with which machine checking works directly.

The record has the following form:

```coq
Record Distinction := mkDistinction {
  positive   : Prop;
  negative   : Prop;
  exclusive  : ~(positive /\ negative);
  exhaustive : positive \/ negative
}.
```

Each of the four fields formalizes one of the four moments of the act analyzed in “The Structure of the Act of Distinction”:

- `positive : Prop`—the positive side of the distinction. A statement denoting what *is* in the sense of the division being made.
- `negative : Prop`—the negative side of the distinction. A statement denoting what the positive *is not*.
- `exclusive : ~(positive /\ negative)`—mutual exclusion. A proof that the positive and the negative cannot hold at once.
- `exhaustive : positive \/ negative`—joint exhaustiveness. A proof that one of the two necessarily holds.

The name of the constructor, `mkDistinction`, follows the usual Rocq convention for record constructors: the prefix `mk` stands for *make*. Given four fields with the right types, one can build an instance of the record by the call `mkDistinction p n e h`, where `p, n, e, h` are the values of the corresponding fields.

### What `Record` Is in Rocq

For the reader who is familiar with standard mathematics but has not worked with Rocq before, let us explain the construction `Record`.

`Record` is a syntactic construction of Rocq equivalent to an inductive type with a single constructor. That is, the entry

```coq
Record Distinction := mkDistinction {
  positive   : Prop;
  negative   : Prop;
  exclusive  : ~(positive /\ negative);
  exhaustive : positive \/ negative
}.
```

is formally equivalent to the definition

```coq
Inductive Distinction : Type :=
  | mkDistinction :
      forall (positive negative : Prop),
        ~(positive /\ negative) ->
        positive \/ negative ->
        Distinction.
```

together with automatically generated *projections* (the functions `positive`, `negative`, `exclusive`, `exhaustive`, which extract the corresponding fields from an instance). The `Record` form is more compact and more convenient to work with; in substance it does not differ from the inductive definition.

What does it mean “to define the type `Distinction`”? A new type is introduced into the type system of Rocq, whose instances are tuples of four consistent values. What does it mean “to have an instance of `Distinction`”? It means having concrete values for all four fields, the third and fourth fields being *proofs* of the corresponding statements in terms of the first two. Without these proofs an instance cannot be built: the Rocq compiler will reject an entry in which `exclusive` does not prove $\neg(`positive` \wedge
`negative`)$ or `exhaustive` does not prove $`positive` \vee `negative`$.

This is one of the manifestations of what we discussed in “Over Everything: The Work of Sufficient Reason”: Sufficient Reason is built into the very work of Rocq. It is impossible to build an act of distinction without explicitly presenting grounds for all four of its moments. The formal system requires justifications; the requirement of grounds is the work of Sufficient Reason.

Here it is important to distinguish two cases at once—the *general* record and the *canonical* construction—for in the text that follows we use both, and confusing them leads to imprecision. In the general record `Distinction`, the field `negative` is a *self-standing* proposition of type `Prop`. It is *not* defined syntactically as the negation of the field `positive`: the connection between the positive and the negative sides is given by the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive`—exclusivity and exhaustiveness—not by an equality. The canonical case—the construction `distinction_of`, to which the chapter will pass in “The Construction distinction_of: Building a Distinction from a Proposition”—chooses the negative side equal to $\neg P$: for an arbitrary proposition $P$ it builds the canonical distinction of $P$ and $\neg P$. The general record is thus wider than the canonical construction: it leaves the negative side an explicit part of the act, without reducing it in advance to the negation of the positive. When below we speak of the negative side as “what the positive is not,” what is meant is the canonical case; the general record does not require this.

### Equivalent Mathematical Notation

For convenience of reference in the main text, besides the Rocq notation we shall use standard mathematical notation. The act of distinction as a tuple is written as follows:

$$
D = (P, N, \varepsilon, \eta),
$$

where

- $P : \mathbf{Prop}$ is the positive side;
- $N : \mathbf{Prop}$ is the negative side;
- $\varepsilon$ is a proof of $\neg(P \wedge N)$;
- $\eta$ is a proof of $P \vee N$.

The type of all acts of distinction we shall denote by $\mathbf{Distinction}$; this is a *mathematical notation*, meaning the type of the objects considered in the general text of the volume. In Rocq listings and in references to the formal code we shall use the monospaced `Distinction`—the name under which the same type figures in the repository. This is *one and the same content* in two notations: $\mathbf{Distinction}$ for mathematical work in the main text, `Distinction` for formal work in code blocks. For a concrete instance $D \in \mathbf{Distinction}$, its fields are accessible through the standard projections:

$$
\text{positive}(D) = P, \quad
  \text{negative}(D) = N, \quad
  \text{exclusive}(D) = \varepsilon, \quad
  \text{exhaustive}(D) = \eta.
$$

Note that $\varepsilon$ and $\eta$ are *proofs*, not *truth values*. This is a consequence of typing in `Prop`, which we shall discuss in detail in “Why Prop and Not bool: The Position of Logical Realism.” Here we fix only this: $\varepsilon$ and $\eta$ carry *structural* information—they show *how* exclusivity and exhaustiveness are established in the given distinction—and do not reduce to the Boolean values “true/false.”

### The Universe `Prop`

A few words about the universe `Prop`, in which the fields `positive` and `negative` live. The full discussion of the choice of `Prop` (as opposed to `bool`) and of the position of *logical realism* connected with it is the subject of the next section, “Why Prop and Not bool: The Position of Logical Realism.” Here we indicate the technical side.

In CIC—the Calculus of Inductive Constructions, on which Rocq is built—there is a hierarchy of universes:

- `Prop`—the universe of *propositions*, that is, of statements. The elements of `Prop` are *statements* that can be proved or refuted.
- `Set`—the universe of *computable data*. The elements of `Set` are types of concrete values: `nat` (natural numbers), `bool` (Boolean values), `list` (lists), and so on.
- `Type`—a more general universe that encompasses `Prop` and `Set` and admits definitions of higher-order types.

When we write `positive : Prop`, we fix that the positive side is a *statement* (something that can be true or false), not a computable value and not a data type. The same holds for `negative`. The fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive` have no type declaration of their own in the form `Prop`; they are declared with a concrete type that depends on the values of `positive` and `negative`—namely $\neg(`positive` \wedge `negative`)$ and $`positive` \vee `negative`$.

This is a characteristic feature of *dependent types* in CIC: the type of a field can depend on the values of other fields. The record `Distinction` uses this possibility: the types of the third and fourth fields are constructed from the first two. If we tried to define `Distinction` in a system without dependent types (for example, in an ordinary typed theory), this would require workarounds.

### What the Formalization Gives

Let us sum up what we have obtained together with the entry `Record Distinction`.

**The first formal object of the present volume.**  Up to this point the exposition worked at the substantive level: formulations, discussions, references to Volume I, on metaphysics. With the introduction of `Distinction` there appears the *first* object that the Rocq compiler accepts into its language, that can be written in a file, checked by machine, and used in further definitions. This transition is central for all the subsequent work: with it *properly mathematical* work begins.

**A universal template for subsequent structures.**  The record `Distinction` sets the pattern by which the subsequent structures of the volume will be built. The idea “fix the type, specify its fields, require justifications” works in all the parts:

- In Part IV, the record `CauchyProcess` as a process with a convergence field.
- In Part XI, the record `Group` as a structure with an operation, an identity element, inverse elements, and associativity fields.
- In Part XII, the record `Category` as a structure with objects, morphisms, composition, and fields of associativity and identity.

Each time the approach is the same: a structure is a record with fields; the fields divide into *carriers* (data) and *justifications* (proofs of properties). This is the work of Sufficient Reason at the mathematical level: each structure has its grounds, and these grounds are a full part of its formal definition.

**Readiness for deriving the laws.**  With the record `Distinction` in hand, we have the ground for further steps. In Chapter 3 the laws of logic $L1, L2, L3, L4$ will be *derived* as theorems about the properties of this record. Each of the four fields will yield the corresponding statement, and the proof of that statement will be trivial—it will reduce to a projection onto the corresponding field or to a simple transformation. Chapter 3 will show this in detail.

Having established the record `Distinction`, we must now explain the essential technical and philosophical choice that lies at its foundation: why the fields `positive` and `negative` are typed in `Prop` and not in `bool`. This question has not only a formal but also a central ontological meaning, to which we pass in the next section.

## Why `Prop` and Not `bool`: The Position of Logical Realism

### A Natural Question

A mathematical reader, seeing `positive : Prop` and `negative : Prop` in the record `Distinction`, may ask a natural question. Distinction is a division into “yes” and “no”; why not use `bool`, the standard type of Boolean values, designed precisely for binary alternatives? The record would look like this:

```coq
Record Distinction_bool := mkDistinction_bool {
  positive_b : bool;
  negative_b : bool;
  ...
}.
```

This question is not technical. The answer requires clarity about *what* distinction *is* and about *which ontology* of a formal system ToS accepts. The present section—the central philosophical section of Chapter 2—is devoted to this answer. It unfolds in several steps: the technical difference between `Prop` and `bool` in CIC; the philosophical difference between proof-irrelevance and proof-relevance; the position of logical realism; the relation to constructivism, Platonism, and formalism; the consequences for the whole of the present volume.

### The Difference between `Prop` and `bool` in CIC

Let us begin with the technical side.

**`bool` is a type of values.**  `bool` in Rocq is an inductive type with two constructors:

```coq
Inductive bool : Set :=
  | true  : bool
  | false : bool.
```

A statement about a Boolean variable $b : `bool`$ is *decided by computation*. If we have an expression returning `bool`, we can *compute* it and obtain, in a finite number of steps, either `true` or `false`. For example, the expression `Nat.eqb 2 2` (Boolean equality of natural numbers) computes to `true`; `Nat.eqb 2 3`, to `false`. In both cases the checking algorithm is available and terminates.

In this sense `bool` is a type of *computable results of checking*. Every statement written in `bool` is *decidable*: there is an algorithm giving the answer “yes” or “no” in a finite number of steps.

**`Prop` is a type of statements.**  `Prop` is the universe of *propositions*—statements that can be true or false but are *not necessarily* decided by computation. A statement $P : `Prop`$ is *proved* by building a *term* (an object in type theory) that has type $P$. The existence of such a term guarantees the truth of $P$.

The key difference: `Prop` *does not presuppose a checking algorithm*. A statement $P : `Prop`$ can be:

- proved, if the corresponding term has been built;
- refuted, if $\neg P$ has been proved (that is, a term of type $P \to \mathtt{False}$ has been built);
- undecidable within the current system, if neither $P$ nor $\neg P$ is provable without introducing additional axioms.

But the *meaning* of the statement $P$ *does not depend* on the presence or absence of a checking algorithm. $P$ has meaning and structure regardless of whether we can check it by computation.

**A simple example of the difference.**  A Boolean predicate is good where we have a decision procedure in advance: for example, `Nat.eqb n m`—the check of equality of two natural numbers—returns `true` or `false` in a finite number of steps. But statements with quantifier structure—of the form `forall n, exists p,\
…`—naturally live in `Prop`: they have an inner logical structure (quantifiers, connectives) and require a *proof*, not a single computed value `true`. For some such statements one can build an associated Boolean test and prove a lemma connecting the test with the statement (a so-called *reflection* lemma); but `bool` by itself does not replace propositional structure—it stores the result of a computation, not the statement itself with its quantifiers and proof.

This is a characteristic example of `Prop` being *wider* than `bool`. Everything that is decidable by computation is expressible in `bool` (and in `Prop` as well, through a correspondence); but statements with quantifier structure that do not reduce to a single computable test live in `Prop`.

### Proof-Irrelevance and Proof-Relevance

Besides the technical difference in the mode of verification, there is a subtler difference between `Prop` and `bool`, connected with the *status of proofs* (or, in the case of `bool`, of values).

**Proof-irrelevance.**  If a statement $P : `Prop`$ has two different proofs $p_1 : P$ and $p_2 : P$, are they considered *different*? In standard mathematical practice, no. A proof is valuable because it establishes truth; different paths to one truth are considered equivalent as proofs. This position is called *proof-irrelevance*—the indistinguishability of proofs.

In `bool`, proof-irrelevance is trivial: each Boolean value has *exactly one* way of being itself. `true` is `true`, `false` is `false`; there are no “different ways of being `true`.”

**Proof-relevance.**  In a subtler version of type theory, proofs have *structure*, and different proofs of one theorem can carry *different information*. This position is called *proof-relevance*—the distinguishability of proofs. It is characteristic of Martin-Löf’s intensional type theory and of homotopy type theory (HoTT), where proofs of identity are interpreted as paths in a topological space, and different paths may be homotopically inequivalent.

**The position of ToS.**  The present volume uses standard CIC, where `Prop` does *not* by default have strict proof-irrelevance, but in most concrete arguments we can accept it (for our purposes of structural analysis, different proofs of one lemma are equivalent).

But *the very structure* of the object `Distinction` is *relevant*. Different acts of distinction, even if they lead to one and the same abstract fact, can be *structurally different*: different positive sides, different negative sides, different justifications of exclusivity and exhaustiveness. The record stores this structure; it does not reduce the act to a simple Boolean outcome.

In this sense the choice of `Prop` instead of `bool` *preserves structural information* where `bool` would lose it. If we wrote `positive : bool`, we would reduce the positive side to one of two values (`true` or `false`); the whole structure of the statement, its content, its connection with other statements—all of this would vanish. The choice of `Prop` preserves this content as part of the formal object.

Let us stress that what is essential for the choice of `Prop` is *not* the difference between proof-irrelevance and proof-relevance as such—this question is secondary for our purposes—but the difference between a *proposition* and a *computed result*. `Prop` preserves the statement itself: its quantifiers, connectives, dependencies, its place in a proof. `bool` preserves only the result of a computable test—one bit. For the record `Distinction`, in which the positive and the negative sides are *statements* with an inner logical structure, it is precisely the former that is essential. The question of the status of proofs—whether they are relevant or irrelevant—matters here only insofar as it sets off this main difference.

### Logical Realism: The Basic Position

Let us pass from the technical analysis to the position that underlies it. This position was established in the Methodological Introduction and works throughout the present volume; here it receives its concrete application to the choice of `Prop`.

**What logical realism says.**  Logic is the structure of being itself, not the rules of a formal system or computational algorithms. Reality exists *in accordance with* logic; without logical structure—without the self-identity of things, without the impossibility of $A$ and $\neg A$ at once, without a ground by which one thing *is* and another *is not*—there would be no reality at all. Logic is *discovered* as the condition of the very possibility of existence; it is neither *chosen* nor *created*.

From this position follows a definite view of mathematical statements. When we assert that the positive side of a distinction *is something*, we assert a structural fact *about the real*—about a property of real things, about the structure of real processes, about the logic of real phenomena. The positive side is not a statement about “a mathematical object as an entity” but *a formal record of a statement about the structure of what is*. This fact may be algorithmically checkable (in the rare cases when the problem is decidable) or not (in most substantive cases). But regardless of whether a checking algorithm exists, the statement *has meaning* (since it describes a real structure) and *has structure* (since it works in a formal record in the language of mathematics).

This agrees with the understanding of mathematics as a language for describing the logical structure of reality, fixed in the Methodological Introduction: the positive side of a distinction records an aspect of real structure; it has meaning because what is described *is*.

**From realism, the choice of `Prop`.**  This is precisely the reason why ToS types the positive and the negative sides of the act of distinction in `Prop`, not in `bool`.

In writing `positive : Prop`, we assert that the positive side of a distinction *is a statement*—something that can be true or not true, something that has a structure irreducible to a computable value. A statement exists as a structural object; its truth-value is established by proof, not by a checking algorithm.

If we chose `bool`, we would in effect be saying that the positive side of a distinction *is a computable result*—one of two outcomes given in advance. This reduces the act of distinction to an algorithm; an algorithm, in turn, is a technical implementation, not an ontological structure. The choice of `bool` would thereby reduce Logic to computability—which contradicts the ontological primacy of Logic established in Volume I.

**Logic is primary; computability is a particular case.**  This is the central methodological position of the whole work of ToS. Computability is a *particular case* of the work of Logic—the case in which the structure of a statement is such that it admits an algorithmic solution. But not every work of Logic is computable, and not every true statement has a checking algorithm. Gödel showed this formally for arithmetic (there exist true arithmetical statements unprovable in a given formal system); the metaphysics of Volume I shows it ontologically (Logic as the structure of being *does not reduce* to the technique of computation).

The choice of `Prop` is the consistent carrying out of this position at the most basic level. The present volume begins with it; from it everything further branches out.

### Constructivism and the Theory of Systems

A common objection from a constructively minded reader may run as follows: “If ToS emphasizes explicit constructions, finite procedures, and checkability, why is the first formal object typed through `Prop` rather than through the computable `bool`? Is not a computable type closer to the spirit of constructivism?”

The answer is important and therefore requires care—including in the very posing of the question. Constructive mathematics and type theory do *not* work predominantly with Boolean values: they work actively with types, propositions, dependent types, witnesses, and proofs. `bool` is an important instrument for decidable predicates, but it is not a synonym for constructivism. Therefore the objection cannot be understood as “constructivism $=$ `bool`”; the question is subtler, and the answer to it follows below.

**ToS does not reject constructivity.**  As was shown in the Methodological Introduction and in the preview of Chapter 5, ToS uses the $E/R/R$ mechanism with an explicit transfer of status, without the Axiom of Choice. This is a typically *constructive* approach—even more strictly constructive than standard classical mathematics. The principle $P4$ (infinity as a property of processes, Part IV) is a direct constructive position: work with the infinite proceeds through explicit processes, not through appeal to “completed” infinite objects.

ToS works constructively *wherever possible* and demonstrates that many classical results remain provable without appeal to non-constructive axioms (Choice, Infinity as a completed object).

**What is rejected is the reduction of Logic to computability.**  The difference is subtle but essential. ToS does not assert that *Logic is computation*—that every true statement has a checking algorithm, or that *nothing exists* outside the algorithmically checkable.

This reduction is characteristic of Brouwer’s radical intuitionism in his early works: “to exist mathematically” was identified with “to be constructible in consciousness in a finite number of steps.” ToS accepts the constructive methodology (concrete constructions instead of abstract postulates), but not *ontological* intuitionism (the identification of existence with constructibility).

In terms of positions: ToS is a *moderate realism* that uses constructive methods. Logic exists as the structure of being (realism); work with mathematical descriptions proceeds through constructive buildings (methodological constructivism). These two positions are consistent and complement each other.

**Concretely, for the choice of `Prop`.**  The entry `positive : Prop` agrees both with the realist reading (there is a structural fact about the real that does not depend on the existence of a checking algorithm) and with the constructive reading. But the constructive reading must be understood precisely here. It does *not* consist in the requirement to *prove* `positive` in order to build a `Distinction`: this is not required. The positive side `positive` is a *statement*, not a proved statement; to build an instance of `Distinction`, one must present two propositions `positive` and `negative` and—here is where constructivity comes in—*proofs* of the declared connections between them: `exclusive` (exclusivity) and `exhaustive` (exhaustiveness). The constructive aspect consists in these connections having to be presented as proof terms rather than postulated. A proof of `positive` itself will be needed only when we wish to establish that the positive side actually holds—but that is a separate step, not needed for building the record. The two readings—realist and constructive—do not contradict each other; they cover different aspects of one position.

### Relation to Platonism and Formalism

To clarify the position of ToS fully, let us place it in the wider landscape of philosophies of mathematics.

**Platonism.**  Gödel, Mac Lane (in some respects), and a considerable part of working mathematicians are Platonists in the broad sense: mathematical objects exist independently of us, in an ideal realm (or, in more restrained formulations, as objective structures that mathematics *discovers* rather than creates).

From the standpoint of ToS, there is a deep truth in Platonism—the idea that mathematical objects are *real*, do not depend on the individual subject, and have an objective structure. This part of Platonism agrees with the operational realism of ToS formulated in the Methodological Introduction.

What ToS does not share is the strong ontological thesis about the *place* of mathematical objects (a separate ideal realm). ToS works with mathematical descriptions as *structural actualizations* that unfold through acts of distinction. They are objective and real, but their mode of existence is not a “separate realm” but an *actualized structure* in the action of Logic on $A = \exists$.

**Formalism.**  Hilbert, in his foundational program: mathematics is a *game of symbols* played by formal rules. “Existence” in mathematics reduces to the *consistency* of symbolic manipulations; the question of what these symbols “mean” in substance is bracketed out.

From the standpoint of ToS, there is truth in formalism as well: a formal system with its rules of inference *is* the technical basis of mathematical work. Rocq as a formal system is the Hilbertian program in its modern type-theoretic implementation.

What ToS does not share is the reduction of mathematics *as a whole* to a symbolic game. ToS works in a formal system, but not *from* a formal system: a formal system is a technical implementation of the work of Logic, not its ground. The content of mathematical statements is given ontologically (through the work of Logic on $A = \exists$); formalization is a subsequent step of recording.

**The operational realism of ToS is a third position.**  The position of ToS formulated in the Methodological Introduction is a *third position*: it *reformulates* the question on other grounds rather than reconciling Platonism and formalism as a compromise between them. ToS accepts that mathematics is a language for describing the logical structure of reality. From this position it follows that *mathematical “objects” do not have the primary ontological status of a separate realm of entities*—neither as ideal entities in a self-standing realm (Platonism) nor as formal constructions outside all reality (formalism). Their status is *derivative*: they are *stable formal records of logical structures discovered in what really exists*. This is not a denial of the objectivity of mathematics: mathematical structures are stable and do not depend on arbitrariness—but their stability is the stability of a record of the real, not of a separate ideal world.

|  | **Platonism** | **Formalism** | **The operational realism of ToS** |
|---|---|---|---|
| The nature of “mathematical objects” | Ideal entities in a separate realm | Symbols in a formal game | Formal records of the logical structures of the *real* |
| Mode of access | Insight into the ideal | Manipulation of symbols by rules | Discovery of structure in reality; formalization in Rocq |
| Status of truth | Correspondence to the ideal | Consistency of inference | Adequacy to the real being described; formal checkability in Rocq |
| “Pure mathematics” (without applications) | Genuine (it explores the ideal) | Genuine (any consistent rules) | Genuine only if it describes a real logical structure |

The three dimensions—the nature of the records, the mode of access, the status of truth—are consistent with one another and form a single position. The choice of `Prop` instead of `bool` is a concrete manifestation of this position at the most basic level of formal work: the positive side of a distinction *is not a computable value*; it is a formal record of a statement about the structure of the real; `Prop`, as the universe of statements, naturally accommodates such a record, and `bool`, as a type of computable values, does not.

### Implications for the Subsequent Parts

The choice of `Prop` in the record `Distinction` is not a local decision concerning only “Formalization in : The Entry Record Distinction.” From this point on, all further work will be conducted in the `Prop` style. Every statement about mathematical objects is a proposition, not a Boolean value. This will make it possible to work with objects whose *truth-value* is nontrivial (it requires proof) while their *structure* is clear.

Let us briefly indicate where this position will show itself in the subsequent parts, and *what in reality* each of these formalisms describes.

**Part IV: `RealProcess` and analysis.**  Processes of approximation really exist—geometric approximations of the length of a circle, measurements of physical quantities with increasing precision, iterative computations. These processes have a common logical structure: a sequence of approximations with convergence. The type `RealProcess := nat -> Q` is a formal record of this structure in the language of Rocq. The process itself, $r : `nat` \to `Q`$, is a computable function living in `Set`. But statements about the process—that it converges, that it represents a concrete geometric or physical value, that it dominates another process—are propositions in `Prop`. In the subsequent parts this will be used as a working approach to the formalization of classical analysis: the data and the processes themselves live in computable types, while statements about their convergence and about the values they represent are formulated in `Prop` (see the discussion of the principle $P4$ and of the continuum hypothesis in the Methodological Introduction).

**Part V: topology.**  Continuous processes really exist—motion, deformation, the transfer of a state from one point of space to a neighboring one. They have a common logical structure: the preservation of “closeness” under transformations. The openness of a set, closedness, compactness, connectedness are formal records of aspects of this structure in the language of topology. They are propositions in `Prop`, not computable predicates: for many real topological properties there is no direct checking algorithm. Work in `Prop` here is not a choice of convenience but a way of preserving the logical structure of really discoverable properties of continuous processes.

**Part XII: category theory.**  Families of structures of one kind with systematic transfers between them really exist—physical systems and their transformations, data types in programming and the functions between them, algebraic structures and homomorphisms. These families have a common logical structure: the composition of transfers, identity transfers, the matching of domains. The categorical apparatus is a formal record of this logic in the language of Rocq. The equality of morphisms, the commutativity of diagrams, the existence of universal constructions are propositions about the real structure of connections in the families. An attempt to reduce them to `bool` would destroy the very possibility of working with these real structures.

**Part XV: quantum information.**  Quantum systems with their measurable properties really exist—the orthogonality of states, unitary evolution, Hermitian observables, limits on information (entropy). These properties have a logical structure expressed in the formalism of Hilbert spaces. The record of the properties of quantum states and operators in Rocq consists of propositions in `Prop`. The structural properties of physical systems are statements about real structure, not results of computations: for many interesting properties there is no direct checking algorithm, and work in `Prop` serves as the natural way of recording them.

In all four cases one and the same connection is at work: *processes and structures really exist; mathematics records their logical structure formally; in Rocq these records live as propositions, not as computable values, because the real structures being described are in general not decidable by a checking algorithm*. The choice of `Prop` instead of `bool` is the systematic carrying out of this position through all the subsequent work.

Thus the choice of `Prop` in the record `Distinction` is not a local technical detail but a *decision of principle* that shapes the face of the present volume. It is a concrete manifestation of the position of logical realism at the very initial level of formal work. All the subsequent mathematical work begins with it and passes through it, and in it the ontological position of the metaphysics of Volume I and the technical practice of work in Rocq are brought into agreement.

Having established the record `Distinction` and grounded its typing in `Prop`, we can now pass to the universal construction that builds a distinction from any proposition. This construction, denoted `distinction_of`, is a bridge between the general statements of Rocq and concrete acts of distinction. The next section is devoted to it.

## The Construction `distinction_of`: Building a Distinction from a Proposition

### Statement of the Problem

Having established the record `Distinction` and grounded its typing in `Prop`, we face a natural question. We have the *form* of the act of distinction—four fields with definite types. But we do not yet have a single *concrete* instance. Can it be shown that this form is not empty?

Moreover, can an instance of `Distinction` be built from an *arbitrary* proposition? If such a way exists, it *connects* the record `Distinction` with the whole universe of statements of Rocq: every proposition available for consideration in the formal system receives a corresponding distinction.

The natural candidate: for a proposition $P$, take

- the positive side $:= P$,
- the negative side $:= \neg P$.

This is the most obvious way to build a distinction from a statement: to single out the statement itself as the positive side and its negation as the negative side. Let us call this construction `distinction_of`.

In this section we implement `distinction_of` formally, examine which axioms of Rocq it depends on, and discuss its substantive meaning.

### What Is Described in Reality

Before passing to the formal definition, let us spell out what this construction describes in reality—in keeping with the position, fixed in the Methodological Introduction, that mathematics is a language for describing the logical structure of the real.

In reality, `distinction_of` $(P)$ describes the following phenomenon: *every substantive statement about the real automatically generates a structure of distinction between the state in which this statement holds and the state in which it does not hold*.

In other words, if in reality one can say “$P$”—say, “the number $n$ is even,” “the function $f$ is continuous at the point $x_0$,” “this molecule has a $C_3$ symmetry axis”—then together with this statement there arises a structural distinction between the *situation where $P$ holds* and the *situation where $P$ does not hold*. This distinction is not a separate phenomenon but an *inseparable aspect* of the statement itself: to assert $P$ is *at the same time* to distinguish the case $P$ from the case $\neg P$.

The construction `distinction_of` formalizes this aspect: it shows that the structure of distinction is already contained in the very structure of a statement. There is no need to create an act of distinction *separately* for each proposition—the statement by itself already carries it within as its own logical structure.

This agrees with what was established in Chapter 1: the act of distinction is the simplest manifestation of the work of the laws of Logic, and this work *is already taking place* whenever there is a substantive statement. `distinction_of` makes this taking place *explicit* in the formal record.

### The Definition in Rocq

The formal definition of `distinction_of` in Rocq:

```coq
Definition distinction_of (P : Prop) : Distinction :=
  mkDistinction P (~ P) _ _.
```

Here the underscores `_` stand in place of *proof obligations*—they must be replaced by concrete proofs of the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive`. These obligations are as follows:

- `exclusive`: $\neg(P \wedge \neg P)$, the law of non-contradiction for the proposition $P$.
- `exhaustive`: $P \vee \neg P$, the law of excluded middle for the proposition $P$.

Let us examine each of them.

**The proof of `exclusive`.**  The statement $\neg(P \wedge \neg P)$ is provable in Rocq *without any axioms*, constructively. The proof is simple: suppose $P \wedge \neg P$, that is, we have at once $p : P$ and $np : \neg P$. By definition, $\neg P$ is a function $P \to \mathtt{False}$. Applying $np$ to $p$, we obtain an element of type `False`, which is the required contradiction.

In Rocq this is written:

```coq
Lemma no_contradiction_P : forall P : Prop, ~(P /\ ~ P).
Proof.
  intros P [p np]. exact (np p).
Qed.
```

The tactic `intros P [p np]` introduces $P$ as a parameter and splits the hypothesis $P \wedge \neg P$ into two: $p : P$ and $np : \neg P$. The command `exact (np p)` applies $np$ (of type $P \to \mathtt{False}$) to $p$ (of type $P$), obtaining an element of type `False`, which completes the proof.

This is a manifestation of the Law of Non-Contradiction $L2$ on a concrete proposition $P$. The law works *universally*: for any $P$, the statement $\neg(P \wedge \neg P)$ is established by one and the same short argument.

**The proof of `exhaustive`.**  Here the situation is different. In pure CIC, the statement $P \vee \neg P$—the law of excluded middle—is *not derivable* for an arbitrary proposition $P$. This is a most important fact, deserving explicit analysis.

### Dependence on the Axiom `classic`

In CIC, the Calculus of Inductive Constructions on which Rocq is built, all constructive buildings must be *explicit*: a term proving a statement must be presented algorithmically.

Applied to $P \vee \neg P$, this means: to prove constructively “either $P$ or $\neg P$” for an arbitrary $P$, one must *present* a concrete path—a proof of $P$ or a proof of $\neg P$. But for an arbitrary $P$ such a path is *unknown*: we do not know whether a concrete $P$ holds or not without knowing the very content of $P$.

Put differently: an algorithm that for any proposition $P$ would return a proof of $P$ or a proof of $\neg P$ *does not exist*. Many propositions (for example, statements about infinite sets, existence statements, many number-theoretic conjectures) have no known constructive solution and may have none in principle. Therefore, in pure CIC, the law of excluded middle as a universal statement is *not a theorem*.

**The axiom `classic`.**  To work with the law of excluded middle in full generality, Rocq provides the axiom:

```coq
Axiom classic : forall P : Prop, P \/ ~ P.
```

This axiom postulates that for any proposition $P$, $P \vee \neg P$ holds. It is not proved constructively (as we have seen); it is *accepted* as a statement of classical logic.

In ToS the acceptance of `classic` is grounded ontologically: according to the metaphysics of Volume I, the Law of Excluded Middle is one of the conditions of the work of Logic on $A = \exists$. In reality there is *always* a determinate state of affairs: either something holds or it does not (in one respect). That we have no algorithm to establish, for each concrete $P$, which of the two it is does not cancel the ontological fact: the state of affairs is determinate.

Constructive undecidability is an epistemological limitation (the impossibility of always learning which of the two holds); `classic` as an axiom fixes ontological determinacy (one of the two always holds). The acceptance of this axiom is a clear expression of the position of logical realism discussed in “Why Prop and Not bool: The Position of Logical Realism”: Logic is ontologically primary, and computability is its particular case.

One caveat is important here, so as not to ascribe to `classic` more than it gives. The axiom `classic P` gives a proof of the disjunction $P \vee \neg P$ in `Prop`—but it does *not* give a computable test that would return `true` or `false` depending on which side holds. In other words, `classic` asserts that one of the two is true but does not indicate which. For building the field `exhaustive` this is enough: `exhaustive` is a proof of $P \vee \neg P$, not a computed indicator of the side. But if an *informative* choice of branch is needed later—computational branching that depends on whether $P$ holds—`classic` alone is not enough: an additional principle of witnessing is needed, discussed in the later chapter on the axiomatic layer of ToS.

**Double numbering and status.**  The Methodological Introduction fixed the following: in the ontology of Volume I the Law of Excluded Middle is *derived* from Identity and Non-Contradiction; in the formal system Rocq it is a *self-standing* $L3$ resting on the axiom `classic`. This is a particular case of the double numbering of the laws, and it concerns precisely the construction `distinction_of`: the field `exhaustive` in a record `Distinction` built from an arbitrary proposition $P$ rests on `classic`.

A detailed discussion of all the technical axioms on which ToS rests (`classic` and `L4_witness`), and the ontological clarification of their status, is the subject of the later chapter on the axiomatic layer of ToS. Here it is important for us to fix that `distinction_of`, as a universal construction, requires `classic`, and that this requirement is ontologically grounded.

### The Full Proof in Rocq

Taking the above into account, the full definition of `distinction_of` in Rocq has the form:

```coq
Definition distinction_of (P : Prop) : Distinction.
Proof.
  apply (mkDistinction P (~ P)).
  - (* exclusive : ~(P /\ ~ P) *)
    intros [p np]. exact (np p).
  - (* exhaustive : P \/ ~ P *)
    apply classic.
Defined.
```

Let us go through it line by line.

- `Definition distinction_of (P : Prop) : Distinction.`—we declare a function that takes a proposition $P$ and returns an act of distinction. Using a period instead of `:=` switches Rocq into interactive mode, which allows the construction to proceed step by step with tactics.
- `apply (mkDistinction P (~ P)).`—we apply the constructor `mkDistinction`, fixing the first two arguments (`positive := P` and `negative := ~ P`). Two goals remain: the proof obligations for the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive`.
- The first goal: `~(P /\ ~ P)`. We unpack the hypothesis into $p : P$ and $np : \neg P$, apply $np$ to $p$, and obtain `False`. This is written in one line: `intros [p np]. exact (np p).`
- The second goal: `P \/ ~ P`. We apply the axiom `classic`: `apply classic.`
- `Defined.` (rather than `Qed.`)—completes the construction, making the definition *transparent* for further work (the terms `distinction_of` $(P)$ can be unfolded in other proofs).

In mathematical notation the same object is written more briefly:

$$
\text{distinction_of}(P) \;=\; \big(P, \neg P, \varepsilon_P, \eta_P\big),
$$

where $\varepsilon_P$ is a proof of $\neg(P \wedge \neg P)$ (constructive), and $\eta_P$ is a proof of $P \vee \neg P$ (resting on `classic`).

### What `distinction_of` Shows

Let us sum up in substance. The construction `distinction_of` shows the following.

**The universality of the record `Distinction`.**  Here it is worth separating two different claims—the *nonemptiness* of the form and its *universality*—for they require different things. That the type `Distinction` is not empty is seen already in the simplest example: a distinction can be built from `True` and `False`, and no classical principle is needed for this—the exclusivity and exhaustiveness of the pair `True` / `False` are proved directly. But `distinction_of` does more than show nonemptiness: it establishes the *universality* of the record. For *every* proposition $P$ there is a corresponding distinction `distinction_of` $(P)$—the canonical distinction of $P$ and $\neg P$. There are no propositions for which an act of distinction cannot be built. And it is precisely this universality, not mere nonemptiness, that requires the classical principle `classic`: in order to present, for an *arbitrary* $P$, the field `exhaustive`—a proof of $P \vee \neg P$. The type `Distinction` is thus not merely nonempty: it is rich enough to cover the whole universe of propositions—and the price of this richness is the appeal to `classic`.

**The canonical structure of a statement.**  Every proposition carries within itself the *canonical* distinction between itself and its negation. This distinction *is not chosen from outside*—it is given by the statement itself, as soon as the statement has meaning. To assert $P$ is at the same time to distinguish $P$ and $\neg P$; `distinction_of` makes this simultaneity explicit in the formal record.

**A bridge between statements and acts.**  `distinction_of` is a bridge between two levels of work: the level of *statements* in Rocq and the level of *acts of distinction* as the structural objects of the present work. Any statement available in Rocq can be considered *as* a distinction and worked with in this structure—through the four fields `positive`, `negative`, `exclusive`, `exhaustive`.

**Preparation for deriving the laws.**  From `distinction_of` the laws of logic $L1, L2, L3, L4$ will be derived as theorems about the properties of `Distinction`. Chapter 3 will show concretely that for any $D : `Distinction`$, in particular for $D = `distinction_of` (P)$, formal statements expressing the classical laws of logic are checked. This is the central result of Part I.

**Connection with operational realism.**  `distinction_of` agrees with the fixed ontological position: mathematics as a language for describing the logical structure of the real. The construction does not *create* a distinction where there was none; it *makes explicit* the logical structure already present in the statement itself. This is the structure of the *real*: in any real situation where the statement $P$ has meaning, the distinction between $P$ and $\neg P$ has meaning as well.

With the construction `distinction_of` in hand, we are ready to consider the question of the *minimality* of the record `Distinction`: can one do with fewer fields without losing substantive work? The next section is devoted to this.

## Minimality of the Record: Can One of the Four Fields Be Removed?

### Posing the Question

The record `Distinction` has four fields: `positive`, `negative`, `exclusive`, `exhaustive`. A natural question, especially for a mathematical audience: *is this a minimal set*? Can the same substantive work be obtained with fewer fields?

This question is not aesthetic. In (see the corresponding chapter) of Chapter 1 it was established that the first formalizable must stand *at the point of balance* between minimality and productivity. If the record were redundant, we would obtain additional structure not justified by proximity to the boundary of formalization. If the record were insufficient, we would lose the possibility of deriving further structures from it (the laws of logic, further constructions). Minimality is a structural characteristic that must be *shown*, not *postulated*.

The method of showing is structural analysis: let us consider what happens without each of the fields in turn and make sure that in each case the record loses the status of a *distinction*. This is not a rigorous proof in a formal system (the minimality of `Distinction` is a metatheoretical observation about the choice of formalism, not a theorem inside Rocq), but a concrete structural analysis on which our choice stands.

### Without the Fields `positive` or `negative`

The obvious case: without one of the sides *there is no division itself*. A record with three fields—for example, `positive`, `exclusive`, `exhaustive` without `negative`—would be syntactically meaningless: the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive` refer to `negative` in their types ($\neg(`positive` \wedge
`negative`)$ and $`positive` \vee `negative`$, respectively). Without `negative` these types cannot even be written.

This case requires no detailed analysis: `positive` and `negative` are the two sides of the division; without both there is no division. More interesting are the cases where the sides themselves are in place but one of the conditions (`exclusive` or `exhaustive`) is absent.

### Without the Field `exclusive`

Consider a record from which the field `exclusive` has been removed:

```coq
Record Distinction_NoExcl := mkDistinction_NoExcl {
  positive   : Prop;
  negative   : Prop;
  exhaustive : positive \/ negative
}.
```

What have we obtained?

In this structure `positive` and `negative` can be *true at once*. No condition excludes their coinciding. This means that we can build an instance of such a record with contradictory content:

```coq
Definition trivial_both : Distinction_NoExcl :=
  mkDistinction_NoExcl True True (or_introl I).
```

Here `positive := True`, `negative := True`; the field `exhaustive` is trivially satisfied (`True $\vee$
True`—even one of the sides gives the whole condition). We obtain a “record” in which both sides are at once *true*—which is a direct violation of the very nature of division.

**What this means structurally.**  Without the field `exclusive`, *mutual exclusion* as part of the structure is lost. The Law of Non-Contradiction $L2$ ceases to be built into the record itself—it works “from outside,” and one would have to check separately each time that the concrete `positive` and `negative` are in fact incompatible.

This is not merely an inconvenience. It is a *loss of the ontological function* of the record. A distinction is by definition a structural division into two *incompatible* sides; removing the condition of incompatibility, we obtain not a distinction but merely *a pair of statements one of which holds*. This is an entirely different object, expressing merely a disjunction of two propositions rather than the work of Logic on $A = \exists$.

**In the terms of the metaphysics of Volume I.**  The four moments of the act of distinction, as we saw in “Four Moments of One Act,” are aspects of one act that mutually require one another. Removing mutual exclusion means removing one of these aspects. The remaining aspects lose their connection with being aspects of a *distinction*: they become merely components of a pair of statements, not the structure of an act.

### Without the Field `exhaustive`

Consider the reverse case—a record without the field `exhaustive`:

```coq
Record Distinction_NoExh := mkDistinction_NoExh {
  positive  : Prop;
  negative  : Prop;
  exclusive : ~(positive /\ negative)
}.
```

What have we obtained now?

Here `positive` and `negative` can be *false at once*. The condition of exclusivity is trivially satisfied when both statements are false (false statements cannot coincide in a true conjunctive statement). We can build:

```coq
Definition trivial_neither : Distinction_NoExh :=
  mkDistinction_NoExh False False
    (fun H => match H with conj h _ => h end).
```

`positive := False`, `negative := False`; the condition `exclusive` is trivial (if both are false, their conjunction is false too, and its negation is true). We obtain a “record” in which *neither side holds*—which is likewise a direct violation of the very nature of division.

**What this means structurally.**  Without the field `exhaustive`, *joint exhaustiveness* as part of the structure is lost. The Law of Excluded Middle $L3$ ceases to be built into the record—a “third way” may appear, a state “neither positive nor negative,” which destroys the idea of a *complete* division.

And again: this is a loss of ontological function. A distinction is a *complete* division of what exists into two sides, with no gap between them. Removing the condition of exhaustiveness, we obtain not a distinction but merely *a pair of incompatible statements* between which a third option can slip through. This is a different object—such a structure would suit, for example, the description of a three-valued logic or of intermediate states, but not work with the *simplest* act of distinction, in which exhaustiveness guarantees the completeness of the division.

### Summary: The Four Fields Are Necessary

The structural analysis shows that each of the four fields of `Distinction` carries its own work, and the removal of any of them leads to the loss of the status of a *distinction*.

| **Field** | **Work** | **What is lost without it** |
|---|---|---|
| `positive` | The content of the positive side | A side of the division is absent |
| `negative` | The content of the negative side | A side of the division is absent |
| `exclusive` | The impossibility of the sides coinciding | The sides can be true at once—the division falls apart |
| `exhaustive` | The absence of a third between the sides | The sides can be false at once—a “third way” appears |

Each field is responsible for one piece of work in the structure of the act of distinction; none duplicates another; none can be derived from the rest.

This minimality must be understood precisely. It is *not* an absolute metatheorem about the impossibility of any other encodings of the act of distinction. One can imagine another record as well—for example, one that stores only a single base proposition, with the negative side defined as its negation, exclusivity proved trivially, and exhaustiveness obtained by applying the classical principle. Such an encoding would be poorer in expressiveness, but it is formally possible. The minimality at issue here is the minimality of the *chosen structural record* `Distinction`, in which the positive and the negative sides are represented *explicitly*, while exclusivity and exhaustiveness are *inner fields* of the act. In another encoding part of this structure could be computed or derived—but then precisely that ontological explicitness of the sides and their connections for the sake of which `Distinction` is introduced would be lost. The statement “no field is derivable from the rest” holds *within* this record: once we have decided to represent all four moments explicitly, none of the four fields is redundant.

**A metaphysical parallel.**  This structural minimality agrees with the metaphysical position analyzed in “Four Moments of One Act”: the four moments of the act of distinction are aspects of one whole that mutually require one another, and none can be separated without the act falling apart. The formal record reflects the ontological structure: the four fields of `Distinction` correspond to the four moments, and their jointness is not a technical convenience but a *direct reflection* of the structural integrity of the act.

### Can the Record Be Extended?

It is logical to ask the converse as well: if four fields are the minimum, can a fifth be *added* to obtain a richer structure?

Formally, yes: nothing prevents us from adding an extra field. Such an extension, however, *would not be the first formalizable* in the sense of Chapter 1: it would contain additional structures not justified by proximity to the boundary of formalization.

In other words, at the very first step of mathematical work we have no other objects that could justify additional structures in the record of a distinction. Each field must be justified *by itself*, not by reference to objects of the theory already at hand. At the level of the act of distinction taken in its purity, four fields are *enough*; more is redundant.

**Further extensions come in later chapters.**  This does not mean that the present volume introduces no structures that extend `Distinction`. On the contrary, the later chapters will bring:

- In Chapter 6 (Part I), the type `Level` as a self-standing structure for formalizing the Law of Order $L5$. `Level` *does not enter* `Distinction` itself, because $L5$ concerns the hierarchy of acts of distinction, not the inner structure of a single act.
- In Part III, structures of relations between distinctions, resting on `Distinction` as the base object but introducing their own superstructure.
- In Part XI, algebraic structures (groups, rings), in which `Distinction` is used indirectly, through records about the properties of elements and operations.

Each of these extensions is *justified* by the level of work at which it is introduced. But in the record `Distinction` itself, as the first formalizable, these extensions are absent. The minimality of the record means that *at the beginning* we carry with us no structure beyond what is strictly necessary for the act of distinction itself.

### Preparation for Chapter 3 and Beyond

The minimality of `Distinction` has a direct consequence for Chapter 3, in which the laws of logic $L1, L2, L3, L4$ will be *derived* from this record as theorems of Rocq.

Since each of the four fields of the record is responsible for a concrete piece of work (the identity of the sides, exclusion, exhaustiveness, joint grounding through Sufficient Reason), *each* of the derived laws will naturally rest on one of the fields or on their combination. Chapter 3 will show this concretely: $L1$ is derived from `positive` and `negative` (the identity of the sides); $L2$, from `exclusive`; $L3$, from `exhaustive`; $L4$, as the self-grounding of distinction: from the positive side together with `exclusive` (the presence of the positive side and exclusivity give a ground for negating the negative side).

Minimality guarantees that this connection will be *clean*: each law rests on exactly the part of the record that is necessary, without redundant details. This is one more manifestation of `Distinction` standing at the ontologically right level: the structure is minimal in content and at the same time rich in consequences.

Having established the minimality of the record, we can pass to the last substantive question of Chapter 2: how are the positive and the negative sides related? Can a `Distinction` be built with only one of them? A preview of this topic is the subject of the next section, with its full unfolding in the later chapter on the co-definition of the positive and the negative.

## The Co-Definition of the Positive and the Negative

### The Impossibility of Building a `Distinction` without Both Sides

Having established the minimality of the record `Distinction`, we can make one more structural observation, which will have essential consequences for all the further work. From the definition

```coq
Record Distinction := mkDistinction {
  positive   : Prop;
  negative   : Prop;
  exclusive  : ~(positive /\ negative);
  exhaustive : positive \/ negative
}.
```

it follows that to build an instance of `Distinction`, one must present *both* sides—both `positive` and `negative`—together with the justifications `exclusive` and `exhaustive` that explicitly connect these sides. One cannot “have only the positive and put off the negative”: the record will not accept such a construction, since the types of the fields `exclusive` and `exhaustive` require both sides in their definitions.

This is a formal, syntactic fact about the record. But behind it stands a structural ontological principle that deserves to be spelled out explicitly.

### The Structural Unity of the Sides

When we define `positive := P`, we at the same time define `negative` structurally as $\neg P$—this is exactly how the universal construction `distinction_of` $(P)$, analyzed in “The Construction distinction_of: Building a Distinction from a Proposition,” works. The positive side *is defined by resting on* the negative one, and vice versa: the very *what* that the positive side posits has meaning only through its distinction from what the negative side *negates*.

In other words, the positive and the negative *do not exist separately*; they are *co-defined* in one act.

This principle is a deep ontological fact about the structure of distinction, consistent with the position fixed in the Methodological Introduction: mathematics as a language for describing the logical structure of reality. In reality there is no “positive without its negative”—every statement about the real acquires meaning through its distinction from what it *does not assert*. To say “this is a $C_3$ symmetry axis” is at the same time to say what this axis *is not* (not $C_2$, not $C_4$, not $C_6$). To say “the function $f$ is continuous at the point $x_0$” is at the same time to say that it is not *discontinuous* at this point. The positive content of a statement and its negation *come together*.

The record `Distinction`, which requires both sides in one act of building, reflects this structural feature of the real. It is not that one side exists first and then the second is “added” to it. Both are *actualized* in the very act of distinction—this agrees with what we saw in “From ‘Is’ to ‘Distinguished’”: distinction does not *create* what exists but *actualizes* in it the structure of division.

With this section the main content of Chapter 2 is complete. The record `Distinction` has been introduced; its typing in `Prop` has been grounded; the universal construction `distinction_of` has been built; the minimality of the record has been shown; the principle of the co-definition of the positive and the negative has been fixed. It remains to sum up Chapter 2—what has been achieved. The last section is devoted to this.

## Summary of Chapter 2

Chapter 2 introduced the first formal object of the present work—the record `Distinction`, which formalizes the act of distinction. Let us sum up what has been achieved.

### What We Have Gained

**The first formalizable object.**  The structural entry `Record Distinction` with four fields (`positive`, `negative`, `exclusive`, `exhaustive`) is a formal record in the language of Rocq of the logical structure of the act of distinction as it works in reality. The four fields correspond to the four moments of the act analyzed in substance in “The Structure of the Act of Distinction,” and they work as aspects of one whole, not as independent statements.

**Typing in `Prop`.**  The positive and the negative sides of a distinction are propositions, not computable Boolean values. This is a concrete manifestation of the position of *logical realism* fixed in “Why Prop and Not bool: The Position of Logical Realism”: Logic is ontologically primary, and computability is its particular case. Every statement about the real *has meaning* regardless of whether a checking algorithm exists, and a formal record in the language of mathematics must preserve this structure.

**The universal construction `distinction_of`.**  For any proposition $P$, the construction `distinction_of` $(P)$ builds the corresponding distinction with the positive side $P$ and the negative side $\neg P$. This is a bridge between the statements of Rocq and acts of distinction as structural objects: every substantive statement about the real automatically carries within itself a canonical distinction.

**Dependence on `classic`.**  The construction `distinction_of` rests on the axiom `classic` ($\forall P, P \vee \neg P$) for building the field `exhaustive`. This dependence is technical: in pure CIC the law of excluded middle is not derivable. Ontologically the law is derived (from Identity and Non-Contradiction); formally it is a self-standing axiom, which agrees with the double numbering of the laws fixed in the Methodological Introduction.

**The minimality of the record.**  The structural analysis (“Minimality of the Record: Can One of the Four Fields Be Removed?”) has shown that each of the four fields is necessary; the removal of any of them leads to the loss of the status of a *distinction*. Without `exclusive` the sides can coincide (the record loses exclusivity); without `exhaustive` both sides can be absent (the record loses completeness). The minimality of `Distinction` is a structural property, not a choice of convenience, consistent with the position of this record as the *first formalizable* (Chapter 1, § 1.4).

**The co-definition of the positive and the negative.**  It is impossible to build a `Distinction` with only one side. The positive and the negative *co-arise* in one act; neither side exists separately. This principle is a fundamental feature of the act of distinction, reflecting the structure of the real: every substantive statement acquires meaning through its distinction from what it *does not assert*. The full unfolding is in Chapter 5.

### Connection with Chapter 1

Chapter 1 established the methodological matrix of the present volume: on meeting a structural boundary of formalization, acknowledge it, do not shrink it, and move on to the nearest formalizable object. In Chapter 2 this matrix was *applied for the first time*.

- **Acknowledge the boundary.** $A = \exists$ remains the pre-formal ground and is not formalized in the entries of Rocq. The boundary established in § 1.3 is preserved.
- **Do not shrink it.** No artificial constructions that try to get around this boundary are introduced (such as `Axiom A_exists : True` or the like); $A = \exists$ remains a fundamental fact of the ontology of Volume I.
- **Move on to the nearest formalizable.** This formalizable turned out to be the act of distinction, unfolded in Chapter 2 as `Distinction`. The first formal definition has been made; properly mathematical work has begun.

In this sense Chapter 2 completes the work begun in Chapter 1: what Chapter 1 pointed to as the first formalizable has become, in Chapter 2, a real object of Rocq, ready for further constructions.

### Connection with the Ontological Position

The record `Distinction`, according to the position fixed in the Methodological Introduction, is *a formal record, in the language of mathematics, of the logical structure of real acts of distinction*—neither a self-standing mathematical entity existing in some “ideal realm” nor an empty symbolic construction.

Acts of distinction really take place—in physical processes (the distribution of states, phase transitions), in biological processes (the classification of stimuli), in mental activity (conceptual demarcations), in mathematical work (definitions and their application). These acts have a common logical structure: two sides, their exclusion, their joint exhaustiveness, the grounding of the act itself. `Distinction` *records* this common structure in the language of Rocq, making it accessible to formal work.

The universality of `distinction_of`—the fact that this record is applicable to any proposition—reflects the universality of the work of Logic: wherever the real is in question, the structure of distinction is always at work, and the formal language of mathematics can fix it.
