## The Problem of the Beginning

### The Regress of Justification

Every mathematical statement requires justification. When we assert that
$\sqrt{2}$ is an irrational number, we rely on a proof; the proof relies
on properties of the integers; the properties of the integers rely on
those of the natural numbers; and the natural numbers are built as an
inductive type with its own formation rules. The chain of justifications
goes *deeper*, and the natural question is this: where does it end? Does
it end at all?

If we suppose that the chain of justifications *has no end*, we obtain
an infinite regress. Each statement rests on the previous one, each
previous one on one still earlier, and so on to infinity. In such a
system *no* statement is justified: justification requires passing
through the whole chain, and an infinite chain cannot be passed through.
A regress without end is, in effect, an *absence* of justification. One
cannot point to what exactly a given particular statement stands on if
its grounds stretch away into infinity.

This result is structural, not rhetorical. Aristotle in the *Posterior
Analytics*[^1] puts it this way: for science to be possible, there must be
*starting points*—statements that we accept *not on the basis of* other
statements. Without such starting points scientific knowledge is
impossible.

In the terminology of the Methodological Introduction, the regress of
justification is a direct requirement of the *Law of Sufficient Reason*.
Sufficient Reason says: every statement requires a ground. An infinite
regress *does not satisfy* this requirement, because it presents no
*actual ground* for any single statement; it only promises a ground at
infinity. Sufficient Reason requires the ground to be *available* at a
definite point of the chain, not postponed into the unreachable. Thus
the problem of the beginning is a consequence of the very work of Logic
on the structure of justification, not the result of an arbitrary
decision that “one has to stop somewhere.”

### Three Classical Strategies of Stopping

The history of the foundations of mathematics has offered three
different answers to the problem of the beginning. Each answer accepts
the structural necessity of stopping, and each interprets its nature in
its own way.

**Euclid’s strategy: self-evident truths.**  In the *Elements*, Euclid
formulates five postulates and five common notions—statements accepted
without proof. The first postulate, “to draw a straight line from any
point to any point” (trans. T. L. Heath), is not proved; it is accepted
as *self-evident*. The nature of
this self-evidence is substantive: the postulate describes a real
property of geometric objects, one that is seen directly, without the
mediation of other statements. Euclid’s strategy presupposes that the
mathematician has *intuitive access* to the starting points: they are
“seen” to be true.

**Hilbert’s strategy: formal rules of the game.**  In the foundational
program that Hilbert developed at the beginning of the twentieth century,
axioms lose their substantive self-evidence. Axioms are *formal
statements* in a strictly defined language; their function is to set the
rules of inference, not to describe reality. Hilbert is known for the
thesis that instead of “points, straight lines, planes” one can say
“tables, chairs, beer mugs,” as long as the relations given by the axioms
are observed.
For Hilbert, the nature of the starting points is *conventional*: we
*choose* axioms on grounds of convenience, consistency, and
fruitfulness. The substantive truth-value of an individual axiom ceases
to matter; what matters is only the formal consistency of the system as a
whole.

**Brouwer’s strategy: fundamental intuition.**  Brouwer and his
followers in intuitionism rejected both Euclid’s self-evident truths and
Hilbert’s formal conventions. For Brouwer, mathematics is the *mental
activity* of a subject, unfolding in time. The primary starting point is
a *fundamental intuition*: the capacity of consciousness to distinguish
“one step” and “the next step,” thereby producing a sequence. From this
intuition the natural number series is derived, and from the natural
number series all of constructive mathematics. For Brouwer, the nature of
the starting point is *phenomenological*: it is rooted in the structure
of consciousness, neither in external reality nor in choice.

### What Is Common and What Differs

The three strategies differ in the *nature* of the starting point:
substantive intuition (Euclid), formal convention (Hilbert),
phenomenological givenness (Brouwer). Yet all three agree on one thing:
*a stopping point of justification is necessary*, and this stopping point
has a specific status that sets it apart from the statements inside the
theory. A starting point is not proved; it is relied upon.

The difference shows in how each strategy answers the question “but why
precisely these starting points?” Euclid answers: “because they are
self-evident”—but self-evidence appeals to an intuition that the
formalism itself cannot check. Hilbert answers: “because we chose
them”—but the choice seems arbitrary, and in 1931 Gödel showed that even
the consistency of the choice cannot be established from within the
system itself.[^2] Brouwer answers: “because they are rooted
in the structure of consciousness”—but the phenomenological analysis of
consciousness is not itself a mathematical method.

Each strategy thus *redistributes* the problem of the beginning rather
than *removing* it: what gets justified is the choice of a *type* of
starting point, in place of the starting point itself. The regress
persists, shifting from the level of the theory to the level of the
metatheory. And at the metatheoretical level the discussion takes other
forms—philosophical, methodological, pragmatic. Here it becomes clear
that in the three classical strategies the question of the beginning
stays *within methodology*: it concerns *how* we choose foundations,
while *what* foundations are by their ontological nature lies outside
it.

### The Aporia of the First Premise

The structure of the problem becomes especially distinct in one
classical formulation—the question of the *first premise* of reasoning.
This question was already discussed in antiquity, in a line going back
to Aristotle and his analysis of the conditions of demonstration. It is
posed thus: *how is the first premise of reasoning to be proved?*

- If we demand a proof of the first premise, it ceases to be first: it becomes intermediate, resting on a still earlier statement. The regress goes off to infinity.
- If we agree that the first premise is *not* proved, we accept something without justification. Yet the *whole* remaining edifice of the theory rests on this unjustified starting point. The rigor of the theory—the ideal of mathematics—turns out to be founded on a nonrigorous foundation.

This aporia can be written in the form of a structural statement:

$$
\begin{aligned}
    &\text{Stopping the regress requires an explanation} \\
    &\text{that cannot itself be regressive.}
  \end{aligned}
$$

This is *the formula of the problem of the beginning*. It outlines a
structural condition that any solution must satisfy; it does not
describe a solution. Any theory of foundations is obliged to say *what
exactly* works as the starting point, *what status* this starting point
has, and *how* handling it differs from handling the ordinary statements
of the theory.

### The Position of the Volume on the Problem of the Beginning

The question “where to begin?” is not a philosophical decoration placed
in front of the mathematical content of the volume. It is the *first
mathematical question*. Before we formalize number, function, or space,
before we write a single definition in Rocq, we must decide: *what do we
accept* as the condition of the very possibility of this formalization?

In this respect the volume takes a special position relative to the three
classical strategies. It neither *chooses* among them nor
*proposes* a fourth strategy as a methodological alternative on a par
with the previous ones. The present volume works on the ontological basis
established in [[Metaphysics|“Metaphysics”]], and this basis *already gives an
answer* to the question of the nature of the starting point—an answer
that goes beyond methodological choice and moves the discussion into the
ontological register.

In “Metaphysics”, the problem of the beginning is resolved
by *discovering* what is the starting point structurally, not by
*selecting* a suitable starting point (as with Euclid, Hilbert, and
Brouwer)—that is, by discovering what *is* the starting point because it
*cannot not be* the starting point, rather than because we so decided.
This discovery is the work of Logic considering the very primary
givenness of existence.

The content of this discovery is the subject of “Metaphysics”. The
volume rests on its result: the *primary
fact* of existence (written as $A = \exists$), which we shall accept in
the next section and which will serve as the starting point of the
mathematical unfolding. From the standpoint of the present volume this fact is
the *primary givenness* on which mathematical work becomes possible—not
the choice of a strategy and not the proposal of a fourth alternative.

In § 1.2 we consider this primary fact substantively: what it asserts,
why it is not empty, how it witnesses itself. In § 1.3 we show that a
direct attempt to formalize it by the means of Rocq runs into a
structural boundary—and this boundary is ontologically substantive: it
says something important about the relation between a formal system and
what the system stands on. In § 1.4 we pass to the *first
formalizable*—the act of distinction, which will prove to be the first
object that we can write in Rocq and with which the mathematical
work of the volume proper begins.

Correct handling of the boundary between the pre-formal and the
formalizable is the central methodological position of the volume, to
which we shall return many times: in Part IV (in discussing the principle
$P4$—infinity as a property of processes rather than of completed
objects), in Part X (in discussing sets as a mode of consideration), and
in Chapter 14.4 (in discussing the fundamental openness of the category
$\mathbf{ToS}$). Each time the structure of the work will be the same:
*acknowledge* the boundary, *do not attempt* to formalize what lies
beyond it, and *move on* to the nearest formalizable object on this side
of the boundary.

## The Thesis $A = \exists$: A Substantive Discussion

### The Formula and Its Reading

Having established in § 1.1 that the volume rests on the ontological
resolution of the problem of the beginning given in “Metaphysics”, we can now
present the *primary fact* itself on which mathematical work stands.

This fact is written by the formula

$$
A = \exists,
$$

read as: *something exists*. More precisely: some $A$ *is*, where $A$ is
a variable for *any something*—not a pointer to some particular
object—and $\exists$ is taken as underived: *the very fact of being*,
prior to distinguishing what exactly exists.

The formula $A = \exists$ is neither a definition
nor an axiom. It is the record of a *primary fact*, established in
“Metaphysics” and accepted by the volume as the ground of the further
unfolding. From the standpoint of the metaphysical exposition, this fact
is discovered by analyzing the work of Logic on its own primary
givenness; from the standpoint of the present volume, it is taken *as a
point of support*—that from which mathematical work can begin.

Before considering the substantive content of the formula, let us draw
two lines of demarcation: let us indicate what $A = \exists$ *is not*, in
order to avoid natural confusions.

**Not Anselm’s ontological argument.**  Anselm of Canterbury, in the
*Proslogion* (eleventh century), proposed deriving the existence of God
from the very concept of God as “that, than which nothing greater can be
conceived”: what exists in reality is greater than what exists only in the
mind; therefore, if God is that than which nothing greater can be
conceived, God exists in reality.[^3] The formula $A = \exists$ asserts
nothing of the kind. What is at issue here is the *acknowledgment* of
existence as an initial givenness, not the *derivation* of existence
from a concept. $A$ is simply *something*, about whose existence we can
speak because it already is; it is not “something whose definition
requires existence.”

**Not the Parmenidean $\tau\acute{o} \acute{\varepsilon}\sigma\tau\iota\nu$ as an ontological thesis.** 
Parmenides, in the poem *On Nature*, asserts that what is, is, and what
is not, is not[^4]. The formula $A = \exists$ can be read as akin to this
assertion, but there is an essential difference between them. Parmenides
advances an ontological thesis about the *character of being*: being is
one, motionless, without parts, opposed to becoming. The formula
$A = \exists$ says nothing about the *character* of what exists. It fixes
*only the fact that existence takes place*—without determining *what*
the existing is like or *how much* of it there is. This is a minimal
statement on which the most diverse ontological positions converge.
Parmenides, Heraclitus, Aristotle, Spinoza, Leibniz—all would agree with
$A = \exists$, parting ways in the further description of the character
of what exists.

### Why the Statement Is Not Empty

A mathematician confronted with the formula $A = \exists$ will naturally
ask: *what in it has content*? If $A$ is an arbitrary “something” and
all that is asserted is that it “exists,” is the formula not empty? Does
it not reduce to the tautology “what exists, exists”?

The substantive content of the formula comes to light when we examine
*the attempt to deny it*. Suppose we have presented the formula
$A = \exists$ and now try to assert the opposite:

$$
\neg(A = \exists)  \text{ i.e. }  \text{“nothing exists”}.
$$

This statement, however, meets a structural difficulty. Its very
utterance requires:

- The *existence* of the one who utters it (the denier).
- The *existence* of the negative statement itself (as an act of thought or speech).
- The *existence* of a context in which the denial has a definite content (the difference between “exists” and “does not exist” must be established).

Structurally: to *deny* existence, one must exist. This is a concrete
logical observation, not a rhetorical objection. If nothing existed,
there would exist neither the denial, nor the act of denial itself, nor
the one who denies—and so the statement “nothing exists” would not be
uttered, would not be thought, would not take place. Performing the act
of denial *presupposes* the very thing that is denied. This is a
*performative self-refutation*, not a formal contradiction within an
already given calculus: the very act of asserting uses what the content
of the assertion denies.

Let us write this structurally. Let $E$ be the statement “something
exists,” and $\neg E$ its denial. Schematically—as a meta-level pointer
to the structure of self-refutation, not as a formula of an already
given object language—this can be written as follows:

$$
\neg E \;\Longrightarrow\; \neg(\text{the statement } \neg E \text{ exists}),
$$

which contradicts the very fact that $\neg E$ *is asserted*. This record is not a proof of $E$ in a formal
system: at this point we do not yet have a formal system, and the record
itself mixes the object level ($\neg E$) with the meta level (the
presence of the assertion). It is an *observation* of the structural
impossibility of denial, not a theorem.

Here it becomes clear what the content of $A = \exists$ consists in.

- Structurally, $A = \exists$ is *not empty*, because its denial is *structurally impossible*. A statement whose denial is impossible *by the inner structure of denial, not by the weakness of the denier*, carries content—the content of *the ineliminability of some “is.”*
- $A = \exists$ *stands opposed to absolute nothingness*—the state that Parmenides called “is not” and that is unutterable: one can neither think nor speak of it without falling into contradiction. The content of $A = \exists$ is precisely the denial of absolute nothingness as structurally inadmissible.
- $A = \exists$ is *a presupposition of the theory*. If nothing exists, there is neither reasoning, nor a reasoner, nor an object of reasoning. The very presence of the present theory—the mathematical work of the volume, the reader, the physical text on which these words are written—is *testimony* that $A = \exists$ holds. Work that did not presuppose the primary fact of existence could not be carried on.

### Self-Witnessing as the Work of Logic

The structure we have just described—a statement whose denial leads to
contradiction through itself—has a special name in the Methodological
Introduction: *self-witnessing*.

Self-witnessing is the mode in which a statement *confirms itself
through its own nature*, with no need of external justification. In
“Metaphysics” this property is tied to the very nature of
Logic: Logic is self-witnessing through being that which grounds.

Applied to $A = \exists$, this means the following. The paradox of
denial that we considered in the previous subsection *is not* something
external to Logic. It *is* a direct manifestation of the work of Logic
at the most primary level—the work of the Law of Sufficient Reason.

Let us unfold this claim. Sufficient Reason says: every statement
requires a ground. Let us apply Sufficient Reason to the statement
“$A = \exists$”: what is the ground of this statement? And let us apply
it to the denial “$\neg(A = \exists)$”: what is the ground of the
denial? For $A = \exists$ the ground is the very fact of existence
(including the existence of the reasoning, of the assertion, and of the
one who reasons); for $\neg(A = \exists)$ *no ground can be presented*,
because any attempt to present one already uses what is denied.
Sufficient Reason *does not admit* a statement for which a ground is
*impossible*. Therefore $\neg(A = \exists)$ is excluded by the very work
of Logic, and $A = \exists$ is affirmed by that same work.

This gives the following important turn. What looked like a
“paradox”—the self-contradictoriness of denying existence—is the *normal
work of Logic* on its primary structure, not a *paradox* in the sense of
a problem awaiting resolution. Sufficient Reason, applied at the most
primary level, gives $A = \exists$ as the only statement that has a
ground, and $\neg(A = \exists)$ as a statement for which a ground is
*structurally impossible*.

In this sense $A = \exists$ is *the first thing that Logic establishes
about itself* when it turns to the question of existence, *not the first
proposition laid down by “Metaphysics”*. The self-witnessing of $A = \exists$
is Sufficient Reason at work.

### Substantive Content through the Philosophical Tradition

The formula $A = \exists$ and its self-witnessing status are not our
invention. This thesis belongs to a great
philosophical tradition, and the volume can rely on this tradition as on
*converging independent testimonies* to a structural fact.

**Parmenides.**  Already in the fifth century BCE, Parmenides formulates
the ineliminability of existence as a fundamental philosophical thesis.
In the poem *On Nature* he distinguishes two ways of inquiry: the way of
what *is and cannot not be*, and the way of what *is not and necessarily
is not*[^5].
The second way, however, proves *impassable*: “For thou canst not know
what is not—that is impossible—nor utter it”[^6]. What Parmenides calls “impassability” is precisely the
structural impossibility of denial of which we have spoken: the way of
“is not” cannot be traveled, because traveling it already requires
being.

Parmenides formulates this differently from the way we do here and
builds it into a further ontological thesis about the character of being
(one, motionless, eternal), which this volume does not fully share. But
the *structural core*—the ineliminability of existence through the
impossibility of denial—is established by Parmenides for the first time
in the history of philosophy, and it remains valid.

**Aristotle.**  In Book IV of the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle formulates
the Law of Non-Contradiction—the first principle of all that is and of
all reasoning[^7].
His argument in IV.4 shows that one who denies the Law of
Non-Contradiction cannot say anything without making use of it[^8]. The
structure of the argument is the same as in our discussion of the
self-witnessing of $A = \exists$: *the very act of denial* uses what is
denied. Aristotle applies this structure to the Law of
Non-Contradiction; our “Metaphysics” applies it at a deeper level—to the very
fact of existence.

**Descartes and Augustine.**  The early modern period gives a well-known
formulation of self-witnessing in the context of doubt. In the
*Meditations*, Descartes reaches the point at which radical doubt runs
into its own limit: even supposing that I am deceived about everything,
I cannot doubt that I doubt, and hence that I exist as one who
doubts[^9]. Twelve centuries before Descartes, Augustine formulates the
same discovery in the form *si fallor, sum*—“if I am deceived, I
am”[^10].

Structurally, Descartes’s *cogito* and Augustine’s *si fallor* are
applications of $A = \exists$ to a *particular* case—the existence of
the one who thinks. They show that the ineliminability of existence
manifests itself at the individual level as well: an individual cannot
deny their own existence without falling into contradiction, just as
Logic cannot deny existence as such. The volume does not use the *cogito*
as an independent ground (that is already a step toward subjectivity,
which has a specific place in “Metaphysics”), but it
acknowledges the *cogito* as *converging testimony* to the same
structural fact.

**Heidegger.**  In *Being and Time* (1927) and later works, Heidegger
introduces the *ontological difference* between *being* (*Sein*) and
*beings* (*Seiendes*)[^11]. *A being* is any concrete “something”: a
thing, a fact, an event. *Being* is *that something is*, *the very fact
of the being of anything*, irreducible to any concrete being.

The formula $A = \exists$ is closer precisely to the second—to being in
Heidegger’s sense. $A$ is left indeterminate (any something); $\exists$
fixes *the very fact of being*. This agrees with Heidegger’s program of
a *return to the question of being*, and “Metaphysics”
largely follows this program. The present volume, resting on the ontology of
“Metaphysics”, inherits this distinction without unfolding it to its full
Heideggerian depth.

**Wittgenstein.**  The *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, in § 6.44,
gives a formulation especially close to the content of $A = \exists$:
“*Not how the world is, is the mystical, but that it is*”[^12].
Wittgenstein separates the *how* of the world (the subject matter of
science) from the *that* of the world (the subject matter—in his
view—of mystical contemplation; philosophy, for its part, must remain
silent). The volume does not follow Wittgenstein in his conclusion about
silence, but it fixes the same distinction: $A = \exists$ asserts
precisely the *that* of the world, not the *how*.

This convergence of testimonies—Parmenides, Aristotle, Descartes,
Augustine, Heidegger, Wittgenstein, so different in their epochs,
languages, and methodological commitments—upon one and the same
structural fact testifies to its *ineliminability*. $A = \exists$ is
neither our invention nor a thesis on which only
“Metaphysics” insists. It is a structural discovery to which the
philosophical tradition returns at every major stage of its development,
reformulating it in the language of its time.

### $A = \exists$ and Mathematical Work

A natural objection, for which we must be prepared: “All this is
philosophy. What does $A = \exists$ have to do with the mathematical work
of the volume proper?”

A direct and essential one.

Mathematics as we know it *already rests* on $A = \exists$ at the most
basic level—in the work of the existential quantifier $\exists$. When we
write

$$
\exists x \in ℕ.\; x > 0,
$$

we assert that some natural number greater than zero *exists*. The
quantifier $\exists$ itself, however, is not *defined* within the theory
that uses it. It works at the level of the theory’s very *language*, not
at the level of its content. No definition of $\exists$ can be given by
the means of a theory that does not use $\exists$ or a construction
equivalent to it—we would run into circularity.

In terms of the type theory CIC, on which Rocq is built and on which our
formal apparatus works, two levels must be distinguished here. A type
can be *formed*—that is, be a correct object of the language, built by
the type-formation rules; this does not yet mean that it is *inhabited*.
The inhabitedness of a type means the presence of a *term* of that
type—an object having the given type. Under the Curry–Howard
correspondence, for propositions inhabitedness corresponds to the
presence of a proof. These two levels do not coincide: a type can be
impeccably formed and yet empty—such, for example, is the type `False`
in `Prop`, a correct object of the language that has not a single
inhabitant.

The basic case of an inhabited type: the type $ℕ$ of natural numbers is
inhabited because it has *constructors*—$0$, $S(0)$, $S(S(0))$, and so
on. Each constructor produces a concrete term of type $ℕ$ and thereby
confirms that the type $ℕ$ is *not empty*—that natural numbers *are*.

Therefore the formal inhabitedness of a type is *not identical* to the
first principle $A = \exists$. Inhabitedness is already an *internal*
mode of existence—the existence of a term within a working type system.
The first principle itself lies *lower*: it is the condition for there
being language, types, terms, and formation rules at all—and the very
difference between formed and inhabited, between inhabited and empty.
Let us look closely: the inhabitedness of the type $ℕ$ rests on the fact
that the constructors $0$, $S$ themselves *are*, given in the type
system. And the very fact that they are given—that they can be written,
mentioned, applied—is a manifestation of *the same primary fact*
$A = \exists$. If nothing existed, the constructors would not exist
either; if the constructors did not exist, there would be no type $ℕ$;
if there were no type $ℕ$, there would be no mathematics of numbers.

This does not mean that $A = \exists$ is an “axiom of arithmetic.” It
means that $A = \exists$ *is at a level below the axioms of
arithmetic*—at the level of the conditions of the very possibility of
writing the axioms down. The Peano axioms formulate *properties* of the
natural numbers; they *presuppose* that the natural numbers are. Their
“are” is not introduced by the Peano axioms; the axioms accept it as the
condition of the very possibility of formulating them.

So it is at every level of mathematics. A group exists as a structure
because its elements *are* and the group operation *is*. A topological
space exists because points and open sets *are*. A category exists
because its objects and morphisms *are*. At every level $A = \exists$
works as *the condition of the very possibility* of speaking about the
mathematical objects of that level.

This work of $A = \exists$ remains *implicit* in ordinary mathematical
practice: the mathematician uses the quantifier $\exists$, type
constructors, and notations for objects without asking what “existence”
is in general. The volume makes this work *explicit* at the very first
stage. Having acknowledged $A = \exists$ as the primary fact, we obtain a
ground for the whole further unfolding: we *unfold* the naturals, the
reals, groups, and spaces from that basic “is” which already works at
the ontological level, rather than *postulating* their existence.

In this sense the formula $A = \exists$ is not an “introduction to
mathematics” in the ordinary sense of the word. It is *the presentation
of what mathematics already stands on*, and the condition without which
its unfolding would lack a ground.

Having established the primary fact $A = \exists$ substantively, having
considered its converging testimonies in the philosophical tradition,
and having traced its connection with ordinary mathematical practice, we
pass to the natural question: *can this fact be written in a formal
system*? Can $A = \exists$ be formulated as a statement in Rocq? The
next section is devoted to this question.

## The Boundary of Formalization

### The Natural Attempt

Having established in § 1.2 the primary fact $A = \exists$ as the
substantive ground of the volume, we face a natural question that any
mathematician accustomed to working in a strictly formal system must
ask: *how is this fact to be written in Rocq*?

In other words: if $A = \exists$ is the statement on which mathematical
work stands, should not the first line of our repository be precisely
its record? Should there not be, say, a definition

```coq
Axiom A_exists : True.
```

or some other axiom of this kind, fixing $A = \exists$ as a formal
statement to which all further theorems can refer?

This move seems natural. It corresponds to the usual practice of
mathematical formalization: if there is a statement on which the theory
rests without proof, let us write it down as an axiom. Hilbert’s
foundational program, which we examined in § 1.1.2, requires exactly
this—to define a formal system as a set of axioms and rules of
inference. If $A = \exists$ is the ground of the volume, it must have a
place in the formal system of Rocq in which this volume is written.

This section shows that this natural move *does not work*, and
discovers why it does not. The discovery is no criticism of the method;
on the contrary, it *confirms* the ontology of “Metaphysics” and clarifies
the status of $A = \exists$ as a primary fact rather than a postulate.

### What the Entry `Axiom A_exists : True` Does

Let us examine carefully what exactly happens if we add to the system
the entry

```coq
Axiom A_exists : True.
```

To understand this, we need to recall what `True` is in CIC, on which
Rocq is built.

`True` is an inductive type with a single constructor:

```coq
Inductive True : Prop :=
  | I : True.
```

The type `True` is *inhabited*: it has the inhabitant `I`. This means
that the statement `True` in Rocq is *already provable without any
axioms*, because the inhabitant `I` provides a direct witness. At any
point of any proof one can write `exact I` and close any subgoal of the
form `True`.

What does the axiom `Axiom A_exists : True` add to the system CIC?
Technically: the name `A_exists`, referring to a witness of the type
`True`. What new information does it give the system? *None*. The type
`True` already had the witness `I`; adding a name for *the very same*
(or an equivalent) witness extends neither the set of theorems nor the
capabilities of the system.

This is the first move in discovering the boundary. The attempt to
formalize $A = \exists$ through the trivial type `True` *degenerates*.
The entry either *tells nothing new* (if it is formally equivalent to
what is already in the system) or *introduces a name without content*
(if it claims something that `True` does not contain, while formally
having the same type).

### The Attempt through `Inhabited`

A natural objection: “`True` is an unsuitable choice. Let us try another
formalization—say, through the type class `Inhabited`.”

In Rocq and in the standard library `Coq.Logic.Inhabited` there is a
type class asserting that a certain type is *inhabited*, that is, has at
least one inhabitant:

```coq
Class Inhabited (X : Type) : Prop :=
  inhabited_witness : X.
```

(The exact definition in the standard library differs slightly, using
`inhabits`, but this is immaterial for our discussion.)

If we wanted to formalize “something exists” through this construction,
the attempt would look like this:

```coq
Axiom A_exists_inhabited :
  exists (X : Type), Inhabited X.
```

or, equivalently,

```coq
Axiom A_exists_inhabited :
  exists (X : Type) (x : X), True.
```

Here we have tried to write the statement “there exists a type that has
an inhabitant”—in other words, “something exists” in formal form.

This move does not work either—but in a way different from the first.

**In substance.**  In the system CIC we already have, for example, the
type `unit` with its single inhabitant `tt`, or the type `nat` with the
inhabitants `0, 1, 2, …`—and so on. The statement “there exists a type
that has an inhabitant” is *already provable* in CIC, without any
axioms:

```coq
Theorem some_type_inhabited :
  exists (X : Type) (x : X), True.
Proof.
  exists unit. exists tt. exact I.
Qed.
```

Declaring this statement an axiom is redundant: it is provable as it
stands. If instead we try to declare as an axiom precisely “the
existence of something *prior to* particular types” (without presenting
`unit` or `nat` as a witness), we will need *something that is a type*
and yet *is not any of the types already defined*. There is no such
object in CIC.

**Structurally.**  To prove `Inhabited X` for some `X`, one needs a
*concrete inhabitant*. Otherwise the proof is impossible. But if
confirming the existence of `X` requires its inhabitant, and inhabitants
are objects that *already* exist in the type system, then we have
returned to the same question: *where* do these objects in the type
system come from? The answer: they appear *through the work of the type
system itself, which rests on CIC, which in turn rests on the fact that
existence *is**.

This is the same circularity as in the first attempt, only more
explicit. To formalize $A = \exists$ through `Inhabited`, one must
present a type with an inhabitant. But *presenting* a type with an
inhabitant *already uses* the fact that types and inhabitants *are*.
Formalizing $A = \exists$ requires $A = \exists$ as the condition of its
very possibility.

### The Structure of the Boundary

Two attempts—through `True` and through `Inhabited`—are enough to see
the structure of the discovery. One could try several more
variants—through `ex` in the standard library, through more general type classes,
through higher sorts of types—but in every case *one and the same*
structure will be found:

- Either the entry is formally equivalent to what is already in CIC *without any axiom*, and adding it is redundant.
- Or the entry requires for its proof the *presentation* of a concrete existing object, *and this presentation is exactly what we were trying to formulate as an axiom*.

In both cases $A = \exists$ *cannot be written* as an independent
statement of the formal system. This is not because we have failed to
find the right formulation. It is because *the formal system itself
already works on the ground of $A = \exists$*, and the attempt to write
this ground from inside the system runs into a structural circularity:
the ground is *the condition of the very possibility* of the system; it
*is not* an object of the system.

Let us write this structure formally. Let $S$ be a formal system with
language $L(S)$—the set of well-formed formulas that $S$ is able to
express. A statement $\phi$ is written in the system $S$ if
$\phi \in L(S)$.

**Claim.** 

Within an already given formal system $S$, every formula that claims to
express $A = \exists$ as the ground of the system itself falls into one
of two situations:

1. it becomes an ordinary internal formula or axiom of the system $S$—and thereby already presupposes the language, rules, and objects of $S$;
2. it degenerates into the trivial inhabitedness or provability of what $S$ already has.

Consequently, $A = \exists$ cannot be an *internal ground* of the system
$S$ in the same sense in which an ordinary axiom is an internal ground
of a theory. It can only be a *pre-formal condition of the possibility*
of formalization itself.

We do not prove this claim formally: in the strict sense, no such proof
exists, because what is at issue is a metatheoretical observation, not a
theorem within one system. We *discover* it through the analysis of
concrete attempts (as was done in the subsections “What the Entry Axiom
A_exists : True Does” and “The Attempt through Inhabited”) and through
structural reasoning about the status of formal systems as technical
implementations of an already-working Logic (see the Methodological
Introduction, the section on Logic as the structure of being).

**The boundary is ontologically substantive.**  The thesis of the section “The
Structure of the Boundary” should not be read as a defect of the method
or a limitation of formalization. It is the *discovery* of a structural
property of what we are trying to write down. The boundary says:

> $A = \exists$ is *not an object formalizable within a formal
> system*; it is *the condition of the very possibility of a formal system*.

In other words: the attempt to write $A = \exists$ in Rocq is like an
attempt to paint the whole room in which the painter stands from inside
the picture itself—without stepping beyond the canvas. The image could
include a mirror reflecting part of the room with the painter, but both
the mirror and its observer ultimately remain *outside* the picture
itself. Internal formal means cannot encompass the condition of their
own possibility.

In the terms of “Metaphysics”: Logic cannot be written in
a formal system, because a formal system *is* a technical implementation
of Logic, not Logic itself; $A = \exists$ cannot be written in a formal
system, because a formal system *works* on the ground of the fact of
existence without defining it.

### Connection with the Incompleteness Theorems

Here one important clarification is in order, especially for a reader
familiar with Gödel’s results. The thesis of the section “The Structure of the
Boundary” *is not* Gödel’s incompleteness theorem, but it is
*structurally akin* to what Gödel showed.

Gödel’s first theorem[^13] establishes that in any consistent formal
system $S$ capable of expressing arithmetic *there exist true statements
about the natural numbers that are not provable in $S$*. These
statements belong to the language $L(S)$—that is, they are *expressible*
in $S$—but their proof lies beyond $S$.

The thesis of the section “The Structure of the Boundary” is different. It speaks not
of the *unprovability* of a formula in the system but of the fact that
$A = \exists$ cannot be an *internal ground* of the system. Every formula
claiming to express $A = \exists$ as the ground of $S$ either becomes an
ordinary internal formula of $S$ (and thereby already presupposes the
system) or degenerates into a triviality. Here the boundary lies between
what the system *states* within itself and what is *the condition of its
own possibility*, rather than between the expressible and the provable.

The difference can be pictured as follows:

- Gödel: within a formal system there are expressible but *unprovable* formulas. The boundary lies between the *expressible* and the *provable*.
- The present volume: for $A = \exists$ the boundary lies *earlier*—between *what is expressible by the formal system* and what is *the condition of the possibility* of this formal system. The boundary lies between the *expressible* and the *presupposition of expressibility*.

These boundaries are not identical, but they belong to one structural
type: *both* are discoveries that a formal system has a *limit*—something
that lies in its relations to its own ground rather than in its content.
Gödel showed a limit within the system; here we fix a limit *at the
entrance* to the system, before the first axiom is written.

In the Methodological Introduction we said that formal systems are
*technical implementations* of Logic. Gödel adds: even as technical
implementations, they are *incomplete*—they cannot encompass everything
that Logic establishes at their level. The thesis of the section “The Structure of the
Boundary” says: they cannot encompass the conditions of their own
possibility either. Both discoveries clarify the status of a formal
system as a *means*, not as a *ground*.

### Objections and Replies

The natural objections to the thesis “The Structure of the Boundary” are
worth stating explicitly.

**Objection 1.**  “Perhaps we simply have not found the right
formalization. There must be a better formulation in which $A = \exists$
is expressible without circularity.”

*Reply.* Every concrete attempt displays *the very same* structure—either
degeneration into triviality or a return to the condition. This is a
consequence of the *nature* of $A = \exists$ as a condition of the formal
system, not an imperfection of one particular formulation. It is like
the impossibility of defining the operation of equality through
equality, or of deriving the rules of inference from those very rules:
the structure does not allow it.

Moreover, the argument in the subsection “The Structure of the Boundary”
was *structural*, not tied to a particular formulation: to express
$A = \exists$ in $L(S)$, one must either use the inhabitants of the
system (but their existence is exactly what we are trying to write down)
or introduce an entry formally equivalent to truths the system already
has (and then the entry tells nothing). No “new, better formulation”
gets around this structural dilemma.

**Objection 2.**  “But Hilbert’s program works—we postulate axioms and
develop the theory. Why does this not work here?”

*Reply.* Hilbert’s program postulates axioms for *particular* objects:
the Peano axioms for the natural numbers, the axioms of geometry for
points and lines, the group axioms for elements and an operation. These
axioms are inside the formal system, fixing *properties* of definite
objects. They work *within* an already-accepted $A = \exists$; they do
not *axiomatize existence as such*.

This is easy to see. The Peano axiom “there exists $0 \in ℕ$” is the
*presentation* of the particular object $0$ as an element of the type
$ℕ$, given that both $0$ and $ℕ$ *are* as objects of the language; it is
not an axiomatization of existence. The axiom works at the level of the
*properties* of $ℕ$, not at the level of the *presence* of $ℕ$. The present volume
does not reject Hilbert’s program at its own level; it points out that
*below* the axiomatic level lies the condition of the very possibility
of these axioms, and that this condition has a different status.

**Objection 3.**  “ZFC has the axiom of the existence of the empty set:
$\exists x. \forall y. y \notin x$. This is a *direct* axiomatization of
existence. Why does it work there but not here?”

*Reply.* The empty set axiom in ZFC axiomatizes *the existence of a
particular set with a definite property* (being empty). It works within
the already-accepted language of first-order predicate logic. All the
linguistic elements of this language *are*—they are *presupposed* before
the axiom. Before the empty set axiom is written down, ZFC already
*works on the ground* of the facts that (a) there is a predicate
language, (b) there are formulas of this language, (c) there are
variables and the existential quantifier, (d) there is the membership
relation $\in$, (e) there is a mode of reasoning about sets as objects of
the theory.

The symbol $\emptyset$ itself appears only *after* this—as the notation
for an object whose existence and uniqueness are established within the
theory; it is not a primitive symbol of the language. All the listed
items are manifestations of $A = \exists$ *at a level below the axiom*.
The empty set axiom axiomatizes the existence of *one particular set*;
it does not axiomatize existence *in general*. The volume does not
criticize ZFC for this: ZFC *makes no claim* to axiomatize existence in
general, and it works correctly within its own framework. We merely
point out that the question of *existence in general*, unlike the
existence of the empty set, has a different status and is not subject
to axiomatization in the ordinary sense.

**Objection 4.**  “Then philosophy is not mathematics. Perhaps there is
no need to introduce any form of $A = \exists$ at the very beginning;
perhaps we should simply start from particular axioms, as ordinary
mathematics does?”

*Reply.* This is indeed a possible approach—it is how ordinary
mathematics works. The present volume does otherwise *for two reasons*.

First, ordinary mathematics, without asking about $A = \exists$,
*implicitly* accepts it at every step. The volume makes this explicit in
order to *present* the ground that works in any case, not in order to
change ordinary work.

Second, and more important: the next chapter (Chapter 2) unfolds the
*first formalizable* object—the act of distinction. This object
*implicitly* rests on $A = \exists$, like any formal construction. But
in Chapter 3 the act of distinction will become the ground for *deriving*
the laws of logic as theorems of Rocq. For this structure to work—for it
to be clear what the act of distinction itself stands on and in what
relation it stands to Logic (which, as we know from the Methodological
Introduction, is ontologically primary)—$A = \exists$ must be fixed
*before* formalization. Otherwise the act of distinction would appear as
the “first principle” in the formal sense, which is ontologically wrong:
it is the *first formalizable*, not the *first as such*.

The distinction between the *first as such* ($A = \exists$, pre-formal)
and the *first formalizable* (the act of distinction, written in Rocq) is
the central methodological work of Chapter 1 and Chapter 2. The volume
makes this distinction explicit so that it works throughout all the
subsequent parts.

Having established the structural boundary of the formalization of
$A = \exists$, considered its substance, and answered the natural
objections, we pass to the last section of Chapter 1—a description of
how the volume handles this boundary and of the step that follows its
acknowledgment.

## What to Do with the Boundary: The Transition to the Act of Distinction

### Not to Explain, but to Move On

Having established in § 1.3 the boundary of the formalization of
$A = \exists$ and discovered that this boundary is ontologically
substantive, we face a natural methodological question: *what next*?

Several answers to this question are possible. One path is to *unfold*
the discussion of the boundary. Whole volumes could be devoted to
analyzing what lies on the other side: the relation between Logic and a
formal system, the nature of the condition, the asymmetry between the
expressible and the presuppositions of expressibility. In the twentieth
century Heidegger, Levinas, and Derrida devoted substantial texts to this
work, and each of them is a philosophical tradition of its own, with its
own terminology, methodology, and subtleties.

The volume does not take this path. This is not because the path is
unimportant or fruitless; the task of the volume is simply different.
The present volume is a mathematical unfolding on the ontological basis
established in “Metaphysics”. The detailed discussion of the boundary between
the pre-formal and the formal belongs to the metaphysical exposition and
receives its full development there. The volume *inherits* the
result of this analysis and moves on to its own task.

The strategy of the volume with respect to the boundary is as follows:

> Acknowledge the boundary of formalization, do not try to *shrink* or
> *bypass* it, and move on to the nearest formalizable object on this
> side of the boundary.

This is a working method, not a philosophical digression. The
acknowledgment of the boundary was made in § 1.3 (the Proposition “The
Structure of the Boundary”). Now we must take the second step: *find*
the object that, resting on $A = \exists$, is the *first* thing the volume
can write formally—that is, formalize in Rocq and work with as an object
of mathematical theory.

In other words, the question changes. We no longer ask *what is
$\exists$?*—a question that leads into “Metaphysics”. We
ask *what is the first formalizable after $\exists$?*—a question that
leads into Chapter 2 and beyond.

This shift—from *ontology* (what is) to *operational ontology* (how to
work with it)—is characteristic of the whole volume. The volume
does not stop at every boundary for a substantive analysis of it; it
*fixes* boundaries where they occur and *passes on* to working with what
lies on this side of the boundary. This strategy will recur many
times—in Part IV (the boundary separating completed actual infinity as
an object from infinity as a property of processes; the principle $P4$),
in Part X (the boundary of “the set as a primary object”),
and in Chapter 14.4 (the boundary of “the universe of everything,” the
openness of the hierarchy). Each time the structure of the work will be
the same: *acknowledge*, *do not shrink*, *move on*.

### Tracing the Conditions: Applying Sufficient Reason to $A = \exists$

To find the first formalizable after $A = \exists$, we neither *choose*
it from a list of possible options nor *postulate* a convenient object.
We *trace* the work of Logic on the very fact $A = \exists$ and fix what
this work discovers. This is a discovering move, in keeping with the
very nature of $A = \exists$ as a fact (not an axiom); to whoever traces
it attentively, something opens up that was there all along—*already
working* in the very primary givenness of existence, neither chosen nor
assigned.

The course of the discovery is as follows. We have $A = \exists$ as a
primary fact. We apply to it the *Law of Sufficient Reason*: what are
the conditions that make this fact possible? What *must be* for the
existence of anything to take place? This is not a rhetorical question;
Sufficient Reason is the work of Logic, and Logic by its nature leaves
no fact without a ground.

The detailed discovery of these conditions is the task of
“Metaphysics”. For the present volume it is enough to rest on its result and
briefly repeat what is essential.

“Metaphysics” shows that for the existence of anything to take place, *there
must be a structure* in which the distinguishable *would be*
distinguishable. Namely:

- What exists must be *identical to itself*—otherwise it would not be a determinate *something* but would merge into the indistinguishable. *The Law of Identity*.
- What exists cannot at once *be* and *not be* in the same respect—otherwise the very difference between “is” and “is not” would lose its meaning, and $A = \exists$ would assert nothing. *The Law of Non-Contradiction*.
- The distinguishable must *unfold* in a structure where one thing is the condition for another—otherwise all distinctions would arise simultaneously and without relation to one another, which is chaos, not structure. *The Law of Order*.

These are *conditions* that Sufficient Reason, applied to $A = \exists$,
discovers to be *necessary*—*not axioms we have arrived at* and *not
rules we have chosen*. Without any one of them the existence of anything
is impossible.

Derived from Identity and Non-Contradiction is the *Law of Excluded
Middle*: if something *is* (Identity) and cannot be at once itself and
not itself (Non-Contradiction), then between “is” and “is not” *there is
no third*—nothing that would be neither the one nor the other. In
“Metaphysics” this law is recognized as derived; in the
formalization of the present volume, as we recall from the Methodological
Introduction, it receives the status of an independent $L3$ for
technical reasons of Rocq.

These laws—Identity, Non-Contradiction, Excluded Middle (derived),
Order—are *the structure of Logic itself*, which makes the fact
$A = \exists$ possible. They are ontologically *primary*: they do not
*follow* from existence; they are *its conditions*. “Metaphysics” unfolds
this picture in full; the volume accepts it and passes on to its properly
mathematical task.

*The position of Sufficient Reason.* Sufficient Reason does not enter
this list as one more law. Sufficient Reason is a *meta-law*, the very
action of Logic through which the listed laws are discovered as
conditions. Applying Sufficient Reason to $A = \exists$ is precisely the
move by which the other laws were revealed; Sufficient Reason works
*over* them, not *alongside* them. This agrees with the thesis of
“Metaphysics”: Sufficient Reason is grounding itself, not one
of the grounded. Later we shall see that in the act of distinction, too,
Sufficient Reason acts as the meta-structure in which all four fields of
the record work, rather than as one of its conditions.

Here it is important to distinguish the ontological and formal levels at
once, so that no terminological ambiguity arises later. In the
ontological exposition, Sufficient Reason is a meta-law—the very action
of Logic, which demands a ground. In the formal record of the volume,
this action has several distinct technical traces, and they must not be
confused. In the structure `Distinction` it shows itself as the
self-grounding of distinction: from the positive side follows the
negation of the negative one. In the axiomatic layer it shows itself
differently—as the requirement of a definite witness for individual
existence. And throughout the volume it works as the general
methodological principle of an explicit ground. These technical traces
neither *exhaust* Sufficient Reason nor are *identified* with it: each
expresses only one working side of the meta-law within the formal
system. When below we speak of the law $L4$ as a theorem of the
formalization, what is meant is precisely one of these traces, not
Sufficient Reason in its ontological fullness.

### The Simplest Manifestation of the Laws: The Act of Distinction

Having established that the conditions of existence are the laws of
Logic, we can now pose a mathematically precise question: *what is the
simplest structural movement in which these laws manifest themselves
jointly and which is accessible to a formal record*?

This question is a question of *ascertainment*, not a question of
choice. The laws of Logic work always and everywhere there is existence;
but the simplest structural movement in which they manifest themselves
*as a single whole* has a definite form. This form we discover; we do
not invent it.

The simplest structural movement is the *division* of some “is” into a
positive and a negative side. Let us examine it carefully:

- The positive side is what *is* in the sense of the division being made. The statement `positive`. The Law of Identity applies to it: the positive side is identical to itself—`positive` is `positive`.
- The negative side is what the positive *is not*. The statement `negative`.
- The Law of Non-Contradiction requires that the positive and the negative cannot hold at once. `exclusive`: $\neg(\texttt{positive} \wedge \texttt{negative})$.
- Between the positive and the negative there is no third; together they exhaust the possible sides of the division. `exhaustive`: $\texttt{positive} \vee \texttt{negative}$. (This is Excluded Middle, derived from Identity and Non-Contradiction in the ontology of “Metaphysics” but written in the Rocq formalization as an independent field.)

Over all this works the *Law of Sufficient Reason* as a meta-law.
Sufficient Reason is *the very action of Logic* in which distinction
takes place; it is not one of the four laws listed. Let us unfold this
claim in substance.

Without Sufficient Reason, the division of what exists into the positive
and the negative would be an act without a ground—a random cut in
something that has no cause in itself to be cut in just this way.
Sufficient Reason *makes* distinction *possible* by requiring that for
something to be distinguished there must be a *ground* for the
distinction—a contentful side that *is*, and a side that *is not*,
resting on the first. Each field of the record of the act of distinction
works not *by itself* but through Sufficient Reason:

- The field `positive` has content because there is a ground for asserting it; Sufficient Reason requires that this content be presented.
- The field `negative` has meaning because there is a ground for the negation: precisely *this*, and nothing else, is posited as absent.
- The field `exclusive` requires a ground for exclusivity: *why exactly can these two sides not coincide*? Sufficient Reason requires that exclusivity rest on the structure of the division itself rather than be an arbitrary condition.
- The field `exhaustive` requires a ground for exhaustiveness: *why exactly do these two sides together cover everything*? Sufficient Reason requires that exhaustiveness be a manifestation of the structure rather than an accidental addition.

In this sense Sufficient Reason is the *meta-law* of structure—the law by
which the other laws work. Each of the four fields of the act of
distinction works through Sufficient Reason; and the act of distinction
itself, as a structural whole, is *the work of Sufficient Reason on the
simplest possible content*—on the very fact that something is, in which
no further distinctions have yet been made.

 Thus the act of distinction is *the simultaneous
manifestation of all the laws of Logic*: of Identity and
Non-Contradiction in the content of the fields; of Excluded Middle in
their jointness; of Sufficient Reason in the very work of distinction as
a grounded act. None of the laws can be removed without the structure
falling apart: remove Identity, and `positive` ceases to be determinate;
remove Non-Contradiction, and `exclusive` loses its force; remove
Excluded Middle, and `exhaustive` becomes arbitrary; remove Sufficient
Reason, and the act of distinction itself loses its ground, becoming
random.

This is the *minimal structure* in which the work of Logic is expressed
in full. There is nothing smaller: anything smaller would not contain all
the laws; anything larger would already contain additional structures
not justified at this level (relations between divisions, properties of
each side, a hierarchy of divisions—all of this will come in later
chapters).

This structural movement we call the *act of distinction*. And it is
precisely this act that the volume *discovers* as the first
formalizable: it ascertains this act as the simplest manifestation of
all the laws of Logic (including Sufficient Reason) accessible to a
record in Rocq; it neither chooses nor assigns it.

### In What Sense the Act of Distinction Is “First”

The word “first,” applied to the act of distinction, requires precise
clarification, since it works in several senses at once, and it is
important not to confuse them.

**First in the ontology: generating time.**  In [[Metaphysics|“Metaphysics”]], time begins with the first act: “The first act is the first moment: time begins.” Before the first act, Logic and the Source exist, but there are no events; accordingly, there is no sequence; and there is no time either. Time *is born* in the very act of distinction—as the structure of the sequence of acts that starts from the first distinction.

In this sense the act of distinction is *temporally first* in the most
exact sense of the word: it *generates* time itself as a structure. Not
“first there was one distinction, then another in time”—but *in this
first act time begins to be*, and all subsequent distinctions are placed
along this structure of sequence.

The present volume does not unfold this ontology in its fullness: that is the work of [[Metaphysics|“Metaphysics”]], and a reader who wishes to obtain it should turn to it. But the volume
*inherits* the result: the act of distinction is the act through which
time itself becomes possible as a structure, not one of many events
occurring in time.

**First in the record of the volume: structurally basic.**  In the
mathematical work of the volume the act of distinction plays the role of
the *first formalizable*: the first object that is written in Rocq and
from which what follows is *derived*. This role is *structural*, not
temporal (it asserts neither the order in which the chapters were
written nor the order of the author’s research): the act of distinction
lies at the foundation of the deductive chain, and every
subsequent object of the volume (number systems, functions, spaces,
categories) unfolds from it through a sequence of formalizations.

This structural role agrees with temporal primacy in the ontology:
precisely because the act of distinction generates time itself as a
sequence, it also turns out to be that with which any structural chain
*begins*. Not “because we ordered it so,” but because without it there
would be no sequence—neither ontologically (time) nor structurally
(deduction).

**First in the record in Rocq: technically prior.**  In the repository
`theory-of-systems-coq` the record `Distinction` historically appeared
earlier than the other main structures and serves as the basis for
further definitions. This is a technical fact about the order of files
and theorems in the repository, with no claim to an ontological
statement.

All three senses of “first”—ontological (generates time), structural
(basic for deduction), technical (priority in the repository)—agree with
one another, but they have different grounds and work at different
levels. When we say that the act of distinction is *the first
formalizable in the volume*, we use all three senses at once, since in
our case they coincide on one object.

The properties of the act of distinction as the first formalizable—that
it rests on $A = \exists$ without being identified with it; that it is
formalizable in CIC; that it is minimal; that further structures are
derivable from it (the laws of logic as theorems, typing, the
co-definition of positive/negative)—are *observable characteristics* of
the discovered object. They do not *guide a choice* (because there was
no choice); they *record what the discovered object has*.

### A Preview of the Act of Distinction

The first formalizable in the volume is the act of distinction. It will be
introduced and unfolded in Chapter 2; here we merely outline what
Chapter 2 will contain.

The *act of distinction* is formalized in CIC as a record with four
fields:

```coq
Record Distinction := mkDistinction {
  positive    : Prop;
  negative    : Prop;
  exclusive   : ~(positive /\ negative);
  exhaustive  : positive \/ negative
}.
```

Here `positive` and `negative` are two statements representing the
positive and the negative sides of the distinction; `exclusive` is the
statement of their mutual exclusion; `exhaustive` is the statement of
their joint exhaustiveness (between the positive and the negative sides
there is no “third” in the form of an empty space).

In substance, `negative` is read as the negative side of the
positive—what the positive *is not*. Technically, however, the field
`negative` in the record `Distinction` is left an *independent* field of
type `Prop`: the record does not require the syntactic equality
`negative = ~ positive`. The connection between `positive` and
`negative` is given by the proofs of `exclusive` and `exhaustive`, not by
an equality. The canonical case is the construction `distinction_of`,
which builds a distinction from a proposition $P$ by choosing the
negative side equal to $\neg P$; but the general record `Distinction` is
wider: it keeps a broader structural form, leaving the negative side an
explicit field of the act. This distinction between the general and the
canonical case will be important in Chapter 2.

The type `Distinction` is a record with four fields, and each of its
instances is a *particular* distinction, given by particular `positive`
and `negative` together with confirmations of their exclusivity and
exhaustiveness. This is the first *explicitly constructive* object of
the volume: a formal construction that Rocq accepts into its language and
that one can work with—not a mention, not a description, not a
reference.

Chapter 2 will consider the nature of each of the four fields; the
reason for typing in `Prop` (rather than in `bool`—a central
methodological position connected with logical realism); the
construction `distinction_of`, which builds the corresponding
distinction from any proposition $P$; and the minimality of the record
(the necessity of each field).

Chapter 3 will show that the laws of logic $L1, L2, L3, L4$ are
*derived* from the structure `Distinction` as theorems of Rocq about the
properties of this record. The Law of Order $L5$ will be included in the
unified scheme of laws, but it is not extracted from the four fields of
`Distinction` as directly as $L1$–$L4$: its formal carrier is a separate
structure of levels, `Level`, with the relation `<<`. Therefore $L5$ is
the next layer of the formalization, connecting the act of distinction
with the hierarchy; its status will be discussed separately.

The later chapter on the co-definition of the positive and the negative
will unfold *co-definition*: one cannot build a `Distinction` having
only one of the sides. The positive and the negative *co-arise* in the
act itself, and this co-arising structure is the basis for the further
understanding of dualities in the volume.

The later chapter on the axiomatic layer of Rocq will spell out the
technical axioms on which work with `Distinction` rests (`classic` and
`L4_witness`), and will unfold for the first time the mechanism that
replaces the Axiom of Choice—the transfer of status through E/R/R with
the use of $L4$ and $L5$.

Chapter 7 will gather the ontological positions of the volume, to which
it will return throughout all subsequent parts.

### What We Have Gained

Before closing § 1.4 (and with it Chapter 1), let us sum up what has been
achieved, since this result matters not only for Chapter 1 but for all
the further work of the volume.

The boundary of the formalization of $A = \exists$ has been neither
*discarded* nor *ignored*. It has been *fixed* where it structurally
lies—between the formal system and the condition of its possibility.
This is the correct way to handle the boundary: *do not try* to write it
down (which, as we saw in § 1.3, leads to degeneration or circularity),
and *do not pretend* that it is absent (which would be carelessness, a
hidden appeal to what has been denied recognition).

Within the formal system, the volume has a concrete ground: the act of
distinction as the first formalizable. From it are derived the laws of
logic (Chapter 3), typing (Chapter 2.4), and the ontology of the
positive-negative (Chapter 4). The further work of the volume receives a
firm foundation in the form of a formal object on which it rigorously
stands.

At the same time $A = \exists$ remains a *pre-formal ground* to which
the volume refers—through the Methodological Introduction, through the
discussion in Chapter 1, through the returns to it in later
parts—but which it does not *appropriate* into its formal language. This
is respect for the structural boundary. What is ontologically primary
(Logic, $\exists$, Sufficient Reason as the action of Logic) remains
*outside* the formal system and belongs to “Metaphysics”.
What is mathematically the *first formalizable* (the act of
distinction) becomes the central object of Chapter 2 and the foundation
of the whole present volume.

### Connection with the Whole Volume

The strategy unfolded in Chapter 1—*distinguish the pre-formal and the
formal, acknowledge the boundary, move on to the nearest
formalizable*—is the *methodological matrix* of the volume. This matrix
will work at different levels through all 20 parts. A brief preview of
the most important returns:

- **Part IV.** The principle $P4$: infinity is a property of processes, not of completed objects. No exclusion is introduced here; what is *stated* here is a structural fact about the nature of infinity—infinity as a completed object does not exist, since completedness and inexhaustibility are incompatible characteristics. What exists is an infinite *process* as an actual entity, unfolding step by step. The first formalizable in this ontology is `RealProcess` as a function $ℕ \to ℚ$: the primary object of analysis, from which the functions that $ℝ$ performs classically are then reconstructed.
- **Part X.** Sets as a mode of consideration. The boundary lies between the list as an ontologically primary structure and the set as an abstract “already-gathered” object. The first formalizable on this side of the boundary is the list as an inductive type, on which an operator works in a mode that ignores some of the attributes.
- **Chapter 14.4.** The fundamental openness of the category $\mathbf{ToS}$. The boundary lies between the concrete levels of the hierarchy $\Sys(L_0), \Sys(L_1), …, \Sys(L_n)$ and a hypothetical “universe of everything,” which this volume does not formalize as a completed object. The first formalizable on this side of the boundary is the very scheme of transition between levels (the functors `forget` and `embed` with their adjunction).

In each case *one and the same* matrix works:

1. Discovery of the boundary: the substantive observation that some object (actual infinity, the completed set of everything, the universe of types) cannot be formalized without degeneration or circularity.
2. Acknowledgment of the boundary: fixing that the object *is not subject* to formalization in the volume, and keeping respect for this fact rather than attempting to get around it.
3. Movement to the nearest formalizable: finding the object that, resting on the pre-formal, is the *first* formalizable on this side of the boundary.

In this sense Chapter 1 is not only an introduction to Part I but *a
model of the methodology of the whole volume*. Each subsequent
discovery of a structural boundary will repeat the structure of
discovery unfolded here—but in the context of concrete mathematical
work. Having mastered this structure in Chapter 1, the reader has an
instrument that will work many times over.

Chapter 1 is complete. The next chapter unfolds the act of
distinction—the first formalizable—as a full-fledged mathematical
construction: a record with four fields, typed in `Prop`, formalized in
Rocq, and ready to serve as the ground for all the further work of the
volume.
