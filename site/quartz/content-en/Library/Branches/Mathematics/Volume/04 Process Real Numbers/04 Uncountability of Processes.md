## The Question of the Number of Points

### What Chapter 4.3 Left Behind

The previous chapter built the point. It built it as an equivalence
class of processes—the bundle of all processes of rational
approximations with a common limit, taken as one whole under the
relation `cauchy_equiv`—and not as a primary atom of a ready line:
there is no such atom in the $P4$-ontology. The real number turned out to
be a class; the point, a derived mode rather than an original
givenness.

As soon as this point appears, a question arises at once that it is
natural to ask about anything built: *how many* of them are there? How
many points—that is, how many classes? There are unsurveyably many
rational approximations; into how many classes does `cauchy_equiv`
divide them? The question is not an idle one: on the answer depends
whether the process line resembles the customary one or is arranged
differently.

### The Classical Answer and Its Price

Classical mathematics answers this question with Cantor’s famous
theorem: the points of an interval are *uncountably many*. They cannot be
numbered by the natural numbers; no sequence $x_0, x_1, x_2, …$ will
exhaust the interval $[0,1]$. The proof is the diagonal argument: from
any presented list one builds a point that is absent from the list.

This answer is famous, but in its classical presentation it carries
exactly the assumption that the whole present volume declines. An
“uncountable set” is a completed infinity of a special, higher kind: a
collection that is *larger* than the infinite collection of natural
numbers, not merely an infinite collection. To say “the interval is
uncountable” in the classical sense is to accept that the interval *is*
a ready uncountable set of points. And $P4$ does not admit completed
infinite sets—still less “super-large” ones.

A natural concern arises: will $P4$-mathematics lose Cantor’s theorem?
If there is no completed uncountable set, then there would seem to be
nothing to speak of when one speaks of its uncountability. The present
chapter shows that the concern is groundless: Cantor’s theorem is not
lost—it is *reformulated*, and, reformulated, it turns out to be a
statement about processes rather than about a set.

### The Plan of the Chapter

The plan of the chapter is as follows. First, what “uncountability”
*cannot* mean under $P4$, and what its correct, process formulation is
(§ 4.2). Then this formulation itself, through enumerations of
processes: what `Enumeration` is and why an enumeration must be
*regular* (§ 4.3, § 4.4). Then the arrangement of the diagonal
construction: why it divides an interval into *three* parts rather than
two (§ 4.5), and how this division yields a diagonal process with three
proved properties (§ 4.6). Then the transfer of the result into the
language of the process core (§ 4.7). Finally, the summary, an honest
account of the logical means on which the result rests, and the bridge
to Chapter 4.5 (§ 4.8).

The chapter, like the previous ones, is checked against the Rocq
formalization line by line. Its reasoning rests on two files. The
construction of the diagonal process and the uncountability theorem
itself are proved in the file `ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v`; the process
reformulation of the result is in the file `ProcessUncountable.v`. The
chapter will keep these two files apart throughout: the first *builds*
and *proves*, the second *translates* what has been proved into the
language of the process core. And where the formalization states
conditions—the regularity of the enumeration, the reliance on classical
logic—the chapter will state them along with it, without presenting the
result as stronger than it is proved.

## What “Uncountability” Cannot Mean under $P4$

### The Classical Formulation and Its Subject

Cantor’s classical theorem asserts: the set of points of the interval
$[0,1]$ is uncountable—there is no bijection between it and the set of
natural numbers $ℕ$. This statement has a *subject*—two ready sets,
$[0,1]$ and $ℕ$—and it says that there is no one-to-one correspondence
between them. It is a statement about sets: it takes two completed
objects and compares them by “size.”

Under $P4$ this statement has no subject—at least not all of it. $P4$
no longer admits the set $ℕ$ as a completed collection; but one can
still work with $ℕ$ as a type whose range of values is generated by a
process (Chapter 4.1). The “set of all points of the interval” as a
ready completed collection, however, is exactly what, as Chapters
4.1–4.3 showed, the $P4$-ontology *does not have*. A completed $[0,1]$,
assembled from ready point-atoms, does not exist. Hence the classical
“the set $[0,1]$ is uncountable,” in its original form, lacks the
subject it speaks of: there is no set whose uncountability could be
asserted.

### What Cannot and What Can Be Kept

Let us say plainly what *cannot* be kept of the classical theorem under
$P4$. The picture of two ready sets of different “size” cannot be kept.
The “uncountable set” as an object—a completed collection larger than
$ℕ$—cannot be kept. The very setting “let us compare the cardinalities
of two completed infinities” cannot be kept: there are no completed
infinities in $P4$, so there is nothing to compare.

But something—and something essential—*can* and *must* be kept. What is
kept is the *substantive core* of the diagonal argument: the indication
that every attempt to number the points *fails*. This indication can be
expressed without resorting to completed sets at all. Not “the set
$[0,1]$ is larger than the set $ℕ$,” but: *any process of enumerating
the points*—with the refinement of admissibility that will be
introduced below—*does not cover them all*. However one numbers, there
will always remain a point that has received no index. This statement
is about *processes of enumeration* and their *limitation*, not about
completed collections—and it does not contradict the $P4$-ontology.

### Uncountability as a Statement about Processes

This is how $P4$ reformulates uncountability. Not “there exists an
uncountable set” but “there *does not exist* a process of enumeration
that would cover all the process points of the interval.” In E/R/R terms
(the analysis of § 4.8.2) this is the *rule* level: the impossibility
for an enumerator process to cover all the class-roles—not a judgment
about a completed set. The statement becomes *negative* and
*processual*: it speaks of an impossibility—of the fact that a process
of a certain kind (an enumerating one) cannot do a certain thing (cover
everything)—and it says this about processes, not about sets. The file
of the Rocq formalization devoted to this theorem formulates its
content in exactly these terms: under $P4$, “uncountability” means that
no enumeration process captures all Cauchy processes—this is a
statement about processes, not about a set.[^1]

### Why Intervals, Not Digits

Before building the diagonal process, it is worth mentioning one choice
of means that the formalization makes from the very beginning. The
classical diagonal argument is most often presented through *decimal
digits*: from a list of numbers one builds a new number whose $n$-th
digit differs from the $n$-th digit of the $n$-th number of the list.
For process mathematics this is a poor carrier for the proof, and
Chapter 4.3 has already shown why. Extracting a digit is a
*discontinuous* operation: for a number on the boundary between two
decimal intervals, the digit “jumps.” And it is sensitive to double
representations: $0.999…$ and $1.000…$ are two digit
representations of one point, one class (§ 3.7 of Chapter 4.3), and an
argument that works with digits stumbles over such duality.

Therefore the formalization of $ToS$ builds the diagonal *without
digits*. The carrier is *nested intervals* and *rational estimates*: at
each step the diagonal process *narrows the interval* in which the
future limit lies, stepping away from the place where the next
enumerated process is, rather than “choosing a digit.” The interval
diagonal is continuous by construction and insensitive to double
representations—it works with approximations as rational numbers, not
with their positional notations. The following sections unfold
precisely this interval construction.

## Enumeration of Processes

### What an Enumeration Is

To state “no process of enumeration covers all the points,” one must
first define exactly what an *enumeration* is. Intuitively, it is an
attempt to number the points: a process that at step $n$ presents the
$n$-th point. In `ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` the definition looks like
this:

```coq
Definition Enumeration := nat -> RealProcess.
```

An enumeration is a function from indices to processes. This notation
is worth a close look, because it contains an essential feature.

### A Process That Yields Processes

In the classical picture an enumeration is a list of *ready numbers*:
$x_0, x_1, x_2, …$, where each $x_n$ is a point-atom. In the process
picture this cannot be so: there are no ready point-atoms. Therefore
the $n$-th term of an enumeration is itself a *process*: a process of
rational approximations to a point, not a point.

Let us unfold the types. A bare process is a function from step numbers
to rational approximations: `RealProcess` ${}={}$ `nat -> Q`. And an
enumeration is a function from indices to processes:
`Enumeration` ${}={}$ `nat -> RealProcess`. Substituting
one into the other:

$$
\texttt{Enumeration} \;=\; \texttt{nat} \to (\texttt{nat} \to ℚ).
$$

An enumeration is a *process that yields processes*. It is an object
one level higher: where an ordinary process yields a rational number at
each step, an enumeration yields a whole process at each step. The
diagonal argument will work over a process that itself generates
processes, not over a table of ready real numbers as in the classical
exposition. Schematically:

$$
E 0 : \texttt{RealProcess}, \quad
E 1 : \texttt{RealProcess}, \quad
E 2 : \texttt{RealProcess}, \quad …
$$

each term $E n$ is a process of rational approximations to some point.

It should be noted at once that an enumeration may be *redundant*: two
different indices may yield equivalent processes—that is, two
representatives of one and the same point (of one class by
`process_equiv` from Chapter 4.3). This does not hinder the theorem—on
the contrary, it makes it stronger: the theorem asserts that however the
enumeration is arranged, *even with repetitions*, there will be a
point—a class—not represented by any of its terms. The enumeration is
free to spend indices on repetitions; the theorem says that even without
repetitions it would still miss at least one class.

### Admissible Enumeration: Regularity

Not every function `nat -> RealProcess` will do as an enumeration of
the points of the interval. Two conditions are needed. First, each term
must be a *genuine* process of a point—convergent, that is, Cauchy;
otherwise it defines no point. Second, all terms must lie *in the
interval* $[0,1]$—it is the points of precisely this interval that we are
numbering.

`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` introduces two admissibility predicates. The
first, the weaker one:

```coq
Definition valid_enumeration (E : Enumeration) : Prop :=
  forall n, is_Cauchy (E n) /\ (forall m, 0 <= E n m <= 1).
```

Each term is a Cauchy process and lies in $[0,1]$. The second predicate
is stronger, and it is the one the main theorem will need:

```coq
Definition valid_regular_enumeration (E : Enumeration) : Prop :=
  forall n, is_Regular_Cauchy (E n) /\ (forall m, 0 <= E n m <= 1).
```

Here each term is required to be not merely Cauchy but *regular*
Cauchy—`is_Regular_Cauchy`. What this means and why it is required is
the subject of the next section. The file proves that a regular
enumeration is also an ordinary one (the lemma
`valid_regular_implies_valid`): regularity is a strengthening of the
notion of admissibility, not a departure from it.

### The Exact Formulation of Uncountability

Now uncountability can be stated exactly. It asserts: for every
*regular* enumeration of processes of points of the interval $[0,1]$
there exists a Cauchy process lying in $[0,1]$ that is not equivalent
to any term of the enumeration. However many points the enumeration
numbers, there will be a point—a process—that bypasses it.

It is worth fixing at once what this formulation does *not* say, so as
not to read it as stronger than it is. It speaks of *regular*
enumerations, not of arbitrary enumerations of Cauchy processes: a term
of the enumeration must have a known modulus of convergence (§ 4.4). And
it speaks of processes of the *unit interval*, not of “all processes” in
general: both the diagonal process and all terms of the enumeration lie
in $[0,1]$. This, however, does not weaken the result to something
insignificant—on the contrary. Uncountability is found already *within
a single interval*: the matter lies in the very process structure of an
arbitrarily small piece of the continuum, not in the “size of the whole
line.” One interval is enough for a regular enumeration to fail.

## Why the Enumeration Must Be Regular

### Convergence and the Rate of Convergence

The Cauchy property says that the process *converges*: for every
precision the approximations, in due course, draw closer together than
it. But it says nothing about *when* this “in due course” will come. The
witness of the Cauchy property is a function that, given a precision
$\varepsilon$, yields a cutoff $N$; and this witness is hidden inside an
existential quantifier. Knowing only that a process is Cauchy, we know
that a cutoff *exists*, but not *which* one.

Regularity corrects this situation. A *regular* Cauchy process is a
process with a rate of convergence *known in advance*. In
`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` regularity is given by a quite concrete
estimate rather than abstractly (“the rate is somehow known”):

```coq
Definition is_Regular_Cauchy (R : RealProcess) : Prop :=
  forall m n : nat, (m > 0)%nat -> (n > 0)%nat ->
    Qabs (R m - R n) <= (1 # Pos.of_nat m) + (1 # Pos.of_nat n).
```

The discrepancy between the approximations at steps $m$ and $n$ is
bounded by the sum $\tfrac1m + \tfrac1n$. This is an *explicit,
written-out formula*, not the existence of a hidden modulus: by step $m$
the process has certainly come within $\tfrac1m$, or a little more, of
its limit. The rate of convergence ceases to be hidden—it is written
directly into the definition.

### Bishop’s Idea

The requirement of regularity is a substantive principle coming from
Bishop’s constructive analysis, not a technical whim. Bishop observed:
to *work* with a process, it is not enough to know that it converges;
one must know *how fast*. Only then does the process become a genuinely
computational object—one can say in advance how far to go to obtain an
approximation of the required precision.

Under $P4$ this idea fits especially naturally. If the process is
primary and the point derived, then the process must be *controllable*:
the operator must be able not merely to launch it but also to know how
many steps remain to the needed precision. Regularity is exactly
controllability written out as a formula. A regular process is a process
one can *use*, not only one about which it is known that it converges.
The file proves that regularity does not lead out of the class of
convergent processes—a regular Cauchy process is also an ordinary
Cauchy process (the lemma `Regular_implies_Cauchy`); regularity only
adds to convergence the knowledge of its rate.

### What the Diagonal Argument Needs Regularity For

Now one can see why regularity is needed precisely *here*. At each step
$n$ the diagonal process must “step away” from the $n$-th enumerated
process $E(n)$—move aside from the place where $E(n)$ is. But *where* is
$E(n)$? $E(n)$ is a process; at different steps it yields different
rational approximations. To step away from it meaningfully, the
diagonal construction must look at some *definite* approximation of
$E(n)$ and be sure that this approximation is already *close* to the
limit—that the further steps of $E(n)$ will not carry it far.

It is precisely for this certainty that regularity is needed. Were
$E(n)$ merely a Cauchy process, the construction would not know at
which step its approximation is already reliable: the cutoff exists but
is not presented. For a regular process, however, the rate is known by
a formula—and the construction can *compute* the step number (in the
code it is called `trisect_ref` $n$) at which the approximation of
$E(n)$ is guaranteed to have entered a narrow neighborhood of the limit.
Knowing this, it can step away safely: away from the place where $E(n)$
*will certainly remain*, not from a random approximation. Regularity is
what turns “step away from $E(n)$” from an indefinite intention into a
feasible operation.

Hence the reservation attached to the formulation of uncountability.
The theorem is proved for *regular* enumerations; for arbitrary
enumerations of Cauchy processes—without a guarantee of the rate—it is
not established in the repository. This can be read both as a narrowing
and as a refinement of the notion of an admissible enumeration: under
$P4$ an enumeration of points *ought* to give controllable, regular
access to each point—otherwise it is not quite an enumeration but
merely a collection of processes without control over them. On this
reading, regularity does not limit the theorem; it specifies what it
means to enumerate points honestly.

## Trisection, Not Bisection

### The Plan of the Diagonal through Intervals

The diagonal process is built as follows. Take the interval $[0,1]$. At
step $n$ look where the $n$-th enumerated process $E(n)$ is, and
*narrow* the current interval to a part of it that *steps away* from
$E(n)$. The intervals nest inside one another and contract; their
midpoints form a process—the diagonal one. Since at step $n$ the
interval has moved away from $E(n)$, the limit of the diagonal will turn
out to be separated from $E(n)$—the diagonal will not coincide with any
enumerated process.

The whole question is how exactly to “narrow while stepping away.” And
here the construction faces a fork on which it depends whether the
proof goes through or not.

### The Edge Difficulty of Bisection

The simplest way is *bisection*: divide the interval in half. See in
which half—left or right—$E(n)$ is, and take the *other*.
`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` implements this approach (the construction
`avoid_E`, `diagonal_intervals`), but itself marks it as *superseded* by
a better one—because of the edge difficulty.

The difficulty is this. The construction does not know the position of
$E(n)$ *exactly*—it knows it *approximately*, through one of the
rational approximations of the process $E(n)$. Around this
approximation there is an ineliminable uncertainty—let us call it a
*confidence interval*: the strip $[ \text{approximation}-\delta,\;
\text{approximation}+\delta ]$, inside which the limit of $E(n)$ certainly
lies, though exactly where is unknown. And here is the trouble: this
strip may *straddle the midpoint* of the interval being divided. Then
it is unclear in which half $E(n)$ is: part of the confidence interval
lies to the left of the midpoint, part to the right. Whichever half is
chosen as the “opposite” one, the *limit* of $E(n)$ may turn out to be
in it too—right at the boundary. Bisection stumbles on this edge case:
“the enemy is exactly at the midpoint,” and there is nowhere to step
away to.

### The Solution: Division into Three

Trisection removes this difficulty geometrically. Instead of two halves,
the interval is divided into *three* equal thirds—each a third of the
original in width. The Rocq formalization introduces, for the choice of
a third, a type with three values and three corresponding operations:

```coq
Inductive TrisectChoice : Set := TC_Left | TC_Middle | TC_Right.
```

The idea is simple. The confidence interval around the approximation of
$E(n)$ has some width—say, $2\delta$. If this width is *shorter* than
one third, then the confidence interval, however it lies, cannot meet
all three thirds at once. Hence at least one third is *free*—the
confidence interval does not touch it, and $E(n)$ cannot get into it.
It is into this free third that the construction narrows the interval.
“The enemy exactly at the midpoint” is no longer a threat: even
straddling the boundary of two thirds, the confidence interval, being
shorter than a third, leaves the third third untouched. The choice of
the free third is performed by the function `smart_trisect_choice`; it
is arranged as a *deterministic branching* on rational inequalities, not
as a “choice” in any problematic sense—more on this in § 4.8.

### Synchronization of Parameters

For the argument to go through, the key inequality must be
*guaranteed*: the width of the confidence interval is less than the
width of a third—for *all* steps at once, not for some single step.
This is where the regularity of § 4.4 is needed: it makes it possible to
link three quantities so that the inequality always holds. In the
current code the parameters are synchronized as follows:

$$
\texttt{ref}(n) = 48\cdot 3^{n}, \quad
\delta(n) = \frac{1}{24\cdot 3^{n}}, \quad
\text{width}(n) = \frac{1}{3^{n}}.
$$

Here $\texttt{ref}(n)$ is the number of the step at which the approximation of
$E(n)$ is taken (regularity guarantees that by this step $E(n)$ is
already close to its limit); $\delta(n)$ is the half-width of the
confidence interval; the width is the size of the interval after $n$
trisections, decreasing as $1/3^n$. With this synchronization we have

$$
2 \delta(n) \;<\; \frac{\text{width}(n)}{3}
\quad\text{for all } n,
$$

and this is a proved lemma (`trisect_delta_bound`): twice the
half-width of the confidence interval is always less than the width of
a third. No edge cases remain—the inequality holds for every $n$, and a
free third is *always* found.[^2]

The width of the nested intervals decreases geometrically, as
$1/3^n$—a threefold contraction at each step; from this follows the
Cauchy property of the diagonal process (§ 4.6.2). In the process
reformulation this rate is recorded as a separate quantity—
`diagonal_convergence_rate`, equal to $1/3$.

## The Diagonal Process

### The Construction

Putting the trisection together, we obtain the diagonal process. We
start with the interval $[0,1]$. At each step $n$ we take the
approximation of $E(n)$ at step $\texttt{ref}(n)$, build around it a
confidence interval of half-width $\delta(n)$, find a free third, and
narrow the current interval to it. This yields a sequence of nested,
contracting intervals. The *diagonal process* is the process of their
midpoints: at step $n$ it yields the midpoint of the $n$-th interval. In
the Rocq formalization it is called `diagonal_trisect_v2`.

Three properties of the diagonal process are proved, and together they
give the uncountability theorem. Let us analyze them one by one.

### The Diagonal Is a Cauchy Process

First: the diagonal process *converges*—it is a Cauchy process. This is
the lemma `diagonal_trisect_v2_is_Cauchy`. The proof rests on the
contraction of the intervals: the width of the $n$-th interval is
$1/3^n$, and the powers of three grow without bound (the Archimedean
lemma `Archimedean_pow3` from the same file). Hence for every precision
$\varepsilon$ there is a step after which the intervals are already
narrower than $\varepsilon$; and the midpoints, from this step on, all
lie in one narrow interval and therefore differ by less than
$\varepsilon$. The diagonal is a genuine process of a point, not a
divergent collection of rational numbers.

### The Diagonal Lies in the Interval

Second: the diagonal process does not leave $[0,1]$. This is the lemma
`diagonal_trisect_v2_in_unit`: at every step the value of the diagonal
is contained between $0$ and $1$. The proof is simple in essence: the
initial interval is $[0,1]$, and each third lies inside the interval
being divided—trisection does not lead outside. The midpoint of any of
the nested intervals therefore remains in $[0,1]$. This property is
needed for the diagonal to be a *legitimate rival* of the enumerated
processes: they lie in $[0,1]$ by the admissibility condition, and so
does the diagonal—otherwise the comparison would not be quite fair.

### The Diagonal Differs from Every Enumerated Process

The third property is the core of the proof: the diagonal process is
*not equivalent to any* enumerated process. This is the lemma
`diagonal_trisect_v2_differs_from_E_n`. Here we must recall what “not
equivalent” means. In `ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` it is the predicate
`not_equiv`:

```coq
Definition not_equiv (R1 R2 : RealProcess) : Prop :=
  exists eps, eps > 0 /\ forall N, exists m,
    (m > N)%nat /\ Qabs (R1 m - R2 m) >= eps.
```

This is a *positive* statement about a persistent gap, not merely the
negation of the convergence of the difference to zero: there exists
$\varepsilon > 0$ such that after any cutoff $N$ there is a step
$m > N$ at which the discrepancy between the processes is at least
$\varepsilon$. The gap need not stand at *every* late step—the
definition does not require this; it requires that the discrepancy
*reach* $\varepsilon$ *again and again*, arbitrarily far along the
course of the process. Two processes related by `not_equiv` are
separated *in substance*, not “in the limit”: the discrepancy between
them does not fade—it recurs without bound.

Why the diagonal is separated in this way from every $E(n)$. At step
$n+1$ the construction narrowed the interval to a third *free* of the
confidence interval of $E(n)$. The diagonal, from this step on, lies
*inside* the chosen third; while $E(n)$—by the construction of the free
third—is separated from this third, on the other side of the gap. The
gap has a computable positive width (it is obtained from the same
synchronization $2\delta < \text{width}/3$). This gap serves as the
threshold $\varepsilon$ in `not_equiv`: the discrepancy between the
diagonal and $E(n)$ reaches it again and again, arbitrarily far along
the steps. The diagonal differs from $E(n)$ neither by accident nor “in
the limit”—it is separated from it by a persistent gap, and this is
proved for *every* $n$.

### The Main Theorem

Together the three properties make up the uncountability theorem. In
`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` it is written as follows:

```coq
Theorem unit_interval_uncountable_trisect_v2 :
  forall E : Enumeration,
  valid_regular_enumeration E ->
  exists D : RealProcess,
    is_Cauchy D /\
    (forall m, 0 <= D m <= 1) /\
    (forall n, not_equiv D (E n)).
```

Let us read it word for word. For every enumeration $E$, if it is
*regularly admissible*, there exists a process $D$ such that: $D$ is
Cauchy; $D$ lies in $[0,1]$; and for every $n$ the process $D$ is not
equivalent to $E(n)$. The process $D$ is precisely the diagonal
`diagonal_trisect_v2`, and the three conjuncts are the three properties
analyzed.

One point of precision in the signature deserves mention. Of the
diagonal $D$ the theorem asserts that it is a *Cauchy* process
(`is_Cauchy`)—but it does *not* assert that it is *regular*. Regularity
is required of the *enumerated* processes—so that the construction can
know where they are (§ 4.4); the constructed diagonal itself is declared
only as a convergent process in $[0,1]$. The regularity of the diagonal
is not part of the signature of the main theorem, and the chapter does
not assert it: the formalization has proved exactly what is
written—`is_Cauchy`, lying in the interval, and non-equivalence to all
the terms.

## The Process Reformulation

### What a Translation Is Needed For

The theorem of the previous section is proved in
`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v`—a file that took shape around nested
intervals and has its own working language. Meanwhile the main process
core of ToS—the file `ProcessCore.v`, analyzed in Chapters 4.2 and
4.3—speaks a slightly different language. For the uncountability
theorem to take its place in the common line of process mathematics, it
must be *translated* into the terms of `ProcessCore`. A separate file is
devoted to this—`ProcessUncountable.v`.

It is important to separate the roles of the two files at once, without
mixing them. `ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` is the file where the diagonal
is *built* and the theorem *proved*; all the heavy work—trisection,
synchronization, the gap—is there. `ProcessUncountable.v` is a
*translator* file: it does not build the diagonal anew and does not
prove uncountability from scratch; it *transfers* what has already been
proved into the language of the process core. Its own theorem rests
directly on `unit_interval_uncountable_trisect_v2`.

### A Bridge between Two Notations of the Cauchy Property

The translation requires one technical bridge. The Cauchy property is
written slightly differently in the two files. In
`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` the cutoff is taken with a strict
inequality—the approximations draw together for steps *strictly
greater* than $N$. In `ProcessCore.v` it is taken with a non-strict
one, for steps *not less* than $N$. The difference is small and purely a
matter of quantifiers—one and the same convergence condition in two
presentations—but formally these are two different predicates, and they
must be linked. `ProcessUncountable.v` links them by the lemma
`si_cauchy_compat`: the two notations of the Cauchy property are
equivalent. This is a bridge, like the bridge between `is_Cauchy` and
`is_cauchy` in Chapter 4.2: a proved equivalence of two formulations of
one content, not an identity of terms.

A similar bridge is needed for the “difference” of processes. In
`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` difference is expressed by the predicate
`not_equiv` (a persistent gap $\varepsilon$); in `ProcessCore.v` there
is the equivalence relation `process_equiv` (Chapter 4.3).
`ProcessUncountable.v` proves the lemma
`not_equiv_implies_not_process_equiv`: if processes are related by
`not_equiv`, then they are *not* equivalent by `process_equiv`.

### Uncountability in the Language of the Process Core

With these bridges the theorem carries over. In `ProcessUncountable.v`
its process version looks like this:

```coq
Theorem process_uncountable :
  forall E : Enumeration,
  valid_regular_enumeration E ->
  exists D : RealProcess,
    is_Cauchy D /\
    (forall m, 0 <= D m /\ D m <= 1) /\
    (forall n, ~ process_equiv D (E n)).
Proof.
  intros E Hvalid.
  destruct (unit_interval_uncountable_trisect_v2 E Hvalid)
    as [D [HD1 [HD2 HD3]]].
  ...
```

The same uncountability—but in the types of `ProcessCore`. And the
structure of the proof is visible at once: it *destructures* the result
of the main theorem (`destruct … unit_interval_uncountable_trisect_v2`)
and recasts its three conjuncts through the bridges. The translator file
is honest about its source: it does not claim to be an independent
proof; it translates.

### One Point of Precision: $\neg$ process_equiv and not_equiv

A subtlety must be noted here, so as not to read the translated theorem
as stronger than the original. The original theorem in
`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` gives `not_equiv`—a *positive* statement
about a persistent gap: a threshold $\varepsilon$ is presented that the
discrepancy reaches again and again, arbitrarily far along the steps.
The translated theorem in `ProcessUncountable.v` gives
$\neg \texttt{process\_equiv}$—the *negation* of equivalence.

The bridge leads *in one direction*: `not_equiv` implies
$\neg \texttt{process\_equiv}$, and this implication is proved. The converse
implication—that a persistent gap can be *extracted* from the negation
of equivalence—is not singled out as a separate lemma in the
repository. It is therefore most exact to put it this way: at the level
of the current formalization, `not_equiv` is the more *informative*
statement—it carries an explicit gap-witness; while
`ProcessUncountable.v` uses only a *consequence* of this fact, the
negation of `process_equiv`. This is a difference in the *level of
informativeness* of the two formulations in the present code, not a
claim that one statement is unconditionally, mathematically strictly
weaker than the other.

### The Connection with Chapter 4.3: The Diagonal as a New Class

The translation into the language of `process_equiv` gives a direct
junction with Chapter 4.3. There the point was understood as a setoid
class of processes: for representatives of `CauchySeq`, by the relation
`cauchy_equiv`, and for bare `RealProcess`, by the relation
`process_equiv` agreeing with it (the bridge between the two relations
is proved in `ProcessBridge.v`, § 3.3.1). These are two different
relations on two representations, but linked by a bridge—one and the
same “defining one number.” Processes in one class are one point. The
translated theorem says that the diagonal does not stand in the
relation `process_equiv` to any enumerated process. In the language of
Chapter 4.3 this means: the diagonal *represents a new class*—a point
that is not in the enumeration. The enumeration has numbered some
classes; the diagonal presents a class that did not get into the
numbering.

Here, as in Chapter 4.3, one should be careful with the word “class.”
The quotient type of points—a type whose terms would be the classes
themselves—is not built in the repository (Chapter 4.3, § 3.4.3);
Chapter 4.4 does not claim it either. But the *relation* that separates
the classes from one another—`process_equiv`—is built and proved, and
it is this relation that works in the formal theorem: the diagonal is
separated from every $E(n)$ by this relation. Uncountability *pertains*
to classes—but it is expressed through a relation, not through a ready
quotient type: the theorem speaks of equivalence classes, not of bare
processes as separate entries, and formally expresses this by the
relation `process_equiv`.

And the diagonal is separated from $E(n)$ precisely *by the equivalence
relation*, not pointwise. Chapter 4.3 foretold this in § 3.8: the
difference needed for the diagonal argument is a non-coincidence *by*
`process_equiv`, a failure to fall into a common class—not a pointwise
non-coincidence (which, as we have seen, is both insufficient and not
required). The diagonal may at individual steps be arbitrarily close to
$E(n)$—what matters is that it does not fall into one class with it:
the discrepancy between the diagonal process and $E(n)$ cannot become
zero in the sense of process equivalence. The analysis of § 4.8.2 brings
together this echo of the role-classes of Chapter 4.3: the count is kept
by classes, and the diagonal presents a new class-role.

## What Is Proved and What It Rests On

### What Is Proved

Let us gather the result exactly. The following is proved: no regular
enumeration of Cauchy processes lying in the interval $[0,1]$ covers all
the process points of this interval. For every such enumeration—however
many processes it numbers—a diagonal process is built: convergent, lying
in $[0,1]$, and separated by a persistent gap from every term of the
enumeration. The enumeration *always* fails; there always remains a
point that did not get into the numbering.

This is Cantor’s theorem in the $P4$ reading. It differs from the
classical one in its *subject*, not in “strength.” The classical theorem
speaks of two ready sets and their incomparable “sizes”; the process
theorem speaks of the impossibility for a process of enumeration to
cover all the process classes of the interval. There are no completed
infinities in it, either at the input or at the output: at the input,
an enumeration as a process that yields processes; at the output, a
diagonal process that escapes it. Uncountability has turned out to be a
property of a *relation between processes*, not of a set: between the
enumerating process and the enumerated ones there always stands a
diagonal process that did not enter the enumeration.

### E/R/R Analysis: Uncountability as a Statement about Processes

What has been proved has an E/R/R arrangement (Part I), and its analysis
secures the main shift of the chapter. The header of the supporting file
gives the labeling directly: *elements—nested intervals avoiding every
enumerated process; role—the diagonal differs from every enumerated
process; rule—at step $n$ avoid $E(n)$ by choosing a free third*.[^3] We
conduct the analysis in the ontological order Rules $\to$ Roles $\to$
Elements. A reservation about status: the subject is processes over
$ℚ$, the quotient type of classes is not built (Chapter 4.3), and the
“system” is not an object `System` $L$.

*Rules* (the law $L5$). The constitution-construction is the rule of
trisection: at step $n$ narrow the interval to a third *free* of $E(n)$
(rather than halving it—bisection stumbled on the edge case, § 4.5). The
difference of processes is fixed by the rule `not_equiv`—a persistent
gap that the discrepancy *reaches again and again* (the count is kept by
classes, not pointwise). The admissibility condition is the *regularity*
of the enumeration (`valid_regular`), controllable access to each
process (§ 4.4). And the most general constitution is $P4$:
uncountability is a statement *about processes and enumeration*, not
about a completed set. The honest boundary of the means: the proof rests
on $L3$ (`classic`), but *not* on the Axiom of Choice and *not* on
completed infinity (§ 4.8.4 below).

*Roles—the significance of positions* (the law $L4$). The enumeration
$E$ plays the role of the *enumerator*—a process that yields processes
(§ 4.3); the diagonal $D$, the role of the *rival*, a process different
from every enumerated one (this is how the role is named in the file
header); regularity, the role of *controllability*, without which
“stepping away from $E(n)$” is unfeasible (§ 4.4). And the count is kept
*by classes*, as Chapter 4.3 prepared: the diagonal presents a *new
class*-role that did not get into the enumeration.

*Elements—the bearers* (the law $L1$ and the principle $P4$). The
elements are processes: the enumerated $E(n)$ and the diagonal $D$, and
beneath them the nested intervals and their midpoints. By $L1$ each
process is identical to itself. By $P4$ everything is finite at each
step: the diagonal is built step by step, the enumeration is a function
yielding functions—no completed uncountable set of points is formed
anywhere.

Let us gather the analysis into a table.

| Component | What it determines | E/R/R |
|---|---|---|
| processes $E(n)$, the diagonal $D$; nested intervals | bearers (finite, $P4$) | Element |
| $E$—enumerator; $D$—rival; regularity—controllability | roles | Role |
| trisection: avoid $E(n)$ by a free third | constitution-construction | Rule |
| `not_equiv`; count by classes | rule of difference | Rule (concr.) |
| $P4$: uncountability is about processes, not a set | constitution | Rule |
| laws $L1$–$L5$ (here $L3$) | universal layer | Rule (univ.) |

*Well-formedness.* The labeling is unambiguous: the processes are
elements, the enumerator and the rival are roles, trisection and
`not_equiv` are rules. And here again the analysis turns into
*diagnostics*: the classical “paradox” of a super-large uncountable set
arose from a confusion of categories—from reading “all the points of the
interval” as a ready *element*-collection. In the correct labeling,
uncountability is a *rule* about processes, not a property of a
completed set: no enumerator process covers all the class-roles. There
is no completed infinity, either at the input or at the output.

 The analysis closes the process line of Part IV. Chapter 4.2 read the
number as a progression of roles (candidate $\to$ representative $\to$
class), Chapter 4.3 read the point as a role-class; the present chapter
answers *how many* such role-classes there are in an interval—and answers
in the language of processes: *more than any regular enumerator process
can number*. Uncountability has turned from a statement about the “size
of a set” into a statement about the *limit of the enumerating
process*. How many exactly, and how this “surplus” is arranged
structurally, is the question of Chapter 4.5.

### What It Rests On: Classical Logic

Honesty requires naming the logical means on which the result rests.
Both supporting files—`ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` and
`ProcessUncountable.v`—use *classical logic*: the axiom `classic`, the
law of excluded middle. In `ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` this is visible
directly: the file imports the classical module and is provided with a
philosophical note on the role of this law; `ProcessUncountable.v`
inherits the same axiom. The result on the uncountability of processes
is *not* fully constructive: it rests on $L3$.

This is neither a contradiction nor a defect. Chapter 4.1 showed that
$L3$—the law of excluded middle—is in ToS one of the accepted laws of
distinction, not a dubious assumption: a distinction either *is* or *is
not*; the act of distinction leaves no third. The reliance of
uncountability on $L3$ means only that the result stands on the same
logical ground as the whole volume. To name this support is to be exact,
not to admit a weakness.

### What Is Not Here: Choice and Completed Infinity

Having named what the result rests on, let us name just as exactly what
it does *not* rest on—and this is essential. The proof does *not* use
the *axiom of choice*. The trisection construction does not “choose”
anything in the problematic sense of the word: it does not take an
element from an unstructured family of sets. It *branches
deterministically* on decidable rational inequalities—it compares
rational numbers and returns one of the three thirds by an explicit
rule. This is a computable choice of a branch of an algorithm, not a
set-theoretic act of choice; the Axiom of Choice is not needed for it.

And the proof does *not* use *completed infinity* as a ready object. It
nowhere forms a completed infinite set of points: the processes are
functions, the enumeration is a function yielding functions, the
diagonal is built step by step, and at each step everything is finite.
The file `ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v` checks this by machine: for the key
theorems it contains `Print Assumptions` commands, which output the list
of axioms a theorem depends on. Among these dependencies classical logic
appears, and there appears *neither* a separate axiom of choice *nor* an
axiom of infinity of the set-theoretic, ZFC-like kind. (Let us state it
exactly: `Print Assumptions` checks the dependencies *of the specified
theorem*; it does not “scan the whole file”; and what should be spoken
of here is precisely the dependencies of the main uncountability
theorems.)

One more distinction is important here, so that no perplexity remains
for the attentive reader. The type `nat`—the step numbers—takes an
active part in the proof; through it `nat -> Q` and `nat -> RealProcess`
are built. Is not `nat` itself already a “completed infinity”? No—and
Chapter 4.1 analyzed this. In ToS `nat` is read as an *inductive scheme
of generation*—the rule “there is zero; from every number there is the
next”—not as a ready set of all natural numbers. The type `nat`
*presents a rule*, not a completed collection. The set-theoretic axiom
of infinity, by contrast, postulates a *ready* infinite set as an actual
object—and this is what the proof does not use. `nat` as the type of the
steps of a process is compatible with $P4$; completed infinity as an
object is not. The uncountability of processes has been obtained by
means of the first kind: classical logic—yes; completed actual
infinity—no.

### The Bridge to Chapter 4.5

The uncountability theorem raises a new question—and the next chapter
will take it up. The rational points are *countable*: they can be
numbered; a process of enumeration exists for them. The process classes
of the interval—as we have shown—*escape* regular enumeration. Hence
there is some *difference* between the rational points and the process
points—but what is it? Classical mathematics would answer: the
difference is in *cardinality*; the rationals are “countably many,”
while the points of the interval are “uncountably many,” and the second
is “larger” than the first.

But this answer is again in the language of ready sets and their
sizes—a language that $P4$ declines. Under $P4$ the question has to be
posed differently: if the rational points admit an explicit traversal,
while the process classes of the unit interval admit no such traversal,
then how can one speak at all of the “magnitude” of the process
continuum—without resorting to completed cardinalities? What does
“larger” mean when there is nothing to compare except processes and
their enumerations? This is the question of the process reading of the
*continuum*, and Chapter 4.5 leads to it. The present chapter leaves it
open, without prejudging the answer: it has carried the uncountability
theorem through to the end, and it passes on the question of the “size”
of what turned out to be uncountable.

*The summary of the chapter comes in three parts. First: what is proved.
No regular enumeration of Cauchy processes in the interval $[0,1]$ covers
all the process classes of this interval: for every such enumeration a
diagonal Cauchy process is built, lying in $[0,1]$ and separated by a
persistent gap (`not_equiv`) from every term. This is Cantor’s theorem
in the $P4$ reading—a statement about the impossibility of a process
enumeration, not about a completed uncountable set; the construction of
the diagonal is by trisection.*

*Second: what it rests on. The construction and the theorem are proved
in `ShrinkingIntervals_ERR.v`; the process reformulation is in
`ProcessUncountable.v`, which transfers the result into the language of
`ProcessCore` through the bridge `si_cauchy_compat` and the implication
`not_equiv_implies_not_process_equiv`. The theorem requires a *regular*
enumeration—with a known modulus of convergence; for arbitrary Cauchy
enumerations it is not established. Both files use classical logic
($L3$, the axiom `classic`); the Axiom of Choice and the axiom of
infinity are *not* used—trisection is deterministic, and there are no
completed infinities.*

*Third: what remains open. The process theorem gives
$\neg \texttt{process\_equiv}$—the negation of equivalence; the original
`not_equiv`, with an explicit gap, is more informative, and the converse
passage is not singled out as a separate lemma in the code. The diagonal
represents a new equivalence class; the quotient type of these classes
is not built as a separate Rocq type (Chapter 4.3)—what works is the
*relation* separating the classes, not a ready quotient type. And the
question of the “magnitude” of the process continuum remains open: how
exactly the process classes of the interval differ from the countable
rational points, if the difference is spoken of in a language other than
that of completed cardinalities—Chapter 4.5 leads to this question.*
