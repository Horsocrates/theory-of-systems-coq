## The Principle of Positive Existence

### Where Part III Begins

Part II went through the natural number series—$ℕ = \{1, 2, 3, …\}$—to the end: it established its primacy, its arrangement, and its inevitability. The natural number series is the beginning of number. But not the whole of number.

Part III takes the next step: from the natural number series to the rational numbers, by way of the integers. ToS takes this step in the same way as it went through the natural number series: as a *derivation*, not as an accepted convention. The integers and the rational numbers will arise as what a shortfall in what has already been reached compels, rather than as new sorts of numbers introduced by agreement. And before taking this step, we must lay down the principle on which it will stand—a principle that Part III introduces for the first time and carries through all its chapters.

### Everything That Exists Exists Positively

The principle is this:

*Everything that ontologically exists exists positively. There is no ontological status lower than absence. What carries the minus sign is a coordinate position; logical negation is a side of the act of distinction; algebraic invertibility is an operational structure. None of the three is negative existence, yet they must not be identified with one another.*

Let us call this the *principle of positive existence*. Let us examine what it means—and, no less important, what it does *not* mean.

The principle speaks of *existence itself*, not of every measure of every property. It asserts: existence is never *negative*—there is no ontological status below absence. It does *not* assert that every measure of every existing object is strictly positive. This distinction is essential, and it must be drawn at once. Individual magnitudes of an existing object may well take the value *zero*: the zero velocity of a body at rest, the zero electric charge of a neutral particle, a zero balance on an account, the zero extent of a mathematical point. The zero value of an individual property does *not* mean that the object is absent: the body is at rest, the particle is present, the point is. The principle of positive existence is not about this. It is about the boundary between “is” and “is not” of the object *itself*, not about the values of its coordinates and measures.

So the exact formula of the principle is this. If something is presented as *existing*—in whatever mode of counting or measurement—its *presence* is not negative: there is no thing that would exist “in the minus,” below absence. Zero means the *absence* of the given presentation—or the zero value of the given coordinate or measure—but not “existence below absence.” What *can* be negative—and is—are coordinates, orientations, differences, and operational characteristics; the whole chapter will examine what exactly they are. There is one boundary that the principle draws firmly: existence itself is never negative. Between the “is” and the “is not” of a thing there is no third position—there is no “is in the minus” as a way *to be*.

This principle is a direct continuation of what Part II established about zero. Chapter II.1 showed: zero is not a number; zero is a mark of the *absence* of an act of distinction. The natural number series begins with one—with the first accomplished act; zero lies *before* the series, indicating that nothing has been accomplished. The principle of positive existence generalizes this: everywhere, and not only in counting, the boundary of existence itself runs between “is” and “is not,” and below “is not” there lies no way of being.

### An Example: Apples

Let us take the simplest case. I have five apples. I gave away eight. How many apples do I have?

The habitual answer is “minus three.” But “minus three apples” is ontologically impossible. Whatever I do with apples—eat them, give them away, sell them—I can come to zero apples, but not “below zero.” There is no state of the world in which I *have* minus three apples. There is no such real object as minus three apples; there is no object that would be “an apple with a minus sign.”

What is there in fact? I have *zero* apples—not a single one. And over and above that there is something else—an *obligation*: I must return three apples to the one to whom I remain indebted. But an obligation is not “minus three apples.” An obligation is a *positively existing* thing—a relation between me and another, a record of a debt, which is entirely real. This obligation has its own multiplicity—three—and this multiplicity is positive. The “minus” appears neither in the world of apples nor in the world of obligations; it appears only when I begin to *keep accounts*: to record both the apples on hand and the obligations taken on, on a single balance scale. It is this scale—where the minus sign comes from and what it means—that the chapter will be about. For now one thing matters: in the world there are no minus three apples; in the world there is a positively existing obligation to return three apples—a relation of magnitude three. The minus appears when what is on hand and the obligation are recorded in one coordinate system of the balance: not “minus three obligations,” but an obligation of magnitude three, recorded with a minus sign. Everything that is in the world is positively; the minus sign belongs to the *record*, not to the world. In E/R/R terms, the minus sign is a coordinate *role* (an orientation), not a negative object; see the analysis in § 1.8.3.

### What the Principle Sets for the Chapter

The principle of positive existence sets the chapter a task, not an exclusion.

It does not say “there are no negative numbers.” Negative numbers exist, and the chapter will build them. It says something else: a negative number is not an *ontological entity*—not a thing existing “in the minus.” That being so, we must answer *what* it is, since mathematics uses it, and uses it for good reason. The answer is the subject of the whole chapter: a negative number is a *position in a coordinate marking*, and as a position it exists positively. The minus sign points to a *side* of the marking, not to a deficient existence.

To arrive at this answer, we must first separate two questions in which the word “negative” sounds different and which are habitually conflated. This is where the chapter begins.

## Two Questions: Ontological and Coordinate

### Two Senses of the Word “Positive”

In this volume the word “positive” works in two different senses. They do not contradict each other, but they belong to different levels, and it is important for the reader to distinguish them from the very beginning—otherwise the principle of positive existence will be misunderstood.

The first sense is *ontological positivity*. An object is ontologically positive if it *is*—is as an actualized act of distinction or a consequence of one. This is the primary sense; it concerns the *fact of existence* and nothing more. It is precisely this sense that the principle of § 1.1 speaks of: everything that exists exists positively.

The second sense is *coordinate positivity*. A number is positive in the coordinate sense if it occupies a position on one definite side of the reference point—the side that by convention is called positive. This is an indication of a *side*, of the direction of reckoning, not an indication of the fact of existence.

The two senses agree but do not coincide. Every coordinate-positive number—$1, 2, 3, …$—is ontologically positive: it exists as a position. But the converse does not hold. Coordinate-*negative* numbers—$-1, -2, -3, …$—are *also* ontologically positive: they exist as positions, simply on the other side of the reference point. “Negativity” here is a coordinate matter; it concerns the side; ontologically, a negative position exists just as fully as a positive one.

When the principle of § 1.1 says “everything that exists is positive,” it speaks of the first sense, of ontological positivity. The minus sign in the notation $-3$ belongs to the second sense: it is coordinate marking, not ontological negation. To conflate these two senses is to decide that $-3$ “exists worse” than $3$. It does not exist worse: $-3$ is a position, and as a position it exists fully.

Here it is also worth separating three different things that are easily merged under the single word “negative” or a single sign, and whose merging would confuse the whole of Part III. The *minus sign* in the integers is coordinate marking: it indicates the side of a position relative to the reference point (the whole present chapter is about this). *Logical negation* is $\neg P$, the negative side of the act of distinction: in `Distinction` from Part II it is given by the field `negative` on a par with `positive`, as one of the two sides of a complete distinction (Chapter II.1). *Algebraic invertibility* is the opposite element, the $-a$ for which $a + (-a)$ gives the neutral element: this is an operational structure, a property of addition as an operation. These three things are kindred—none of them is negative existence—but they must not be *identified*: the minus sign is marking, logical negation is a side of distinction, invertibility is a structure of an operation. The present chapter speaks of the first—of the minus sign as a coordinate; Part II spoke of the second; the third will emerge when the chapter reaches the addition of integers.

### The Clarifying Question

Distinguishing the two senses of “positive” leads to distinguishing two kinds of *questions* in which number takes part. And this distinction is the methodological core of the whole of Part III.

When a teacher asks children, “What is $5 - 8$?” the right first move is to ask a counter-question, a *clarifying* one, rather than to compute: what system are we talking about?

Two kinds of problems are possible, and the answer depends on which of them is before us.

A problem may be *ontological*—about real existing objects: apples, balls, people. Then the answer to “$5 - 8$” is *zero* or “undefined.” Not $-3$—for, as § 1.1 showed, $-3$ apples ontologically do not exist. One cannot remove eight objects from five; one can remove five and come to zero, and there the series of objects ends.

A problem may be *coordinate*—about positions in a system with a chosen reference point: the balance of an account, temperature, a coordinate on an axis. Then the answer to “$5 - 8$” is $-3$—a position in a coordinate marking, three steps on the other side of the reference point. Here $-3$ is fully meaningful: it is a point of reckoning, not a deficient quantity of objects.

One and the same symbol “$5 - 8$” thus calls for different answers, depending on which question stands behind it. And before answering, the question must be determined.

### The Silent Transition of Standard Mathematics

Standard mathematics answers “$-3$” to “$5 - 8$” at once, without asking the clarifying question. It *silently* passes into the coordinate mode: it assumes that we are speaking of positions, and it returns a position.

This is not an error of standard mathematics. It is a *convenience*, and a justified one: in the overwhelming majority of technical problems we really are speaking of coordinates—balances, scales, axes—and the clarifying question would each time be a superfluous ceremony. Standard mathematics omits it because in its working domain the answer to it is almost always the same: “in coordinates.”

But “almost always” is not “always.” In problems sensitive to ontology—where it matters whether an object *is* or *is not*—the silent transition conceals an essential difference. It returns a coordinate answer to a question that may have been ontological, and it does so without warning. The difference between “there are five objects and a debt of three” and “there is the position $-3$ on an axis” is erased: both are written as $-3$, and it seems as if they were one and the same.

ToS keeps the convenience of standard mathematics but makes the transition *explicit*. When one writes $5 - 8 = -3$ in ToS, one thereby *declares*: we are working in the coordinate system $ℤ$. This means that a reference point has been chosen, a step of the marking has been chosen, and what is recorded is a *position* in this system, not an ontological count of real objects. The notation $-3$ is legitimate; only the silence about our having passed to coordinates is illegitimate.

*The word “positive” has two senses: ontological—the object is; coordinate—the object is on the positive side of the reference point. Coordinate-negative numbers are ontologically positive: they are positions. Hence there are two kinds of questions about number—the ontological (is there an object?) and the coordinate (where is the position?); for the ontological one, the answer to $5 - 8$ is zero; for the coordinate one, $-3$. Standard mathematics silently answers in coordinates; ToS makes the transition to coordinates a declared one.*

### Where This Leads

Having distinguished the two questions, we have also gained a direction. The integers are the answer to the coordinate question. They do not add “negative objects” to the world of objects; they set up a *system of positions* in which it is meaningful to speak of points on both sides of the reference point.

It remains to show where such a system comes from: how it unfolds from the act of distinction. The next section is occupied with this.

## Where Coordinate Marking Unfolds From

### The Reference Point Is the Choice of a Point in a Ready Series

ToS does not introduce the integers by definition, nor does it build them to make up for some shortfall in the natural number series. It *obtains* them—in the same way as Part II obtained the natural number series: by tracing what unfolds from the act of distinction and looking at what comes of it. And the unfolding here does not start from emptiness: the natural number series has already been built (Part II), it is, and coordinate marking begins with it.

Let us perform *one act* on the ready series. Let us choose some point in the series $1, 2, 3, …$ and designate it as the *reference point*—the point from which reckoning will be made. To designate a reference point is an act of distinction: to distinguish “this point is the origin” from “all the other points.” The act is pure and positive; it creates nothing out of nothing but merely singles out one point in the already available series as distinguished.

The reference point is a *chosen point* of the existing series—neither an absence nor an emptiness. This is important to hold on to from the very beginning, and the chapter will return to it specially (§ 1.4): what is taken as the reference point is something existing, not a hole in the series.

### Two-Sidedness Is a Consequence of the Choice of the Reference Point

As soon as the reference point is designated, the existing series *by itself* falls into two sides relative to it. This follows from the reference point’s being chosen *inside* the series; it is not introduced separately.

Indeed. The series $1, 2, 3, …$ is ordered (Chapter II.3): each of its points has neighbors—the preceding one and the following one. The reference point is one of the points of the series. Hence the reference point has a side where the points of the series go from it *in the direction of growth*, and a side where they go *in the direction of decrease*. The point adjacent to the reference point in the direction of growth we shall call the position $+1$; the next one after it, $+2$; and so on. The point adjacent to the reference point in the direction of decrease we shall call the position $-1$; the next one, $-2$; and so on. The reference point itself is the position $0$.

The sign of a position is an indication of the *side* relative to the reference point, and nothing more. “Plus” is the side of growth, “minus” the side of decrease; which to call which is decided by convention (§ 1.5). The two sides did not have to be introduced by a special act: they *came out* of the reference point’s being chosen inside an ordered series, and a point inside an ordered series has neighbors on two sides. Two-sided marking is the existing series read from the reference point chosen in it.

### Minus as the Second Coordinate Orientation

Here the chapter arrives at its central result about the minus side—and this result demands precision, or else it will diverge from the very mathematics of the integers. It is worth going through it slowly.

The integers are habitually drawn as a line: to the left of zero, $-1$, $-2$, $-3$, and onward; to the right, $1$, $2$, $3$, and onward. The question arises: is this picture compatible with the positive ontology of ToS, with the principle of § 1.1? One might think not—that the minus side, going “down,” leads to existence below what is present. But this would be a *wrong* reading of the minus, and the chapter will now show its wrongness.

The minus side is *not* an ontological descent below what is present. It would be so only if we read $-k$ as “a thing existing $k$ rungs below absence”—but it is precisely such a reading that the principle of § 1.1 does not admit, and ToS does not accept it. The minus side is the *second coordinate orientation*—what arises as soon as a reference point and a step are chosen. The reference point is chosen inside an ordered series; a point inside an ordered series has neighbors *on two sides*; and therefore one can reckon from the reference point *in two directions*—in the direction of growth and in the direction opposite to it. The position $-k$ means a *displacement by $k$ steps in the direction opposite to the chosen positive one*, not “existence below the first act.” This displacement is given *positively*: it is $k$ steps—a positive multiplicity—and only their *direction* is opposite. The minus carries a direction, not a deficiency of being.

That is why the minus side does not violate the principle of positive existence: $-k$ is *not* negative existence. It is a positively given directed displacement—$k$ steps in one of two orientations. Existence below what is present is ruled out by the principle of § 1.1—but a coordinate-negative position is no such existence: it is a position oriented by a displacement, and as a position it exists just as fully as a coordinate-positive one (§ 1.2). The principle of § 1.1 does not admit an ontological reading of the minus—and thereby *obliges* us to read it in coordinates: as the side of a displacement, not as a way of being.

And since the minus is a coordinate orientation of a displacement, both sides of the marking are extendable *in the same way*. The plus side is extendable: after each of its positions there is a next one. The minus side is extendable in the same way: after each of its positions there is a next one—one more step further in the opposite direction. This is the extension of the *coordinate record*, the addition of one more formal displacement, not a “generation of existence below the bottom”; and it is just as legitimate for the minus side as for the plus side. There is an asymmetry of plus and minus—but it does not lie in one side’s being finite and the other infinite. The asymmetry lies in *status*: the plus direction coincides with the direction in which the natural number series is generated (the side of the growth of acts), while the minus direction is a purely coordinate orientation opposite to it. As formal displacements, both sides are on an equal footing and equally extendable; they differ in that one side holds on to the ontological growth of the series, while the other is its coordinate mirror.

*The minus side is not an ontological descent below what is present. It is the second coordinate orientation, arising after the choice of a reference point and a step: $-k$ is a positively given displacement by $k$ steps in the direction opposite to the chosen positive one, not “existence below the first act.” That is why the minus does not violate the principle of § 1.1—it is the direction of a displacement, not negative existence. Both sides of the marking are extendable in the same way; the asymmetry of plus and minus lies not in the finiteness of one of the sides but in the minus having a purely coordinate status, while the plus coincides with the direction in which the series is generated.*

### Minus as a Property of the Choice of the Reference Point

One more thing follows from the coordinate nature of the minus, and it clarifies how coordinate marking differs from ontology.

Which position carries the minus sign depends on where the reference point is chosen. Let us move the reference point by one point in the direction of growth—and the position that was zero will become the position $+1$, the one that was $-1$ will become zero, and the whole marking will shift. A point of the natural number series that was *positive* relative to the former reference point may turn out to be *negative* relative to the new one. Whether a given element is “positive” or “negative” is a property of the *choice of the reference point*—not a property of the element itself, nor a property of the series.

This is exactly what § 1.2 called the coordinate character. The minus is not an ontological property—not a mode of existence that a thing would have by itself. The minus is a *position relative to a chosen point*, and which position it marks is set by which point is chosen as the reference point. Move the reference point, and the whole marking will change, although nothing has shifted either in the series or in the acts of distinction. What changes is the marking, not the ontology. The minus sign belongs to the *coordinate system*, not to the things marked in it.

### Coordinate Systems

The two-sided marking that has unfolded from the choice of a reference point in the existing series is not an invention for the sake of mathematics. It is what is common to, and lies at the base of, a multitude of everyday and scientific frames of reference.

*Temperature.* Twenty degrees of frost is a position twenty divisions to the cold side of the reference point, for which the freezing point of water is taken.

*Altitude.* The level of the Dead Sea, about four hundred meters below the ocean, is a position four hundred steps to one side of the reference point, for which the level of the ocean is taken.

*Balance.* An account “in the minus” by fifteen hundred is a position fifteen hundred steps to one side of the reference point, for which the zero balance is taken.

*A coordinate on an axis.* A point on the number line is a position relative to the reference point, for which the origin of coordinates is taken.

Each of these systems is a two-sided marking with a concretely chosen reference point—its own zero point, its own unit of division. And in each, the choice of the reference point is precisely a choice: the freezing point of water is taken as the zero of the Celsius scale, another state as the zero of the Kelvin scale, and one and the same physical axis of temperatures receives different markings from this.

It is worth noting that these systems behave differently on the minus side—and the difference is instructive. The Celsius scale has a physical lower limit, absolute zero; yet in the scale itself any negative value can formally be written. Altitude below sea level is physically bounded by the arrangement of the planet; yet the coordinate axis of altitudes is extendable beyond this limit too. An account balance may have a legal or bank limit; yet the bookkeeping record admits an arbitrary debt. A coordinate on a pure mathematical axis is unbounded in either direction. The difference here is no accident: in *concrete subject-matter* systems the minus side may have its own lower limit, set by the arrangement of the subject domain, whereas in *pure coordinate* mathematics there is no such limit. What all these cases have in common is not the finiteness of the minus side (it differs among them and is conditioned by subject matter) but one thing: the minus sign indicates a *side relative to the chosen reference point*. It is precisely this common feature—the coordinate character of the minus, its dependence on the reference point and on the mode—that the examples show.

*The integers* are the same marking taken in *abstraction* from particulars: $ℤ$ is a two-sided coordinate marking with an integer step, traced independently of what exactly is taken as the reference point and independently of the subject-matter limits that one or another concrete system might impose on the minus side.

### What Remains to Be Built

The marking has unfolded; next we must go through its arrangement in order. And the first thing here is the point with which everything began: the reference point. What it is, how it relates to the zero of the natural number series, and why it is not an absence—this is what the next section examines.

## The Reference Point and the Step

### What Determines a Marking

Coordinate marking has unfolded (§ 1.3): a reference point has been chosen in the existing series, and relative to it the series is read on two sides. Now we must go through the arrangement of the marking in order—and begin with the two things by which every marking is determined.

A marking is determined by a *reference point* and a *step*. The reference point is the point from which reckoning is made; by convention it is called the *zero* of the marking. The step is the unit measure of deviation from the reference point; by convention it is called the *unit* of the marking. Without a reference point there is no point to reckon from, and there is nothing to speak of a position from; without a step there is no measure of deviation, and there is nothing to speak of a position with. The reference point and the step are two choices, and both are *positive*: each is an act of distinction. The choice of the reference point is the act “this point is the origin”; the choice of the step is the act “this deviation is the unit one.” Coordinate marking stands on two acts of distinction, and to that extent it stands on the positive.

Of these two choices, the reference point requires special examination. Connected with it is a question that, if one is inattentive, turns into an apparent contradiction with Part II—and this section examines it directly.

### The Zero of Part II and the Zero of the Marking

Part II established something definite about zero, and established it firmly. Chapter II.1 showed: zero is not a number. Zero is a mark of the *absence* of an act of distinction—a sign that nothing has been accomplished. The natural number series begins with one, with the first act; zero lies *before* the series and is not a number of the series, for marking an absence and counting what has been accomplished are different things.

But the present chapter has also called the reference point of the coordinate marking a *zero*—the zero of the marking. And here the reader has the right to stop. In § 1.3 the reference point was called a *chosen point of the existing series*, positively existing. Yet Part II called *absence* zero. One and the same sign “$0$” turns out to be now an absence, now an existing chosen point. Does Part III not contradict itself—and Part II?

There is no contradiction, but to see this we must distinguish two different *modes* in which the sign “$0$” works. Part II and Part III speak of different things, and the sign “$0$” carries a different ontological status in them precisely because the modes are different.

### Two Modes of the Sign “Zero”

In Part II the sign “$0$” worked in the mode of *counting*. The question there was: how many acts of distinction have been accomplished? And zero was the answer “not one”—a mark of the absence of anything accomplished. In this mode zero really is not a number: it does not count; it marks that there is nothing to count. This is how Chapter II.1 established it, and Part III does not revoke this.

In Part III the sign “$0$” works in another mode—the mode of *marking*. The question here is not “how much has been accomplished” but “from which point to reckon.” And the zero of the marking is the answer to this other question: it is *the point that has been chosen as the reference point*. To choose a reference point is a positive act (§ 1.3); the reference point is an existing point of the series, not an absence. In the mode of marking, zero names the chosen origin; it does not mark emptiness.

One sign, two modes, two ontological statuses. In the mode of counting, “$0$” is a mark of absence; in the mode of marking, “$0$” is the name of the chosen reference point. This is *one* observation—that the sign “$0$” is used in two different jobs—rather than two contradictory statements about one object. To ask “so is zero an absence or an existing point?” is to fail to notice that the question is underdetermined: it depends on the mode. In counting, an absence. In marking, the reference point.

ToS spells out such changes of mode rather than leaving them silent. Standard mathematics uses one sign “$0$” both in counting and in marking, without noting the transition—and so “zero” seems a single object with a single meaning, although in fact it works in different modes. The chapter notes the transition directly: on beginning to speak of coordinate marking, it passes from the mode of counting to the mode of marking, and together with the mode the sign “$0$” changes its ontological status.

*The sign “zero” works in two modes. In the mode of counting (Part II), zero is a mark of the absence of accomplished acts and is not a number. In the mode of marking (Part III), zero is the name of the reference point—the chosen point of the existing series, the origin. This is a change of mode, not a contradiction: one sign is used in two different jobs, and in each it carries its own ontological status. ToS spells out the transition that standard mathematics leaves silent.*

### The Reference Point Is a Chosen Point, Not an Absence

It is worth securing this separately, for the whole chapter stands on it: the reference point of a coordinate marking is *not an absence*.

This can be seen even apart from ToS—from the arrangement of any everyday scale. The freezing point of water is taken as the zero of the Celsius scale. The freezing point of water is a fully definite physical state, present and positive, not an “absence of temperature.” Another state is taken as the zero of the Kelvin scale—absolute zero—and this too is a state, not emptiness. One and the same physical axis of temperatures receives two different markings because two different existing points are taken as the reference point in them. If the zero of a scale were an absence, there would be nothing to choose from and nowhere to move the reference point to; but the reference point is moved, and it is moved precisely because what is taken as the reference point is one existing point or another.

So it is with the zero of coordinate marking in general. It is the point chosen as the reference point—existing, positively existing. An absence cannot be chosen as the origin: from an absence there is nothing to reckon. Reckoning is made *from* something, and this something is.

### The Step

A brief word suffices about the step, for it is arranged more simply than the reference point.

The step is the unit measure of deviation from the reference point—that by which one measures how far a position is removed from the origin. To choose a step is an act of distinction: to distinguish a certain deviation as the unit one, as the measure for all others. On the Celsius scale one degree is taken as the step, on a scale of distances a meter, on the number axis a unit of length. Like the reference point, the step is *chosen*, and this choice is positive: the unit deviation is an existing measure, not emptiness.

The step of the marking is the same unit as the unit of the natural number series, only taken in a new job. In the natural number series, one was the first act of distinction, the beginning of counting (Chapter II.1). In the marking, one is the measure of deviation from the reference point—one step. These are not two different “ones” but one unit working in counting and in marking: in both it is the unit, the least, not further divisible at this level. That at another level of consideration the unit is divided—the chapter will return to this elsewhere: it is the subject of the next chapter, on the rational numbers.

### What Has Been Built

The reference point and the step have been examined. The marking has an origin—the reference point, a chosen existing point—and a measure of deviation—the step. Both are acts of distinction; both are positive. And the sign “$0$” that names the reference point does not enter into dispute with the zero of Part II: it works in another mode.

By this point in the chapter the word “zero” has already worked in several different senses, and it is worth gathering them into a table—otherwise the different zeros will get mixed up:

| Sign | Mode | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ of Part II | counting acts of distinction | a mark of the absence of an accomplished act; lies before the series $ℕ$ |
| $0$ of a coordinate | coordinate marking | the chosen reference point—an existing point, the origin |
| $0$ in a pair $(a, b)$ | technical count of steps | zero steps on the given side of the difference |
| `Z0` | standard $ℤ$ | the neutral position, the zero of the group of integers |

All four are written with one sign $0$, but they mean different things: the first, the absence of an act; the second, the chosen point of reckoning; the third, the boundary value of a step counter; the fourth, the neutral element of arithmetic. The first and the third are technical zeros, marks of absence (in a count of acts or of steps); the second and the fourth are positions (the reference point of a marking or the zero of the group). The chapter uses all four, and to mix them up would be to confuse both the ontology and the arithmetic.

Next the chapter turns to that for whose sake the marking was unfolded—to the positions themselves on both sides of the reference point, to their order, and to their notation.

## Two-Sided Marking

### Positions on Both Sides of the Reference Point

The reference point and the step have been chosen (§ 1.4). This suffices to go through the marking itself—the series of positions for whose sake it was unfolded.

A position in the marking is an indication of how many steps from the reference point, and on which side of it, a point is taken. The reference point itself is the zero position: zero steps of deviation. A point one step from the reference point on one side is the position $+1$; two steps, $+2$; three steps, $+3$. A point one step away on the other side is the position $-1$; two steps, $-2$; three steps, $-3$. Every position is fully determined by two things: the number of steps from the reference point and the side.

It is worth naming directly what does *not* happen here. No new objects appear beyond those that already are. The points of the series have been present since Part II; the reference point is chosen among them; “the position $-3$” is merely a *way of indicating* an existing point—the one that stands three steps from the reference point on the side of decrease—not a newly created entity. The marking adds nothing to the series; it only gives a way of naming its points relative to the chosen origin.

### The Order of Positions

The positions are ordered, and their order is inherited directly from the order of the existing series (Chapter II.3).

The points of the series stand in the relation “before—after.” The reference point is one of the points; the positions are points of the series named relative to the reference point. Hence the positions too stand in the same relation “before—after,” only read from the reference point. Moving from a position to its neighbor in the direction of growth—from $-2$ to $-1$, from $-1$ to the reference point, from the reference point to $+1$, from $+1$ to $+2$—we go along the series in one direction; moving back, in the other. A position “further right” in the marking is a point that comes “after” in the series; a position “further left,” one that comes “before.”

The order of positions is therefore not introduced specially: it is the order of the series read from the reference point. In this order the reference point is a point *inside*—neither the first nor the last point—and so it has neighbors on both sides. This is what secures the two-sidedness of the marking (§ 1.3): the reference point is chosen inside an ordered series, and an interior point has both a “before” and an “after.”

### The Convention of Signs

Which of the two sides of the reference point to call *positive* and which *negative* is decided by convention, and this is worth saying directly, for here it is easy to take the conventional for the essential.

The two sides of the reference point are ontologically on an equal footing. Both are sides of the existing ordered series; neither is “fuller” or “more primary” than the other in its existence. To call one “plus” and the other “minus” is to make a choice that is convenient for notation but not read off from the nature of the marking. By the accepted convention, the side in which the series *grows* is called positive; but this convention itself could have been the reverse, and the marking would have remained the same marking.

The sign of a position, then, carries two things of different nature. It carries the *side*—and this is substantive: the position $+3$ and the position $-3$ are different points, on different sides of the reference point. And it carries the *convention*—which side to mark with the minus; and this is conventional. What is substantive in the sign is that there are two sides and that they are different; what is conventional is which is called what. To conflate these two things is to suppose that “negativity” is a property of the position itself, whereas it is only the side marked with the minus by convention.

Here is the final answer to the question of § 1.1. The minus sign in the notation $-3$ does not point to a deficient, “minus” existence. It points to a side: “three steps from the reference point on the side that the convention has marked with the minus.” The position $-3$ itself is an existing point of the series and exists positively, like any other. There is no negative existence; there is a negative *side*—and it is “negative” only by the name given by convention.

It is worth noting that the difficulty this analysis removes is not new. Ancient mathematics shunned negative numbers: for it, number was multiplicity, and a multiplicity below absence is unthinkable—precisely the difficulty from which § 1.1 also started. For long centuries negative numbers were accepted reluctantly, as “false” or “fictitious” roots. A turning point came into view when they were regarded as *structure* rather than as entities: already in 1837 Hamilton proposed seeing in them elements of an algebraic construction rather than special magnitudes. The coordinate reading at which the present chapter has arrived continues this turn: a negative number is a position in a marking, not an entity existing “in the minus.”

### The Asymmetry of the Two Sides

The two sides of the reference point are ontologically on an equal footing—neither exists more fully. But in one respect they are *not* symmetric, and § 1.3 has already shown in which. It is worth bringing this together here.

The plus side—the side of growth—is potentially unbounded. The series grows: after each of its points there is a next one, for after every act of distinction a next one can be performed ($P4$). However many steps in the direction of growth have been taken, one more can be made.

The minus side is potentially unbounded in the same way. The minus position $-k$ is, as § 1.3.3 showed, a coordinate displacement by $k$ steps in the direction opposite to the chosen positive one—a positively given directed displacement. And this displacement is extendable: after every minus position there is a next one—one more step further in the same opposite direction. To extend the minus side is to extend the coordinate *record*, to add one more formal displacement; and this is just as legitimate as extending the plus side. There is no “bottom” against which the minus side would come up: it would have a bottom only if we read $-k$ as a descent along the existing points of the series below the first act—but it is precisely this ontological reading that § 1.3.3 rejected. As a coordinate displacement, the minus side is bounded by nothing.

Thus the two-sided marking is *symmetric* as a coordinate system: both sides are formal displacements from the reference point, both are extendable in the same way, neither is more finite than the other. But this symmetry is the symmetry of the *coordinate record*, not the symmetry of two ways of existing. There is an asymmetry of plus and minus—and it lies in the *status* of the sides, not in their extent. The plus side coincides with the direction in which the natural number series is *generated*: to go into the plus is to go where the series of acts of distinction grows. The minus side is a purely *coordinate* orientation—the opposite one, the mirror one; nothing in the generation of the series directly corresponds to it; it is a direction given by the *choice of the marking*. As displacements, both sides are on an equal footing and equally infinite; they differ in that one holds on to the ontological growth of the series, while the other is its coordinate mirror.

*The two sides of the reference point are ontologically on an equal footing: neither exists more fully. The sign of a position carries the side—substantively—and the convention as to which side to mark with the minus—conventionally. As a coordinate system the marking is symmetric: both sides are formal displacements from the reference point, both are extendable in the same way, and the minus side comes up against no bottom. The asymmetry of plus and minus lies in the status of the sides, not in the finiteness of one of them: the plus direction coincides with the direction in which the series is generated; the minus direction is a purely coordinate orientation opposite to it. The symmetry of $ℤ$ is the symmetry of the coordinate record, not the symmetry of positive and negative existence.*

### What Has Been Built

The marking has been gone through. There is the reference point—a chosen point of the existing series; there is the step—the measure of deviation; there are positions on both sides of the reference point, ordered by the order of the series, with a sign carrying the side. And there is the asymmetry of the sides—in status, not in extent: the plus coincides with the direction in which the series is generated, the minus is its coordinate mirror; as displacements, both sides are equally extendable.

Now what has been built can be recorded formally—we can see how the two-sided marking appears in a rigorous construction. The next section is devoted to the formal record of the integers.

## The Formal Construction

### The Integers in the Standard Library

The two-sided marking has been gone through in prose; let us record it formally. ToS does not build the integers anew: there is a ready construction for them in the standard library of Rocq, and ToS takes it as a working instrument.[^1]

The type of integers is given by three constructors:

```coq
Inductive Z : Set :=
  | Z0   : Z
  | Zpos : positive -> Z
  | Zneg : positive -> Z.
```

The three constructors correspond to the three parts of the marking. `Z0` is the reference point—the zero position. `Zpos n` is the position $n$ steps to one side of the reference point; `Zneg n`, the position $n$ steps to the other side. The type `positive` consists of the nonzero natural numbers, the multiplicity of the steps of deviation; zero steps correspond to the reference point `Z0` itself, neither to `Zpos` nor to `Zneg`. The record of the marking is direct: a reference point and two sides, and a multiplicity of steps on each side.

### The Construction via Pairs

The standard library is not the only way to record the integers. There is another construction, a theoretical one, and it is ontologically more transparent for coordinate marking. It is worth presenting, for it clarifies what an integer is.

An integer can be given as an equivalence class of *pairs* of natural numbers. A pair $(a, b)$ is read thus: $a$ steps of deviation to one side and $b$ steps to the other. The resulting position is $a - b$—where and by how much one has been displaced in the end. Two pairs give one and the same position when the net result of their displacements coincides; formally, $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ are equivalent if and only if $a + d = b + c$. An integer is a class of such equivalent pairs: the class $[(a, b)]$ corresponds to the position $a - b$.

Here a reservation about the status of zero is needed. For the pair $(0, 3)$ to give $-3$, and $(3, 0)$ to give $+3$, the pairs need *zero* as an admissible number of steps. But Part II established that the natural number series of ToS is $ℕ = \{1, 2, 3, …\}$, and zero does not belong to it (Chapter II.1). There is no contradiction here: the construction via pairs uses a *technical count of steps*—$ℕ_0 = \{0, 1, 2, …\}$, which admits a zero multiplicity of steps—rather than the primary natural number series. And zero in it means the *absence of steps on the given side* of the difference, not “the number of an accomplished act” and not a primary natural number: the pair $(0, 3)$ says “zero steps to one side, three to the other.” This is the same technical zero as the zero-boundary of Part II: a mark of absence, not a positive count. The primacy of one (Chapter II.1) is not thereby revoked: the primary series still begins with one, while $ℕ_0$ is an auxiliary superstructure for recording differences, in which zero is allowed to be the boundary value of a step counter.

This construction is transparent in the following way. The pair $(a, b)$ directly records the *resulting displacement* relative to the reference point—how many steps to one side, how many to the other. And the equivalence class reflects the fact that one and the same position is recorded by different pairs: $(5, 3)$ and $(7, 5)$ both mean “two steps in the direction of growth,” since $5 - 3 = 7 - 5$. A position in the marking does not depend on which pair exactly it is recorded by—and the equivalence class is the formal record of this independence.

It is worth seeing what this construction gives for understanding the minus. An integer appears here as a *formal difference of two positively given multiplicities*—$a$ steps and $b$ steps—*not* as a point “below” the natural number series. Negativity arises as the *result of comparing two positive counts*, not as negative existence: $a - b = -3$ when $b$ exceeds $a$ by three. Both counts, $a$ and $b$, are positive; the “minus” is only the outcome of their comparison. And precisely for this reason the construction via pairs builds the *complete* group of integers, not some bounded “minus side”: the difference $a - b$ is defined for any $a, b$, and it yields negative values without any lower bound—exactly as it yields positive ones. Pairs support the reading of the integer at which § 1.3.3 arrived: an oriented displacement, a formal difference, not a descent below what is present.

The two constructions—via three constructors and via classes of pairs—are equivalent: they give one and the same type of integers. Rocq uses the first as computationally convenient; ToS keeps the second as the ontological reading—an integer is a resulting displacement from the reference point, a formal difference of two positive counts.

### The Symmetry of the Record and the Three Modes of the Integer

Here it is worth saying directly in what sense the formal record `Z` is symmetric and whether this symmetry agrees with the ontology of the chapter.

The type `Z` of the standard library is *symmetric*: `Zpos` and `Zneg` are arranged in the same way, both sides take any multiplicity of steps `positive`, and no “bottom” of the minus side is reflected in the type. The record `Z` draws the integers as a line extendable in both directions in the same way.

And this symmetry is *correct*. It agrees with what the chapter established in § 1.3.3 and § 1.5.4: the minus side is the second coordinate orientation, a formal displacement extendable in the same way as the plus side; it has no ontological “bottom.” The type `Z`, recording both sides in the same way, records the marking *correctly*: as a symmetric coordinate system. The symmetry of `Z` is the symmetry of the *coordinate record*—and such precisely is the marking of the integers.

But this symmetry is not a symmetry of two ways of *existing*, and here it is important not to confuse levels. The coordinate symmetry of `Z` does not mean that “positive” and “negative” existence are on an equal footing as two kinds of being: there is no negative existence at all (§ 1.1). What is symmetric is the *record*—the two orientations of displacement are on an equal footing and equally extendable; what is asymmetric is the *status* of the sides—the plus coincides with the direction in which the series is generated, the minus is its coordinate mirror (§ 1.5.4). The type `Z` carries the first—the symmetry of the record—and carries it correctly; the second—the asymmetry of status—is spelled out by the prose of the chapter. This is a *division of labor*, not a divergence between the instrument and the ontology: the record fixes the coordinate structure, the text names the ontological status of the sides.

It is convenient to bring together the *three modes* in which the integer—and with it the “negative”—appears differently:

| Mode | What the negative is | The minus side |
|---|---|---|
| ontological counting of objects | impossible as an object: there are no objects “in the minus” | below zero there is nothing |
| coordinate marking | the orientation of a displacement relative to the reference point | the second orientation, extendable |
| formal integers $ℤ$ | an oriented displacement / a formal difference of two positive counts | unbounded, like the plus |

The first mode is the mode of Part II and of § 1.1: there is no object “in the minus.” The second and the third are the mode of the present chapter: the minus is a coordinate orientation, and in the formal record $ℤ$ it is extendable without bound. The chapter does not choose between the modes and does not declare one true and the other false; it *distinguishes* them and shows that the question “how many objects” and the question “which position” are different questions (§ 1.2). The type `Z` formalizes the third mode, and formalizes it exactly.

*ToS takes the integers ready-made from the standard library of Rocq: the type `Z` with three constructors—the reference point `Z0` and the two sides `Zpos`, `Zneg`. The equivalent construction via classes of pairs reads the integer as a formal difference of two positive counts. The symmetry of `Z` is correct—it is the symmetry of the coordinate record, and such is the marking of the integers. What is asymmetric is the status of the sides, not their extent: the plus coincides with the generation of the series, the minus is the coordinate mirror; this is what the prose of the chapter adds to the symmetric record.*

### What Has Been Recorded

The two-sided marking has received a formal record. Next the chapter turns to what is performed on the integers—to addition and, in particular, to multiplication with its rule of signs, which is ordinarily memorized and which ToS derives.

## The Integer: Position and Difference

### Multiplication Is the Repetition of a Displacement

Addition and multiplication are performed on the integers. Addition is transparent: to add two integers is to perform one displacement from the reference point and then, from the position obtained, another. Multiplication requires examination, and in particular the rule of signs, which is ordinarily memorized and which ToS derives.

Let us take multiplication in the coordinate reading. To multiply an integer $a$ by an integer $b$ is to *repeat* the displacement $a$ exactly $b$ times. If $a$ is a displacement by two steps in the direction of growth, then $a$ repeated three times is a displacement by six steps in the direction of growth: $2 \times 3 = 6$. Multiplication is the repetition of a displacement, and the result is the final position to which the repeated displacement leads.

As long as both factors are displacements in the direction of growth, everything goes straightforwardly. $2 \times 3$—a displacement by two steps that way, repeated three times—is the position $6$. The difficulty—and the rule of signs—begins where the side enters the matter.

### The Factor Carries the Direction of Repetition

Let us examine what the sign of each factor contributes to multiplication. And we must begin with the fact that in multiplication the two factors play *different* roles.

The first factor, $a$, is the *displacement* that is repeated. Its sign is the side of the displacement: $+2$ is a displacement by two steps in the direction of growth, $-2$ by two steps in the direction of decrease.

The second factor, $b$, is not a displacement. It is an *instruction for how to repeat* the displacement $a$. And this instruction, like every directed action, has its own side. A positive $b$ means: repeat the displacement $a$ *directly*—as it is given. A negative $b$ means: repeat the displacement $a$ with its direction *reversed*. This can conveniently be said more precisely by linking it with the opposite displacement: to repeat $a$ a negative number of times is to repeat the *opposite* displacement $-a$ the corresponding positive number of times. The sign of the second factor carries the side of the *repetition* itself—directly or with a reversal—rather than the side of the displacement; and the reversal is the passage to the opposite displacement. In the usual notation this gives $a \cdot (-n) = -(a \cdot n)$ and $(-a) \cdot (-n) = a \cdot n$.

This is not two different multiplications and not a special rule for negative numbers. It is one multiplication—the repetition of a displacement—in which the repetition, as a directed action, has two sides, just as the marking had two sides (§ 1.5). The first factor sets what to repeat; the second, how many times and in which direction.

A reservation is needed here, or else the role reading may be taken as stronger than it is. The different roles of the two factors—displacement and instruction to repeat—are a *device of exposition*, a way to *understand* the rule of signs, not an assertion that $a \times b$ and $b \times a$ have different ontological natures. Multiplication of integers is *commutative*: $a \times b = b \times a$, and this is a proved theorem of standard arithmetic, which ToS does not dispute. To *derive* the rule of signs, the chapter temporarily reads the first factor as a displacement and the second as a repetition operator; but once the operation is built, it is proved that the result does not depend on such a role reading—multiplication is symmetric in its factors. The role reading is scaffolding by which the understanding of the rule of signs is erected; the erected rule stands without the scaffolding too, and commutativity remains in force.

### The Rule of Signs

Now the rule of signs is derived directly—by examining four cases.

Let the displacement be two steps and the repetitions three. *Plus times plus:* $(+2) \times (+3)$—a displacement by two steps in the direction of growth, repeated directly three times—is the position $+6$. *Minus times plus:* $(-2) \times (+3)$—a displacement by two steps in the direction of decrease, repeated directly three times—is the position $-6$. *Plus times minus:* $(+2) \times (-3)$—a displacement by two steps in the direction of growth, repeated with reversal three times; the reversal turns each step around, and a displacement in the direction of growth, repeated with reversal, leads in the direction of decrease—is the position $-6$.

*Minus times minus:* $(-2) \times (-3)$. The displacement: two steps in the direction of decrease. The repetition: with reversal. The reversal turns the direction of the displacement around: a displacement in the direction of decrease, repeated with reversal, leads in the direction of *growth*. Three repetitions: the position $+6$.

Here is the rule: $(-) \times (-) = (+)$. It is neither memorized nor introduced by convention. It is *derived* from the fact that repetition is a directed action with two sides: a negative factor reverses the direction, and two reversals—the reversal of the displacement and the reversal brought in by the second negative factor—return to the original side. Minus times minus is plus because to reverse a direction twice is to return to the original direction. This is a structural property of two-sidedness, not an algebraic convention.

*Multiplication of integers is the repetition of a displacement. The first factor is the displacement; its sign is the side of the displacement. The second factor is the instruction for how to repeat; its sign is the side of the repetition: directly or with reversal. The rule $(-) \times (-) = (+)$ is derived from this: two reversals of direction return to the original direction. The rule of signs is a structural property of the two-sided marking, not a memorized convention.*

### The Integer: Position and Difference

The chapter has gone through the integer as a *position*—a point in the two-sided marking. In concluding, it is worth noting that the integer also has another side, and it depends on the level at which the integer is considered.

Taken as a position, the integer is *atomic*: it is simply a point of the marking, and at this level it has no internal arrangement. For computation—addition, multiplication—this suffices: positions are added and multiplied without looking inside.

But § 1.6 also showed another reading: the integer is a class of pairs $[(a, b)]$, a resulting displacement—$a$ steps to one side, $b$ to the other, with the net result $a - b$. Taken in this way, the integer is a *difference*—and then it is a system rather than an atom: there is an internal distinction in it between the component of one side and the component of the other. The position $+2$ as an atom is simply a point; the same $+2$ as a difference is $5 - 3$, or $7 - 5$, or another pair with the same net result.

This is a change of the level of consideration, not a contradiction—the very change that Part II noted for number and system in general. One and the same integer is atomic at the level of position and composite at the level of difference; which level to take is decided by the task. For arithmetic, the position-atom suffices. For building the group structure—where the integers will appear as the first additive group, and the opposite element will receive an exact definition through operational invertibility—it will be necessary to remember that the integer is a difference. But this is the subject of the Part devoted to algebra, not of the present chapter.

### What Has Been Covered

Addition and multiplication on the integers have been covered; the rule of signs has been derived. And the integer has been seen from two sides: as a position-atom and as a difference-system. It remains to bring the chapter together and to outline where it leads. This is the subject of the concluding section.

## Summary. The Transition to the Rationals

### What the Chapter Has Covered

The chapter has gone through the integers—as a coordinate marking that has unfolded from the act of distinction, not as a new sort of numbers introduced by agreement.

The chapter began with the principle of positive existence: existence itself is never negative; there is no ontological status below absence; the minus sign is coordinate marking—and it must not be confused either with logical negation or with algebraic invertibility (§ 1.1). From this, two kinds of questions about number were separated—the ontological and the coordinate—and it was shown that a negative number is the answer to the coordinate question: a position, not an object (§ 1.2). Then the chapter traced how the marking itself unfolds: the choice of a reference point in the existing series is one act of distinction, and two-sidedness comes from the reference point’s being chosen inside an ordered series (§ 1.3). The reference point and the step were examined as two positive acts, and the double status of zero—absence in counting, the reference point in marking—was resolved as a change of mode (§ 1.4). The two-sided marking was gone through: the positions, their order, the convention of signs (§ 1.5). The formal record was given—the type $ℤ$ of the standard library and the construction via pairs (§ 1.6). And the rule of signs was derived—as a structural property of two-sidedness, not as a memorized convention (§ 1.7).

It is worth speaking directly about the formal side of this chapter and how it differs from the chapters of Part II. The chapters of Part II rested on the ToS Rocq repository’s own files—`Distinction.v`, `PrimalityOfOne.v`, `Binarity.v`, and others—in which the natural number series and its arrangement are derived and proved. For the integers such a *separate* ToS file now *exists*: `CoordinateIntegers.v` builds its own coordinate construction of the integer (as a formal difference) and proves its isomorphism with the ready type `Z` from `ZArith` (§ 1.6.1). Both this construction and its isomorphism with `Z` are machine-checked—not only the standard `Z` itself. The ontological content of the chapter—that a negative number is read as a coordinate position and a formal difference, not as negative existence—is carried by the prose *over* this machine construction; the standard `Z` remains the target of the isomorphism.[^2]

### The Integer Is a Position

The outcome of the chapter can be reduced to one thing. An integer is a *position in a coordinate marking*—a point taken relative to the chosen reference point, on one side of it or the other, so many steps away.

This removes the very misunderstanding for whose sake the chapter was written. “Minus three” is not “less than nothing,” not a deficient or negative existence. “Minus three” is a position—an existing point of the series, standing three steps from the reference point on the side that convention has marked with the minus. As a position it exists positively, on a par with any other. The integers do not add “negative objects” to the world; they set up a system of positions in which it is meaningful to speak of points on both sides of the reference point.

And the chapter has noted a result of ToS’s own that refines the habitual picture. The integers are *symmetric* as a coordinate system: both sides of the reference point are formal displacements, extendable in the same way, neither more finite than the other. But this symmetry is the symmetry of the coordinate *record*, not the symmetry of two ways of existing. The minus sign does not mean a second, negative region of existence: it means the opposite orientation of a formal displacement relative to the reference point. There is still an asymmetry of plus and minus—but in the *status* of the sides, not in their extent: the plus direction coincides with the direction in which the natural number series is generated, while the minus direction is a purely coordinate mirror opposite to it. The symmetry of $ℤ$ is the symmetry of the coordinate record, not the symmetry of positive and negative existence; there is no negative existence at all—there is a negative *side* of the marking.

### E/R/R Analysis: The Integer-Position as a System

The integer, reduced to a position (§ 1.8.2), is worth analyzing by E/R/R (Part I): the analysis secures why the minus is not negative existence. A reservation about status: this chapter now has *its own* anchor file—`CoordinateIntegers.v` (§ 1.8.1) builds a record of the integer as a formal difference (`Record FormalDiff`) and proves its isomorphism with the standard `Z`. Hence the “system” here is a coordinate marking with its own machine construction (`FormalDiff`), isomorphic to `Z`; both `Z` itself and the isomorphism are machine-checked. The standard `Z` remains the target of the isomorphism (§ 1.6.1).
We conduct the analysis in the ontological order Rules $\to$ Roles $\to$ Elements.

*Rules* (the laws $L5$, $L2$, $L3$). The constitution of the marking is the reference point (the choice of a point in the existing series) and the step (the unit displacement); two-sidedness follows from the choice of the reference point *inside* an ordered series (§ 1.3–1.5). To these is added the rule of signs: multiplication is the repetition of a displacement, $(-a)\cdot(-n)=a\cdot n$—a structural property of two-sidedness, not a memorized convention (§ 1.7). And an equivalent rule: the integer as a *formal difference* of two positive counts (pairs with the identification $a+d=b+c$, § 1.6.2). The universal $L1$–$L5$ are in force; distinctive here are $L5$ (the order of positions) and $L2$/$L3$ (exactly two sides of the reference point).

*Roles—the significance of positions* (the law $L4$). The integer is the role of a *position*: a point relative to the reference point, on one side or the other, so many steps away. The sign is the role of *orientation*: which side of the reference point. Zero here is the role of the *reference point* (the origin), distinct from the counting zero of Part II (§ 1.4). And the role *asymmetry*: the plus side coincides with the direction in which the series is generated, the minus is its coordinate mirror; the sides are equally extendable and differ in status (§ 1.8.2).

*Elements—the bearers* (the law $L1$ and the principle $P4$). The elements are positions (equally: formal differences), composed of the reference point and steps; the material of the steps is the natural counts of Part II. By $L1$, each position is identical with itself. By $P4$, every integer is a *finite* displacement from the reference point—a finite number of steps to one side—although both sides are extendable without bound.

Let us gather the analysis into a table.

| Component | What it determines | E/R/R |
|---|---|---|
| reference point $+$ step; two-sidedness; rule of signs | the constitution of the marking | Rule |
| formal difference (pairs, $a+d=b+c$) | the integer as a difference | Rule |
| position relative to the reference point; sign = orientation | the role of place and side | Role |
| status: plus—generation, minus—mirror | the asymmetry of roles | Role |
| positions/differences (over $ℕ$; formally `Z`) | bearers ($P4$) | Element |
| laws $L1$–$L5$ (here $L5$, $L2$/$L3$) | the universal layer | Rule (univ.) |

*Well-formedness.* The labeling is unambiguous: positions are elements, place and orientation are roles, the reference point with the step and the signs are rules. $P1$ is observed: positions are points *inside* the marking relative to the reference point, and the reference-point zero is not mixed with the counting zero (§ 1.4). And here the analysis turns into a *diagnosis*. “Minus three” as “less than nothing,” as a negative *object*, is a confusion of categories: the minus sign is a coordinate *role* (an orientation relative to the reference point), not an element-object of negative existence. In the world there are no “minus three apples”—there is a positive obligation of magnitude three, recorded with a minus sign (§ 1.1.2). And three things must not be mixed: the minus sign (a coordinate), logical negation ($\lnot P$), and algebraic invertibility (§ 1.2.1). The symmetry of `Z` is the symmetry of the coordinate *record*, not of two ways of existing.

What the analysis yields. The integer is again a *role* (a position), like number on the structural path (Chapter II.5), but now a *two-sided* one: orientation has been added to place. There is no “negative existence”—there is a negative *side* of the marking; in this way E/R/R shows that the seeming paradox of the negative number does not form, while upholding the principle of positive existence (§ 1.1). And the same move sets the line of Part III: the coordinate marking has been built with an *integer* step; the next chapter will make the step *fractional* and unfold the rational numbers.

### What the Marking Does Not Yet Give

The chapter has gone through one side of the nature of coordinate marking—*two-sidedness*. But there is something else in a marking, which the integers do not yet give.

The integers mark positions with an *integer* step: the reference point, one step, two, three. Between the reference point and the position $+1$ there is not a single marked point; the step is indivisible, and the marking proceeds by it as by an indivisible measure. But if we take the act of distinction from yet another side—ask not “on which side and by how many whole steps” but “what lies *inside* the step,” what a third of a step or two fifths of a step mean—yet another side of the marking will unfold. Not two-sidedness but *commensurability*: a marking with a fractional step, in which it is meaningful to speak of positions between the integers.

This is the next thing that unfolds from the act of distinction, and it will give the rational numbers.

### The Transition to the Rational Numbers

The integers have given the two-sided coordinate marking. The natural number series occupied one side of the reference point in it; the other side consists of positions displaced in the opposite orientation. The marking is two-sided, but its step is whole and indivisible.

The next chapter will take the act of distinction from the side of commensurability and trace what unfolds when the step of the marking becomes fractional. In this way the rational numbers will unfold—a coordinate marking with a commensurable step. Chapter III.2 is devoted to them.
