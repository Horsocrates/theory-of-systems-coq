## Where Commensurable Marking Unfolds From

### What the Previous Chapter Gave and Did Not Give

Chapter III.1 went through the integers—the coordinate marking that unfolded from the act of distinction taken as a directed movement from the reference point. This marking is two-sided: positions lie on both sides of the chosen reference point.

But the step of this marking is *whole and indivisible* in the mode in which the integers carry out the marking. The reference point, one step, two, three; in the mode of integer marking there is not a single marked point between the reference point and the position $+1$. And this is not because a distinction is *impossible* there—it is because the given mode of marking sets no internal marks of the step: the integers mark with the step as an indivisible measure, and, staying in their mode, they have nothing to say about a position between two neighboring integers. This is no flaw of the integers—they mark exactly what they mark, and they mark it flawlessly. But coordinate marking carries within itself one more side, one more mode, which the integers have not touched.

The present chapter will take the act of distinction from this side, not yet gone through, and trace what unfolds from it. This is how the rational numbers will be obtained—and obtained, like the integers, by tracing what is already laid down in the act of distinction, not by making up for a shortfall.

### The Act of Distinction Taken Inside the Step

The integers answered the question: on which side of the reference point, and by how many *whole* steps, a position is taken. Let us now take another question—one *inside* the step rather than *outside* it. What lies between the reference point and the position $+1$? What do half a step, a third of a step, two fifths of a step mean?

To ask this is to perform an act of distinction inside the step. Until now the step has been an indivisible measure: the marking moved by it without looking inside. But to distinguish a point *between* the reference point and the position $+1$ is an act of distinction like any other: to draw a boundary, to separate one thing from another. Having performed it, we mark inside the step a point that was not in the marking before.

And as soon as this point is marked, one more side of the marking unfolds. Not two-sidedness—two-sidedness was given by the integers. What unfolds is *commensurability*: the possibility of carrying out the marking with a *fractional* step, smaller than a whole one, and of speaking of positions lying between the integers. A rational number is a position in such a marking—a commensurable one.

### Commensurable Marking Is Obtained, Not Introduced

It is worth naming directly the manner in which the chapter proceeds here, for it is the same manner as in the chapter on the integers.

Commensurable marking *can* also be motivated in the habitual way—as an extension in which division becomes performable: in the integers not every division comes out evenly ($7$ is not divisible by $3$), and fractions supply this performability. Such a motivation is not false, and the chapter does not reject it. But in ToS it is not *primary*. The primary motive here is positive, not negative (not “something is lacking in the integers”): the act of distinction is taken *inside* the step, and thereby commensurable marking unfolds. ToS traces what unfolds from the act of distinction taken inside the step—and sees that a marking with a fractional step unfolds. That division turns out to be performable in this marking is a *gain* of such unfolding, its consequence, not a task set in advance. The integers are in no way deficient here: they mark with a whole step, and mark it flawlessly. It is just that the whole step is not the only thing that unfolds from the act of distinction; the fractional one unfolds as well, once the act of distinction is performed inside the step.

*The integers gave a marking with a whole, indivisible step. The act of distinction performed *inside* the step—the distinction of a point between the reference point and the neighboring integer position—unfolds commensurable marking: a marking with a fractional step, in which positions between the integers are meaningful. A rational number is a position in a commensurable marking. The primary motive here is positive—the act of distinction taken inside the step—while the performability of division is a gain of this unfolding, not a task set in advance.*

### What Lies Ahead for the Chapter

Commensurable marking has unfolded; next we must go through its arrangement. And the first thing that requires examination is commensurability itself: what kind of relation connects the fractional step with the whole one. The chapter begins with it.

## What Commensurability Is

### Commensurability Is a Relation of Multiplicities

Commensurable marking has unfolded from the act of distinction taken inside the step (§ 2.1). Now we must say exactly what *commensurability* itself is—the relation by which the fractional step is connected with the whole one.

The commensurability of two steps is a *relation of their multiplicities*—an indication of how many steps of one kind make up how many steps of the other. Let us take the fraction $p/q$ apart into its components—and do so exactly, for $p/q$ can be read in two ways, and it is important not to mix the two readings.

The first reading is the *positional* one, and in this chapter it is the main one. For the position $p/q$, the denominator $q$ sets the *scale*: the unit step is divided into $q$ equal parts, and the new small step equals $1/q$ of the former one. The numerator $p$ says *how many* such small steps are taken. Then $p/q$ is the position obtained by the $p$-fold repetition of the small step $1/q$:

$$
\frac{p}{q} \;=\; p \cdot \frac{1}{q}.
$$

The sign shows the side relative to the reference point. Thus $2/5$ is the position obtained by a twofold repetition of the step $1/5$: the unit step is divided into five parts, and two are taken.

The second reading takes $p/q$ as a *new step*: a step that, repeated $q$-fold, gives $p$ former steps. The two readings agree—they describe one commensurability from different sides—but in the present chapter the positional one will be the main one: $p/q$ is first of all a *position* in a commensurable marking, not a new step.

Let us gather the components of the position $p/q$ into a table:

| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| sign | the side relative to the reference point |
| $q$ | scale: into how many equal parts the unit step is divided |
| $1/q$ | the small step—the $q$-th part of the unit step |
| $p$ | the number of small steps taken from the reference point |
| $p/q$ | a position in the commensurable marking: $p$ times $1/q$ |

A rational number is precisely a position in a marking carried out with the commensurable step $1/q$. The position $2/5$ is a point standing two small steps from the reference point, each of which is a fifth of the unit step. Commensurability connects two markings—the former one and a finer one—by the relation of their steps.

### Commensurability Requires the Step to Be a System

Here lies the ontological knot of the whole chapter, and it must be gone through slowly.

The commensurability $p/q$ speaks of the *division* of the former step into $q$ parts. But something can be divided into parts only when this something has an *internal arrangement*—when there is something to divide it into. If the step is taken as an *atom*—as something having no internal distinctions—then there is nothing to divide it into: inside an atom there are no parts, no boundaries along which a division could run. “A third of an atom” is meaningless because an atom is by definition that inside which there are no distinctions, not because it is ruled out.

Commensurability, then, makes sense only where the step is taken as a *system*—as something open to internal distinctions. Then “a third of a step” is meaningful: it is a finer distinction *inside* the step, a boundary drawn where there was no boundary before. But precision is needed here: new boundaries alone are not yet enough. Dividing the step into $q$ parts requires $q-1$ new acts of distinction inside it—but $q-1$ boundaries placed at random would divide the step into *arbitrary*, unequal parts. Rational commensurability requires more: that all $q$ parts obtained be *equal*—be one and the same small step. Therefore a *rule of uniformity* must be added to the $q-1$ boundaries: the parts cut off by the boundaries are one and the same repeated step $1/q$. Without this rule there would simply be a partition of the interval into pieces, not a rational marking. Rationality is not “there are points inside the step” but “the points are generated by a repeated equal small step”: $q-1$ boundaries *plus* uniformity, plus the retained order of the parts. Such a division can be performed only if the step is a system capable of accommodating these distinctions.

This is the work of the same distinction of levels that ToS draws everywhere. One and the same step can be taken as an atom—and then it is indivisible—and as a system—and then it is divisible. Which level of consideration to choose is decided by the task. The integers (Chapter III.1) took the step as an atom: for marking by integer positions the internal arrangement of the step is not needed, and there the step is indivisible. The rational numbers take the step as a system: commensurable marking is precisely a marking in which the step is open to internal distinctions.

*The commensurability of two steps is a relation of their multiplicities: the position $p/q$ is the $p$-fold repetition of the small step $1/q$. Commensurability makes sense only where the step is taken as a system—as something open to internal distinctions. Dividing the step into $q$ equal parts requires not only $q-1$ new boundaries but also a rule of uniformity: all the parts are one and the same small step. A step taken as an atom is indivisible: inside an atom there are no boundaries along which a division could run. Commensurable marking is a marking in which the step is taken as a system, not as an atom.*

### What Remains to Be Clarified

What has been said rests on the fact that one and the same step—and, in particular, the unit of the marking itself—can be taken now as an atom, now as a system. But Part II established one as the first act of distinction, as an atom. Is there not a contradiction here? This question—about the double status of one—comes up next.

## The Double Status of One

### An Apparent Contradiction

Commensurability requires the step to be taken as a system—as something open to internal distinctions (§ 2.2). In particular, it requires this of the *unit* of the marking: it is possible to speak of a third of the unit, of two fifths of the unit, only when the unit is divisible, when there is something inside it to divide it into.

But Part II established something else about one. Chapter II.1 showed: one is *the first act of distinction itself*. The first act is that with which every distinction begins, and there are no distinctions inside it: it is itself the boundary that draws a difference, not something along which a boundary runs. In this sense one, as Part II takes it, is *atomic*: “a third of the first act” is as meaningless as “a third of an atom” in general (§ 2.2).

One and the same symbol—“$1$”—thus turns out to be now an atom, now a system. In Part II one is indivisible; in the present chapter one is divided into any number of parts. Does Part III not contradict what Part II established—and itself?

### Two Levels of Consideration of One

There is no contradiction, and the appearance of it is removed by the same distinction by which the chapter on the integers removed the apparent contradiction about zero (Chapter III.1, § 1.4). The sign “$1$” works at two different *levels of consideration*, and at each it carries its own status.

At the level of *counting*, one is the first act of distinction. The question here is how many acts have been accomplished; one is the answer “one act,” and this act is atomic: it is the boundary of a distinction, and there is nothing to divide it into. This is how Part II established one, and Part III does not revoke this.

At the level of *commensurable marking*, one is something else—an *interval*, not an act. It is the unit interval of the marking, the stretch from the reference point to the position $+1$. And this interval is a system: inside it distinctions can be drawn—points lying between its ends can be marked. At this level one is divisible, and “a third of one” is meaningful: it is a point marked inside the unit interval by an act of finer distinction.

One sign, two levels, two statuses. At the level of counting, “$1$” is the first act, an atom. At the level of marking, “$1$” is the unit interval, a system. This is one observation—that the sign “$1$” is used in two different jobs—rather than two contradictory statements about one object. To ask “so is one divisible or indivisible?” is to fail to notice that the question is underdetermined: it depends on the level. In counting, indivisible. In marking, divisible.

### The Same Change of Mode as for Zero

It is worth seeing that exactly the move by which Chapter III.1 dealt with zero is repeated here.

With zero it was like this. In the mode of counting (Part II), zero is a mark of the absence of accomplished acts. In the mode of marking (Chapter III.1), zero is the name of the reference point—an existing chosen point. One sign, two modes, two statuses; and Chapter III.1 showed that this is a change of mode, which ToS spells out explicitly, not a contradiction.

With one it is the same. In the mode of counting, one is the first act, an atom. In the mode of marking, one is the unit interval, a system. One sign, two levels, two statuses. And as with zero, this is a change of the level of consideration, not a contradiction. Coordinate marking in general is a different mode from counting: counting asks “how much has been accomplished,” marking asks “where is the position.” The signs “$0$” and “$1$,” passing from counting into marking, change their ontological status together with the mode: zero turns from a mark of absence into the reference point, one from an act-atom into an interval-system. ToS spells out such changes rather than leaving them silent; standard mathematics uses the same signs in both modes without noting the transition, and so the divisibility of one seems something self-evident, whereas it is a property of one only *at the level of marking*.

*The sign “one” works at two levels. At the level of counting (Part II), one is the first act of distinction—an atom, indivisible. At the level of commensurable marking (the present chapter), one is the unit interval—a system, divisible into any number of parts. This is a change of the level of consideration, not a contradiction—the same as for zero (Chapter III.1, § 1.4): zero turns from a mark of absence into the reference point, one from an act-atom into an interval-system. ToS spells out the transition that standard mathematics leaves silent.*

It is convenient to gather the modes in which the sign “one” works into a table:

| Mode | What “one” is | Divisible? |
|---|---|---|
| counting (Part II) | the first act of distinction | no: an atom |
| integer marking (Chapter III.1) | one step of the marking | its inside is not considered |
| commensurable marking (this chapter) | the unit interval | yes |
| the field $ℚ$ (§ 2.6) | the multiplicative unit | yes: inverse elements are defined relative to it |

One sign “$1$,” four modes. In counting, one is an atom; in integer marking, a step into which that mode does not look; in commensurable marking, a divisible unit interval; in the field, the neutral element of multiplication. To mix up the modes would be to take the divisibility or indivisibility of one for its unconditional property, whereas it is a property of one *in the given mode*.

### What Follows from This

The double status of one has been resolved: at the level of marking one is a divisible interval, and commensurability over it is legitimate. But before building commensurable marking further, we must examine one special case—the one where commensurability is demanded with zero, with a denominator equal to the reference point. This is division by zero, and the next section is about it.

## Division by Zero as Structural Nonsense

### A Special Case: The Denominator Is the Reference Point

The commensurability $p/q$ sets the fractional step through the denominator $q$—through the number of parts into which the former step is divided (§ 2.2). The denominator is a natural number: into two parts, into three, into five. But what if one demands a commensurability in which the denominator equals *zero*—$p/0$?

Standard mathematics answers this by ruling it out: one may not divide by zero. This exclusion is correct, but in the standard exposition it is presented as a *rule*—as a condition that must be observed because otherwise paradoxes result. ToS answers differently and more deeply: commensurability with zero is *structural nonsense*—the absence of an action as such, not an excluded action—and the matter does not rest on a rule that excludes it.

### There Is Nothing to Commensurate with Zero

Let us examine what the commensurability $p/q$ would mean for $q = 0$.

The commensurability $p/q$, by § 2.2, means: the new step is such that $q$ of its repetitions make up $p$ former ones. Let us substitute $q = 0$. It comes out: the new step is such that *zero* of its repetitions make up $p$ former ones. But zero repetitions are the *absence of an act*—nothing has been repeated, not a single step has been taken. Commensurability at $q = 0$ would require setting a step through what has not been accomplished—defining a measure while relying on the absence of any application of it. This is not a difficult task, nor one that is ruled out—it is the *absence* of a task: there is nothing to set and nothing to set it from.

Let us say the same more precisely—through the *equation of commensurability*. The commensurability $p/q$ requires finding a new step $u$ such that $q$ repetitions of this step make up $p$ former steps:

$$
q \cdot u \;=\; p.
$$

It is here that we must check what becomes of this equation at $q = 0$. Let us substitute: $0 \cdot u = p$. And let us examine two cases.

If $p \neq 0$, the equation $0 \cdot u = p$ *has no solution*: zero repetitions of any step give zero, not $p$; no step $u$ satisfies the condition. There is no commensurability, for there is no step sought.

If, however, $p = 0$, the equation $0 \cdot u = 0$ holds for *any* $u$: every step satisfies it. And this is no better than the first case but the same nonsense from the other side: the equation does not *single out* any definite step; there are too many solutions; the measure is not set. Again there is no commensurability—now from a loss of determinacy rather than from a lack of a solution.

In both cases—$p \neq 0$ and $p = 0$—the condition $q \cdot u = p$ at $q = 0$ ceases to set a measure: either there is no solution at all, or it is not unique and therefore determines nothing. The denominator $q$ is the *number of parts* into which the unit step is divided, the number of repetitions of the small step—neither a coordinate position nor the reference point. And $q = 0$ means an attempt to set a scale through a zero number of parts, not “dividing the reference point”—and a zero number of parts sets no measure. “Dividing by zero” does not go beyond the bounds of what is permitted—it does not even reach the point of being an action: the equation of commensurability at $q = 0$ defines no operation.

### Why Division by Zero Cannot Be “Fixed”

From this one can also see why division by zero cannot be “fixed”—have its definition completed so that it would give a result after all.

From time to time it is proposed to define $p/0$ after all—to declare it equal to infinity or to a special symbol. But every such attempt runs into new incongruities: if $p/0$ is infinity, then what is $0 \cdot$ infinity, and so on. In the standard exposition this looks like a series of technical inconveniences. ToS explains why the inconveniences are ineliminable: there is nothing whose definition could be completed. $p/0$ is not an *underdefined* expression that lacks a value and whose definition could be completed by a felicitous convention. It is an expression that *sets no operation*: commensurability with zero does not “not yet have a value”—it is not a commensurability at all. One cannot complete the definition of what has not been begun; one cannot finish saying an unsaid sentence that has no predicate.

Division by zero is not kept out of the rational numbers by a special exclusion imposed from outside. It *itself* does not enter them—for the same reason for which commensurability in general requires two existing measures, not one. The condition $q \neq 0$ in the construction of the rational numbers is not an exclusion; it is a record of the fact that commensurability is given only where there is something to commensurate with.

*Division by zero in ToS is structural nonsense, not an action that a rule excludes. The commensurability $p/q$ requires finding a step $u$ for which $q \cdot u = p$. At $q = 0$ this equation ceases to set a measure: if $p \neq 0$, there is no solution at all; if $p = 0$, any step satisfies it, and therefore none is determined. The denominator is a number of parts, not a coordinate reference point. Division by zero does not have its definition completed by a felicitous convention, for there is nothing to complete: it sets no operation. The condition $q \neq 0$ is a record of the fact that commensurability requires two existing measures, not an exclusion.*

### What Has Been Examined

The special case has been examined: commensurability is given with a nonzero denominator, and this condition follows from what commensurability is, rather than being brought in by an exclusion. In E/R/R terms this is well-formedness: the denominator-role is a number of parts, and zero parts do not realize it (§ 2.8.3). Now commensurable marking can be recorded formally—we can see how the rational number appears in a rigorous construction. The next section is occupied with this.

## The Formal Construction

### The Rational Numbers in the Standard Library

Commensurable marking has been gone through in prose; let us record it formally. As with the integers, ToS does not build the rational numbers anew: there is a ready construction for them in the standard library of Rocq, and ToS takes it as a working instrument.[^1]

A rational number is given by a record with two fields:

```coq
Record Q : Set := Qmake {
  Qnum : Z;
  Qden : positive
}.
```

The two fields correspond to the two components of commensurability. The field `Qnum`—the numerator—is an integer: a position in the two-sided marking of the integers, how many former steps are taken and on which side of the reference point. The field `Qden`—the denominator—is a number of type `positive`: into how many parts the former step is divided. Here it is worth making a reservation about the word `positive`, so as not to overload it. The type `positive` in Rocq is a *technical* inductive type of the positive integers in binary notation, part of the standard library; it is *not* the philosophical “positivity of existence” of which Chapter III.1 spoke. Its meaning in the given record is simple and purely constructive: `positive` has no zero among its values, and therefore the denominator is *constructively nonzero*. Thereby the condition $q \neq 0$ (§ 2.4) turns out to be *built into the type itself*: the denominator simply cannot be zero, and the formal record does not admit division by zero by the arrangement of the type, not by an exclusion. Philosophically this agrees well with the ToS reading of the denominator as a number of existing parts: the number of parts cannot be zero (§ 2.4)—but the agreement itself should not be read as if the technical type `positive` and ontological positivity were one and the same.

### The Equality of Fractions

One and the same commensurable position is written by different fractions. The step two of which give one former step and the step four of which give two former steps are one and the same step; the fractions $1/2$ and $2/4$ give one position. Formally this is expressed by the condition of equality of fractions:

$$
\frac{p}{q} = \frac{r}{s}
\quad\text{if and only if}\quad
p \cdot s = r \cdot q.
$$

The fractions $1/2$ and $2/4$ are equal, since $1 \cdot 4 = 2 \cdot 2$. Technical precision is needed here as to how this is arranged in Rocq. The type `Q`, as the record above shows, is a `Record`: a pair of a numerator and a denominator. It is *not* a quotient type—a type in which all equivalent fractions are literally identified and merged into one object. The records `1#2` and `2#4` are *different* objects of type `Q`—different pairs—and by the structural equality of records (`=`) they are *not* equal. Rational equality is given by a *separate* equivalence relation—`Qeq`, which in Rocq is customarily written with the sign `==`; and it is by this relation, by `Qeq`, that the fractions `1#2` and `2#4` are equal. Three things must be distinguished here: the structural equality of records $p/q = r/s$ as pairs (`=` in Rocq); rational equality $p/q \mathrel{==} r/s$, that is, $p \cdot s =
r \cdot q$ (`Qeq`); and the mathematical idea of a *class* of equivalent fractions. The standard library implements the first and the second separately: `Q` is a type of pairs, and `Qeq` is a relation on it; the third—the equivalence class—is a mathematical reading to which `Qeq` gives access, not the literal arrangement of the type `Q`.

This condition—$p \cdot s = r \cdot q$—is the formal record of the fact that one commensurable position is representable by different fractions. At the level of the *ontology of marking*, the rational number is the commensurable position itself: what is common to all the notations $1/2, 2/4, 3/6,
…$—a position independent of the fraction by which it was written.
At the level of the *formal record* of Rocq, this common element is expressed by the relation `Qeq`, not by merging the records into one object: the records remain different, and `==` makes them equal. A concrete fraction—$1/2$ or $2/4$—is only one representation; the rational position is what `Qeq` declares common to them. In the E/R/R analysis (§ 2.8.3) the rational is a *class*-role (by `Qeq`), not a quotient object; this prepares the reading of the point as a class in Chapter 4.3.

### The Construction via the Field of Fractions

The record via numerator and denominator is not the only way to give the rational numbers. There is another, more general one, and it clarifies the place of the rational numbers among other numerical constructions.

The rational numbers are the *field of fractions* of the integers. The construction of the field of fractions is as follows: a ring without zero divisors is given—in our case the ring of integers—and from it a field is built whose elements are pairs “a numerator from the ring, a nonzero denominator from the ring,” taken with the same identification of equivalent pairs. For the ring of integers, the field of fractions is precisely the rational numbers.

This construction is general: it applies not only to the integers, and in the Part devoted to algebra it will be gone through in full. Here it suffices to note that the rational numbers are the *field of fractions* of a quite definite ring, not an accidental construction, and both records—via numerator and denominator and via the field of fractions—give one and the same object. Rocq uses the first as computationally convenient; the second indicates the place of the rational numbers in the general structure of algebraic constructions.

*ToS takes the rational numbers ready-made from the standard library of Rocq: the type `Q` is a record (`Record`) of the numerator `Qnum` (an integer) and the denominator `Qden` (the type `positive`—a technical type of positive integers, without zero). The nonzeroness of the denominator is built into the type: the formal record does not admit division by zero by its arrangement, not by an exclusion. The type `Q` is not a quotient type: `1#2` and `2#4` are different records, equal not structurally but by the separate rational equality `Qeq` (the sign `==`), that is, when $p \cdot s = r \cdot q$. The rational position is what `Qeq` declares common to all such records.*

### What Has Been Recorded

Commensurable marking has received a formal record. Next the chapter turns to what this marking gives that is new compared with the integers—to the property of being a *field*.

## The Rational Numbers as a Field

### What Commensurability Adds

Commensurable marking has been built and recorded. It is now worth asking what it gives *beyond* what the integers gave—what the structural gain of the transition to the rational numbers consists in.

This gain lies in *division*. On the integers addition, subtraction, and multiplication were performed, and the result was each time an integer: the integers are closed under these three operations. But division takes an integer out of the integers: in the integers $7$ cannot be divided by $3$—there is no integer position that would correspond to a third of seven steps. Commensurable marking has given precisely this missing position: $7/3$ is a point of the commensurable marking standing seven steps divided by three from the reference point. In the rational numbers, division—by a nonzero number—always has a result, and this result is a rational number.

This structural difference has an exact name. The integers are a *ring*; the rational numbers are a *field*. Let us examine what these two words mean.

### Ring and Field

A *ring* is a number system in which addition, subtraction, and multiplication are defined, and none of the three leads outside it. The integers are a ring: the sum, difference, and product of integers are always integers. But division is not among the operations of a ring: a ring does not require every element to have a *multiplicative inverse*—one such that the product of the element by it gives one. The integer $2$ has no such inverse among the integers: the number that gives one in a product with two is $1/2$, and $1/2$ is not an integer.

A *field* is a ring in which, over and above the three operations, there is a fourth as well: every element except zero has a multiplicative inverse, and therefore division by every nonzero element is performable. The rational numbers are a field: every nonzero rational number $p/q$ has a multiplicative inverse—$q/p$—and their product is one.

The transition from ring to field is precisely the structural gain of commensurable marking. The integers are a ring because they mark with a whole step, and the multiplicative inverse of an integer does not, generally speaking, fall into integer positions. The rational numbers are a field because they mark with a commensurable step, and the multiplicative inverse always finds itself a commensurable position. A field is a ring in which commensurability has made division performable everywhere—except at zero.

### The Inverse Element Exists Positively

About the multiplicative inverse element something must be said separately—exactly what the chapter on the integers said about the opposite element (Chapter III.1, § 1.7).

The multiplicative inverse of a nonzero rational number $p/q$ is mathematically written as $q/p$—numerator and denominator change places. A technical reservation about the sign is needed here. In $ℚ$ the numerator can be negative (the field `Qnum` is an integer, $ℤ$), while the denominator in the implementation of `Q` must be positive (the type `positive`). Therefore, with a negative numerator $p$, the notation $q/p$ cannot be understood literally—$p$ cannot stand in the denominator. Under inversion the sign *is carried over into the numerator*: the inverse of $(-3)/4$ is $-4/3$, not $4/(-3)$. In general form, the inverse fraction of $p/q$ with negative $p$ is written as $-q/|p|$; the denominator remains positive. This is the same coordinate role of the sign as everywhere (Chapter III.1): the sign belongs to the numerator-position, while the denominator is a number of parts and is therefore positive.

It is easy to take “inverseness” for some deficiency or for a negation—to suppose that the inverse element is something opposite to the existence of the original. This would be an error of the same kind as taking a negative number to exist “in the minus” (Chapter III.1, § 1.1). The inverse element is a *positively existing* rational number—a commensurable position just like $p/q$, deficient in nothing.

Its “inverseness” is an *operational characteristic*, not an ontological status. To call $q/p$ the inverse of $p/q$ is to say one thing about it: the product of $p/q$ by $q/p$ is one. This is a property of the *operation*—of what comes out in multiplication—not a property of existence. In the same way, the opposite element in the integers (Chapter III.1, § 1.7) was a positively existing position with the operational property “in a sum with the original it gives zero.” In both cases the word “opposite” or the word “inverse” points to the operational role of the element, not to a deficient mode of its existence. The inverse element is—and is positively; it is called inverse by virtue of what the operation gives.

*The integers are a ring: addition, subtraction, and multiplication do not lead outside them, but not every element has a multiplicative inverse. The rational numbers are a field: every nonzero element $p/q$ has the multiplicative inverse $q/p$, and division by a nonzero number is performable everywhere. The transition from ring to field is the structural gain of commensurable marking. The inverse element $q/p$ exists positively—it is a full-fledged commensurable position; its “inverseness” is an operational characteristic (the product gives one), not a deficient mode of existence—just like the opposite element of the integers.*

### The Rational Numbers Are the First Field

It is worth noting the place of the rational numbers in the general structure of number systems, to which the chapter will return in the Part on algebra.

The natural numbers were not a field—there is not even subtraction everywhere in them. The integers are a ring, but not a field. The rational numbers are the *first field* arising on the path of ToS from the act of distinction: the first number system in which all four operations of arithmetic are performable, division by a nonzero number included. This statement is worth refining by mode. The rational numbers are a field as the *standard algebraic system* $ℚ$—the one in which both coordinate sides are unbounded and in which addition, multiplication, and inverses are defined everywhere (except for division by zero). It is precisely the fullness of the four operations, taken over the whole of $ℚ$, that is the property of a field; and it belongs to $ℚ$ as the full coordinate system of commensurable displacements. In the Part devoted to algebra, the field of rational numbers will become the ground for further constructions; here it suffices to note that commensurable marking has brought the number system to the fullness of the four operations—and this fullness, taken over the whole standard $ℚ$, is what is called a field.

### What Has Been Established

The rational numbers have been recognized as a field—a number system with four performable operations. The next section turns to what commensurable marking gives *inside* the unit interval—to its divisibility.

## The Divisibility of the Unit Interval

### The Unit Interval as a System of Positions

Commensurable marking has been recognized as a field. It is now worth looking at what this marking gives *inside* one step—inside the unit interval, the stretch from the reference point to the position $+1$.

The unit interval, taken at the level of marking, is a system (§ 2.3): it is open to internal distinctions, and points can be marked inside it. These points are commensurable positions lying between the reference point and the unit: $1/2$ is the midpoint of the interval, $1/3$ and $2/3$ are the points dividing it into three, and so on. The unit interval is thus not an empty stretch but a *system of commensurable positions* enclosed between its two ends.

### The Interval Is Divided into Any Number of Parts

How many distinctions can be drawn inside the unit interval? Any number.

The interval is divided in two—the point $1/2$ is marked. It is divided in three—$1/3$ and $2/3$ are marked. It is divided into $n$ parts—for every $n \ge 2$ the interior positions $1/n, 2/n, …, (n-1)/n$ are marked. For every natural $n \ge 2$ such a division gives interior points: the commensurability $1/n$ sets a small step $n$ of which make up the unit, and this step is a legitimate commensurable position. For $n = 1$ the division is trivial: no interior points arise—the interval remains a single part, which agrees with the emptiness of the list $1/n, …, (n-1)/n$ for $n = 1$.

Moreover, division does not stop at what has once been carried out. Between two already marked points of the unit interval a new one can be marked—one more, finer distinction can be drawn. This can also be indicated by a formula: if $a/b < c/d$ are two commensurable positions, then between them lies, for example, their *mediant*

$$
\frac{a + c}{b + d},
$$

a new commensurable position obtained from the two given ones. Thus between $1/3$ and $1/2$ lies the mediant $2/5$; between $2/5$ and $1/2$, the mediant $3/7$; and so on without limit. The full theory of the density of $ℚ$—that between *any* two rational positions there lies a rational one, and indeed infinitely many—the chapter does not unfold here; that is the subject of later Parts. Here it suffices to see that commensurable marking admits repeated internal distinction: the unit interval as a system is open not merely to some single depth of distinctions but to ever finer ones—however many of them have been drawn, the next one can be drawn.

This is the work of the same distinction of levels that has run through the whole chapter. The unit interval is divisible not because it has been made so but because it is taken as a system; and every division is the performance of a new act of distinction inside this system. Dividing an interval into parts is not the breaking up of a ready thing but the drawing of distinctions where there were none before.

### The Sign of a Rational Position

It is worth returning briefly to the sign—now as applied to rational positions. For exactly what the chapter on the integers established about the sign works here (Chapter III.1, § 1.5).

The rational position $-3/4$ is a position in the commensurable marking with step $1/4$, standing three such steps from the reference point on the side that convention has marked with the minus. The minus sign attached to it carries—as with an integer—a *side*, not a deficient existence. The position $-3/4$ itself is an existing point of the commensurable marking and exists positively, on a par with any other. Commensurability brings nothing new into the picture of the sign: it has only made the marking denser, adding positions between the integers, while the two sides of the reference point and the meaning of the sign have remained the same as Chapter III.1 established them.

And the asymmetry of the two sides is the same as with the integers, and it must be understood in the same way. Commensurable marking is built over the two-sided marking of the integers and inherits its arrangement. As Chapter III.1 showed (§ 1.3, § 1.5), the minus side is *not* an ontological descent below what is present and is *not* finite: it is the second coordinate orientation, extendable in the same way as the plus side. What commensurable marking inherits from the integers is the *coordinate meaning of the sign*, not a finiteness of the minus side: the minus of a rational number does not mean negative existence—it means the opposite orientation of a displacement relative to the reference point. Splitting the step makes the marking *denser*—it adds positions between the integers on both sides of the reference point—but both sides remain equally extendable. There is an asymmetry of plus and minus, but it lies in the *status* of the sides, not in their extent: the plus direction coincides with the direction in which the series is generated, the minus direction is a purely coordinate mirror. As a coordinate system $ℚ$ is symmetric; this symmetry is the symmetry of the coordinate record, not the symmetry of positive and negative existence.

*The unit interval, taken at the level of marking, is a system of commensurable positions. It is divided into any number of equal parts: for every $n \ge 2$ the positions $1/n, …, (n-1)/n$ are marked; and between any two marked points a new one can be marked. Division is the drawing of new acts of distinction inside the interval, not the breaking up of a ready thing. The sign of a rational position carries a side, as with an integer: the minus is a coordinate orientation, not negative existence. $ℚ$ is symmetric as a coordinate system; the asymmetry of plus and minus lies in the status of the sides, not in the finiteness of one of them.*

### What Has Been Shown

Commensurable marking has been gone through to the end: it has been built, recorded, recognized as a field, and its divisibility inside the unit interval has been shown. Next the chapter sums up and outlines what unfolds beyond the rational numbers. This is the subject of the concluding section.

## Summary. The Transition to Countability

### What the Chapter Has Covered

The chapter has gone through the rational numbers—and, like the integers, as a coordinate marking that has unfolded from the act of distinction, not as a new sort of numbers introduced by agreement.

The chapter began from the fact that the integers gave a marking with a whole, indivisible step, and it took the act of distinction from a side not yet gone through—*inside* the step; from the act performed inside the step, commensurable marking unfolded—a marking with a fractional step (§ 2.1). Commensurability was defined as a relation of the multiplicities of steps, and it was shown that it makes sense only where the step is taken as a system, not as an atom (§ 2.2). The double status of one—an atom in counting, a divisible interval in marking—was resolved as a change of the level of consideration, the same as for zero in the chapter on the integers (§ 2.3). Division by zero was recognized as structural nonsense, not as an action that a rule excludes: there is nothing to commensurate with the absence of an act (§ 2.4). The formal record was given—the type $ℚ$ of the standard library, the rational number as a record with the rational equality `Qeq` (§ 2.5). The rational numbers were recognized as a field—the first number system with four performable operations (§ 2.6). And the divisibility of the unit interval was shown: it is a system of commensurable positions, divisible into any number of parts (§ 2.7).

### The Rational Number Is a Commensurable Position

The outcome of the chapter comes down to one thing. A rational number is a *position in a commensurable marking*—a point taken relative to the reference point, on one side of it or the other, so many fractional steps away, where a fractional step is a commensurable share of the whole step.

The rational numbers do not add a new sort of entities to the number series. They are the same coordinate marking as the integers, only taken with a finer, commensurable step. The integers marked with a whole step; the rationals mark with a fractional one, and so their marking is *denser*: between any two integer positions lie commensurable ones. But the arrangement is the same: the reference point, the step, positions on both sides. And the sign is the same, inherited from the integers: the minus is a coordinate orientation relative to the reference point, not negative existence. $ℚ$, like $ℤ$, is symmetric as a coordinate system; the asymmetry of plus and minus lies in the status of the sides (the plus coincides with the direction in which the series is generated, the minus is its coordinate mirror), not in the finiteness of one of them. Commensurability has made the marking denser without changing its ground.

### E/R/R Analysis: The Rational-as-Commensurable-Position as a System

The rational, reduced to a commensurable position (§ 2.8.2), is worth analyzing by E/R/R (Part I). A reservation about status, as for the integers (Chapter III.1): ToS does not build its own type of rationals—it uses the standard `Q` from `QArith` (a `Record` of numerator/denominator, § 2.5); what is machine-checked is the field arithmetic of `Q` itself and the relation `Qeq`, while the commensurable reading is carried by the prose. The file `Countability_Q.v` (the Calkin–Wilf tree) is the anchor of the *next* chapter and works with *positive* pairs `QPos` (§ 2.8.5). We conduct the analysis in the ontological order Rules $\to$ Roles $\to$ Elements.

*Rules* (the law $L5$). The constitution is *commensurability*: the division of the step into $q$ equal parts with the order retained (§ 2.2); $p/q$ is the position $p\cdot(1/q)$. To it is added the field structure: the four operations are performable, every nonzero element has the inverse $q/p$ (§ 2.6). And the rule of equality: `Qeq` ($p\cdot s=r\cdot q$) is a *separate* relation, not the structural equality of records (§ 2.5). Division by zero is excluded by the constitution as nonsense, not as something ruled out: there is nothing to commensurate with zero parts (§ 2.4).

*Roles—the significance of positions* (the law $L4$). The rational is the role of a *commensurable position*: a place on the marking with a fractional step. Inside the notation $p/q$ there are two roles: the numerator $p$—how many shares are taken; the denominator $q$—into how many parts the step is divided (a number of parts, not the reference point, § 2.4). The sign is the role of orientation, inherited from the integers (Chapter III.1). And above the records stands the role of the *class*: one position is represented by many fractions ($1/2, 2/4, …$), and what they have in common is what `Qeq` declares.

*Elements—the bearers* (the law $L1$ and the principle $P4$). The elements are fractions (a `Record` over the integers of Chapter III.1) and the commensurable positions they represent. By $L1$, a record is identical with itself *structurally*: `1#2` and `2#4` are different pairs (hence `Q` is not a quotient type). By $P4$, every rational is a *finite* object—a numerator/denominator pair—whereas “all the rationals” are a potential, uncompleted marking (§ 2.8.4).

Let us gather the analysis into a table.

| Component | What it determines | E/R/R |
|---|---|---|
| commensurability: the step in $q$ equal parts | the constitution of the marking | Rule |
| field: four operations, the inverse $q/p$ | algebraic fullness | Rule |
| `Qeq` ($p\cdot s=r\cdot q$), separate from `=` | the equality of fractions | Rule |
| commensurable position; $p$ shares, $q$ parts | the role of place | Role |
| class of equivalent fractions (by `Qeq`) | the role “the same number” | Role |
| fractions (`Record` over $ℤ$; not a quotient) | bearers (a finite pair, $P4$) | Element |
| laws $L1$–$L5$ (here $L5$) | the universal layer | Rule (univ.) |

*Well-formedness.* The labeling is unambiguous: fractions are elements, the position and the class are roles, commensurability and the field are rules. And well-formedness is visible in two places. The first: *division by zero*. The denominator is the role “number of parts”; zero parts do not realize this role—$p/0$ is not a “wrong” number but an *ill-formed* record (§ 2.4). The second: *equality*. To mix up the structural equality of records, `Qeq`, and the idea of a class is to confuse an element with a role: `1#2` and `2#4` are different element-records but one role-position, which `Qeq` declares (§ 2.5). The rational as a *class* is a role-mode of identification, not a quotient object; this directly prepares the reading of the point as a class in Chapter 4.3.

What the analysis yields. The rational is the same role-position as the integer (Chapter III.1), but on a *commensurable* marking: the step has become fractional, the marking *denser*, while the ground (reference point, step, orientation) is the same. The field structure has closed the four operations—hence $ℚ$ is the first field on the path of ToS. And the role-class (by `Qeq`) and the contrast of the finite *point* with the infinite *process* (Part IV) lead to the question of the next chapter: can the commensurable positions be *enumerated* (§ 2.8.4–2.8.5)?

### What the Chapter Did Not Unfold

About one thing the chapter spoke with restraint, and it is worth saying why.

More than once the talk turned to “all the rational numbers”—to the field of rational numbers as a whole. But what is this whole? The rational numbers are positions of a commensurable marking, and this marking—like the two-sided marking of the integers—is not completed: after every position reached there is a next one; every interval is divisible still more finely. “The field of all rational numbers” is thus not a completed collection lying ready; it is a marking that is potentially extendable and can potentially be made ever denser.

The chapter cannot say more about it here, for that would require examining in what sense one can speak of an uncompleted marking as a “list” of its positions at all—and this is a special question, the question of *countability*. It is worth, however, drawing a distinction in advance, so that the transition to the next chapter is exact. Formally the type $ℚ$ *exists* as a type of the language of Rocq, and in this sense one can quantify over the rational numbers—say “for every rational.” But ToS does not read this as an *actually completed* collection of all rational positions presented at once. Three things must be distinguished here: the type $ℚ$ as an inductive type of a formal language (it exists); the completed actuality of all the rationals as a ready collection (it does not exist, by $P4$); and the *enumerating process*—a scheme of access to the rational positions one after another. ToS gives the type $ℚ$ a process reading: $ℚ$ is a scheme of marking and a mode of access to commensurable positions, not a ready, gathered list. The chapter only indicates this without unfolding it: the rational numbers are not a completed set but a potential marking, and to speak of them as a gathered whole is to take them in a special mode of consideration, which is yet to be examined.

### The Transition to the Next Chapter

The rational numbers have been built: a coordinate marking with a commensurable step, a field, the first on the path of ToS. This marking is potential—not completed and not gathered into a ready collection.

The next chapter will take up the question that the present one has only indicated: can the positions of a commensurable marking be *enumerated*—gone through one after another so that every one is reached? This is the question of the countability of the rational numbers, and ToS will answer it by directly constructing a traversal, not by referring to a ready set.

In the formalization, the nearest anchor of the next chapter is the file `Countability_Q.v` of the ToS Rocq repository. It constructively enumerates the *positive* rational numbers via the Calkin–Wilf tree: a positive rational is represented there by a pair `QPos` (numerator and denominator, both positive), the function `enum_QPos` traverses the tree, and the theorem `Q_positive_countable` proves—fully constructively, without a single axiom—that this traversal is injective and reaches every irreducible pair. It is worth stating the exact status in advance: a *complete* bijection $ℕ \leftrightarrow ℚ$ with computable inversion has been proved not only for the positive rationals but for the whole of $ℚ$—with sign and zero. The function `enum_Q` adds the sign and the position of zero to the traversal, its inverse `index_of_Q` finds the index of a given rational, and the theorem `Q_bijection` proves—also without axioms—that they are mutually inverse (up to `Qeq`: $2/4$ and $1/2$ receive one index).

And one more distinction is worth outlining here, so that the next chapter is not understood more broadly than needed. It will be about the countability of rational *points*—and this is not the same as the countability of rational *processes*. A rational point is finite: it is a pair of a numerator and a denominator, a finite object, and therefore such pairs are enumerable. A process over the rational numbers—for example, a sequence of rational approximations $n \mapsto ℚ$—is an object of another kind: not a pair but a function, an infinite specification of how to compute the terms. The file `Countability_Q.v` notes this contrast directly: rational points are enumerable, while processes of approximation are not enumerated in this way. This distinction—point versus process—will become essential in Part IV, where numbers will appear as processes; for now it suffices to indicate it.

Chapter III.3 is devoted to the countability of the rational numbers.
