Reflection is the last of the six [[Domains|domains]] of reasoning: in it the path traveled is analyzed from the position above the slice—limits are drawn, patterns of thinking are noticed, and the work returns to the domain where a defect entered. Here: where the domain comes from and how it is arranged, why reflection is a domain by its place in the path and a recursion by its mechanism, what the Principle of Limitation requires, where the limits of reflection itself lie, when the domain has been passed, and where reflection breaks down.

## Etymology

**Reflection** ← Latin *reflexio* “a bending back, a turning back” (a Late Latin word; Russian took it through Polish *refleksja*, English through Old French *reflexion* or directly from Latin) ← *reflectere* “to bend back, to turn back” ← *re-* “back” + *flectere* “to bend”: a gaze bent back upon itself. The Russian equivalent of the sense is *oglyanut’sya*, “to look back”: to turn one’s attention to the path one has traveled. This is the name not of a separate faculty but of a **direction**: the same movement is bent, and *re-* changes only where it is turned. Language suggests the conclusion that the analysis below will also reach: reflection is reasoning itself, bent back. The word acquired the meaning of mirroring later, and this meaning carries a second point: a reflection is not the same as the object; looking at its own reasoning, the witness sees the reasoning’s *image*—and itself remains at the point from which it looks.

## Definition

**Reflection is the comprehension of the reasoning that has been passed and of its limits.**

The first five domains look at the object; reflection looks at the reasoning about the object itself. This is an operation one level higher: the question is not what the object is, but how I reasoned about it and how far this reasoning holds.

Its guiding question—*how did I reason about this?*—is the middle of the three possible questions. *On the object*: is the result itself right, what I arrived at? This is the simplest layer, a check of the conclusion. *On the process*: how did I come to it—by what steps, in what order, was a domain skipped? Here the reasoning itself becomes the object. *On oneself*: what in me led this reasoning—a desire for a particular answer, fear, habit? The hardest layer, because the witness and the observed coincide here. The layers are nested: an error in the result may be rooted in the process, and a failure of the process in the one who reasoned. Deep reflection passes through all three.

## Origin: A Function of the Mental Field

Reflection is **a rise to another level of observation**: above what is observed. Its position is the position of the [[Metaphysics#The Witness|witness’s]] consciousness: when the observer has stepped out of an experience, the point of perception can be moved “above the experience”; with respect to thinking, it is impossible to step out of the mental field completely—but one can rise **above the slice of the field**, and the slice in which the domains worked itself becomes observable. The position works in two modes.

**Passive**—constant observation with attention on one’s inner state; it does not burden the move: this is the witness’s attention, not analysis; the “trigger” serves as a signal of choice—to respond with the standard reaction or to take a pause for analysis and a conscious choice of reaction.

**Active**—analysis itself: a full pass over the experience (the mechanism is below). Reflection with respect to mental functions is the observation and analysis of these functions: it makes it possible to notice and correct **patterns of thinking**. The grounding law is [[Identity]], taken from the side of the boundary: to see *A* as less than *A* and not-*A* together (the part is less than the whole); in the domain it works as the [[#The Principle of Limitation|Principle of Limitation]]—and holds reflection itself: one can rise above the slice, not above the field, and so there is no full view of oneself. Reflection answers to the fact that reality is *inexhaustible*—that it exceeds any partial grasp: the whole is always greater than what is seen ([[Domains#Correspondence to Reality|“Domains”]]).

## The Mechanism: Reasoning with Experience as Its Goal

Ordinary reasoning takes an object as its goal; reflection takes **experience** as its goal. The difference lies in the presence of the object. An object is at hand: one can return to it for additional information—the point of entry is open. An experience is fixed in the past: there is no returning, and the whole analysis proceeds under the condition that the incoming information had a **limitation by the time of collection**—as many steps as the experience lasted, so much has been collected; a gap in the records taken is not filled but fixed as a boundary. The exception is **the experience of thinking**: reasoning about it itself generates the object of observation—one who thinks about one’s own thinking produces the thinking one observes—and access to information is open; on this rest the passive mode and the correction of patterns on the fly.

Since reflection is reasoning, its mechanism is the mechanism of reasoning: **the same domains, a new object**. To [[1 Recognition|recognize]] what is in the records of the experience—under the condition of the time of collection. To [[2 Clarification|clarify]] the constitution of the move that was passed. To [[3 Framework Selection|take a framework]]: which region of the experience and by what measure—for limits, for a pattern, for a defect. To [[4 Comparison|compare]]: the move against the rules of the domains, this move against other moves—a pattern is revealed by comparison against a single measure. To [[5 Inference|infer]]: a record of limits, a record of a pattern, a conclusion about a defect—with a mark of strength. And over this pass reflection is again possible—a recursion that runs up against the Principle of Limitation.

Hence the answer to the question whether reflection is a domain or a position—it is derived, not chosen. Reflection as a **position** is the witness’s consciousness above the slice: the condition of launch, constant. Reflection as a **process** is reasoning with experience as its goal: a new pass of the whole architecture. “Domain 6” is the regular place of this pass in the path: the points of launch (a trigger, junctions, completion) and its special work—the drawing of limits. A domain by its place in the path; by its mechanism, a recursion of the architecture itself from the position above the slice. That is why reflection reaches any domain—it itself passes through all the domains over their records; that is also why it applies to any experience—reasoning applies to any goal.

## The Domain as a System

Analysis by the generating order—from the goal ([[Theory of Systems]]):

- **Goal**—a true record of the outcome of the analysis of the experience passed: the limits of the reasoning—how far it holds—and the patterns of thinking noticed; with correction—the return of the work to the domain where the defect entered, and the correction of the pattern itself. *Analysis of experience*—the object: the path traveled in three layers, and patterns live in the third; *limits*—the range of application, assumptions, open questions; *correction*—return and correction are regular operations, not a failure.

- **Rules**—from the goal, each from its own component. From *analysis of experience*—**rising before analysis**: full analysis is conducted from a point above the slice; the object is what was actually passed, and memory reconstructs it, so its testimony is itself an object of checking. From *true*—**symmetry of checking**: what was is recorded, not how one would like to have reasoned; refuting evidence is sought with the same thoroughness as confirming evidence. From *limits*—**the limit is obligatory**: every answer that has taken place concludes with a range of application, assumptions, and open questions; without it a part is passed off as the whole. From *patterns*—**a pattern by repetition**: a pattern is recorded by its repetition across different reasonings and is corrected where it lives—in the thinker. From *correction*—**return to the domain of entry**: a defect is corrected where it entered. And the rule of measure—from the limitation of reflection itself: **two modes**—passive observation is constant, active analysis comes by trigger and at checkpoints before transitions; what degenerates into rumination is non-stop analysis.

- **Roles**—the witness above the slice (the move is led by the witness’s will: at the signal of a trigger it chooses a reaction—the standard one or a pause for analysis; its manifestation is attention, which in the passive mode is turned to the inner state) and the object—the experience passed: the records of the path in three layers—the result, the process, the one who reasoned. The outcome of an active pass is a record of limits and a record of patterns.

- **Elements**—the experience passed (here, the process of thinking itself) in three layers: the result, the process, the one who reasoned; the inner states that the passive mode watches; triggers—a mismatch, an inconvenient fact, an emotional surge; patterns of thinking—recurring moves brought up by comparing reasonings; at the output—records of limits and patterns with their correction, and, when a defect is found, the return of the work to the domain of entry.

A peculiarity of the domain: it alone works **above** the slice, not within it; its output corrects the instrument itself: limits correct the application of a conclusion, patterns correct the thinker; it does not build up the ladder of the object. That is why it closes the cycle while opening a spiral: an acknowledged limit is the beginning of the next turn.

## The Principle of Limitation

Reflection is guided by the **Principle of Limitation**: *every answer has a range of application, assumptions, and new questions*. It rests on one of the common notions of Euclid’s *Elements*: *the whole is greater than the part* (Common Notion 5 in T. L. Heath’s numbering; axiom 8 in the Russian translation). Reality is the whole; knowledge of reality is a part; a part never exhausts the whole. Hence all knowledge has a limit beyond which it does not hold—not “possibly there is one, it should be checked,” but **structurally inevitably**: the very distinction between knowledge and reality already places them in the relation of part and whole.

The principle rests on [[Identity|the Law of Identity]]—but in a form mirroring the [[1 Recognition#The Principle of Presence|Principle of Presence]]. Presence fixes the *inner* content of identity: *A* is *A*, to see *A* as *A*. Limitation fixes the *boundary* of identity: to see *A* as less than *A* and not-*A* together. Two sides of one law: one is turned inward, to what is in *A*; the other outward, to where *A* ends. This closes the cycle of the domains: recognition and reflection rest on one law—Identity, taken from two sides.

From “the part is less than the whole” follows something direct: where there is knowledge, there is a limit; where the limit is not acknowledged, there is no completeness of knowledge. Knowledge that claims to be the whole without being it is defective in its structure. Dogmatism is a structural error: a part is passed off as the whole. Skepticism is an inversion of the same error: from the impossibility of whole knowledge, the denial of partial knowledge is inferred. The Principle of Limitation passes between them: knowledge is possible as a part, and the part is genuine, though it does not exhaust the whole. Socrates’s “I know that I know nothing”[^1] is an application of this principle: Socrates’s knowledge was a part, and Socrates knew it and knew where its boundary lay; others took the part for the whole and therefore did not see what they did not know.

Here too is the link with projection, against which [[1 Recognition#Addition and Subtraction|recognition]] warns. Where the limit is not acknowledged, the gap does not stay empty: the brain completes it with expectation, analogy, a habitual scheme, and what has been completed passes itself off as knowledge of the whole. Projection is precisely an attempt to cross the limit of partial knowledge by completion. The Principle of Limitation serves as a structural defense against it: to see the limit means not to complete beyond it.

Acknowledging the limit completes reasoning, because only it gives knowledge its genuine form—a part with an explicit limit. Without this acknowledgment a conclusion is like a tool without instructions: it will be applied where it does not work. Reasoning is complete when it is known *what* has been concluded and *from what* it follows—the work of inference; *where* it works, *where* it stops, and *what* remains unknown—the work of reflection.

> [!example]- Case: The Surgical Checklist—a pause before an irreversible step
> Surgeons know what must be checked before an operation—and yet, in the flow of work, under time pressure, in routine, checks drop out. The World Health Organization built a pause into the procedure itself: the team goes through a list of nineteen items aloud three times—before anesthesia, before the incision, and before the patient leaves the operating room: is this the right patient, the right side, is there an allergy, has everyone on the team stated their name and role.
>
> In 2007–2008 the checklist was introduced in eight hospitals in eight cities—in countries of different income levels—and 3,733 patients before its introduction were compared with 3,955 after. Mortality fell from 1.5% to 0.8%, and in-hospital complications from 11.0% to 7.0%. The senior author of the study was the surgeon Atul Gawande.
>
> Structurally the case is telling. The checklist proceeds from an acknowledged limit: the team’s knowledge of what is happening at the moment is a part, and something in it may be missing. The pause is reflection at a checkpoint before an irreversible step: it asks what the thing about to be done rests on, and returns the work to where a defect could have entered—most often to recognition. This is reflection built into the system: the requirement to ask the questions holds independently of the surgeon’s memory.
>
> Source: Haynes, A. B., Weiser, T. G., Berry, W. R., et al. “A Surgical Safety Checklist to Reduce Morbidity and Mortality in a Global Population,” *New England Journal of Medicine* 360, no. 5 (2009): 491–499. DOI: 10.1056/NEJMsa0810119.

## The Limits of Reflection Itself

Reflection has its own limits, and honesty requires naming them. Memory is reconstructive: it rebuilds what has been passed to fit existing schemas, and in retelling what is foreign drops out or is remade into the familiar.[^2] Reflection does the same when it looks back on its own reasoning: it partly composes the reasoning anew, fitting it to how one *would like* to have reasoned. That is why the testimony of memory about the path traveled is itself an object of checking. There are also blind spots, invisible by definition, and the limit of self-observation: observed thinking changes under observation—what is seen is thinking-under-observation, not thinking as it is. Evaluation itself has a blind spot as well: the less competent a person is in a field, the worse they are able to judge their own incompetence—judging requires the very skill that is lacking.[^3] And an excess of reflection turns into its own pathology: rumination, where thought goes in circles without moving, and [[Paralysis of Analysis|paralysis of analysis]], where endless checking replaces action. That is why active analysis is held at checkpoints—before important transitions.

## The Spiral

The cycle of the domains ends with reflection, but the end here is a beginning: having acknowledged the limit of present knowledge, reflection makes this limit the beginning of the next turn; every acknowledged limitation opens new questions, and new questions launch a new pass through the domains. Reasoning goes not in a line but in a spiral—a return to the beginning at a new level. Reflection works not only at the end: the passive mode runs in the background through all the domains—the witness’s attention on its own state and course of thought. This background observation catches an error earlier: the trigger fires, and the choice is the witness’s—to continue the move or to take a pause and return the work to the domain where the error entered. Often passive reflection manifests [[Intuition#Two Modes|intuitively]]—“there is something here”—and this prompts one to stop and turn one’s attention to it for a detailed investigation. Active checks are held at checkpoints—before important transitions: has enough been recognized and clarified before choosing a framework? is the comparison complete before inferring? have the limits been taken into account before acting? A check at the junctions preserves both thoroughness and movement.

## Grounds for Transition

The domain completes the whole pass, not a step. Its ground follows [[Sufficient Reason|the Law of Sufficient Reason]]: the answer is closed by a polar check against the goal, the limits are drawn, the open questions are named. If it does not close—a return to the domains already passed: a regular move of the architecture ([[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|“Movement through the Domains”]]), an iteration of the path. A closed answer with an acknowledged limit itself opens the next turn of the [[#The Spiral|spiral]]: new questions—a new pass. The danger lies at the edges: to name limits only for show and consider the pass closed is premature transition—reflection becomes a ritual after which nothing has changed; to go on checking when the answer is closed and the limits drawn is stalling—endless reflection is a dead end.

## Fallacies of the Domain of Reflection

Reflection is difficult and therefore often fails to do its work. What is usually visible is the result; one’s own course of thought stays in the shadow until one turns to it, and that is a separate effort which the mind avoids, hurrying on to the next task. In addition, a conclusion is *my* conclusion, tied to my competence and self-esteem; whoever mistakes their records about themselves for themselves sees in an error of the conclusion an error in themselves and defends the record—the justification of their position—rather than absolute truth ([[Metaphysics#The Ego Zone|“The Ego Zone”]]). The more tightly a conclusion is interwoven with identity—professional, political, personal—the fiercer the defense; precisely where reflection is needed most, it meets the greatest resistance. In the [[Fallacy Catalog|catalog]] the failures of the sixth domain are assigned the group [[2.6 Domain 6 Reflection|2.6 “Domain 6: Reflection”]]—**22 fallacies**: refusal to acknowledge the limits of a conclusion. Within it are four mechanisms: [[2.6 Domain 6 Reflection#Illusion of Completion|illusion of completion]] (6), [[2.6 Domain 6 Reflection#Self-Assessment Distortion|self-assessment distortion]] (2), [[2.6 Domain 6 Reflection#Past Investment Influence|past investment influence]] (2), and [[2.6 Domain 6 Reflection#Immunization from Testing|immunization from testing]] (12, the most populated). Analyzed below are five frequent representatives: three from the first three mechanisms and two from immunization from testing.

**[[2.6 Domain 6 Reflection#Illusion of Completion|Illusion of Completeness]]**—a conclusion is taken for an absolute truth that is final and requires no revision. Reflection must acknowledge: this is the best that can be said *now*, on *these* data, within *this* framework; the illusion of completeness locks a door that should have been left ajar. “Science has proven that…”—but science builds models that work until the first anomaly: Newtonian mechanics “proved” the laws of motion until Einstein outlined their limit. Check: what data could change this conclusion? if “none,” this is an illusion, not the strength of the conclusion.

**[[Dunning Kruger Effect|Dunning–Kruger Effect]]**—a lack of competence makes it impossible to assess one’s own competence: to recognize an error, one must know what a correct solution looks like. Reflection is carried out, but with a defective instrument—the least skilled systematically overestimate themselves, the most skilled underestimate their standing among others. The beginner is sure; the grandmaster doubts. Check: how do I know that I know enough? which people more knowledgeable than I would disagree with me?

**[[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]**—past investments of time, effort, and reputation distort the assessment of the present. The costs are sunk—no future decision will bring them back—but the psyche does not want to acknowledge the loss, and a conclusion is defended because too much has been invested in it, not because it is right. “I gave twenty years to this theory—it cannot be wrong.” Check: if I were starting anew, without past investments, would I make the same choice?

**[[Confirmation Bias]] in Reflection**—checking is asymmetric: the confirming is accepted easily, while the refuting requires “special circumstances” and “additional checking.” In the catalog it is a syndrome affecting reasoning as a whole; in the sixth domain its mechanism is [[2.6 Domain 6 Reflection#Immunization from Testing|immunization from testing]]: the framework has already been chosen, the comparison made, the conclusion drawn, and what is distorted is the very checking of what has been done. It looks like reflection—a person “checks” the conclusion—but does not seek refutations with the same thoroughness as confirmations. Check: did I actively look for what could refute my conclusion?

**[[2.6 Domain 6 Reflection#Immunization from Testing|Refusal to Revise]]**—the extreme form: even seeing the grounds, a person refuses to accept them. Counter-evidence is ignored, its source is attacked, the subject is changed, the conversation is broken off by emotion instead of argument. “I don’t care what the data show—I know I’m right”; “these studies were paid for, they can’t be trusted.” Check: what could make me change my mind? if “nothing,” this is a refusal of reflection in the guise of firmness.

> [!example]- Case: Lord Kelvin—a great mind that did not revise its conclusion
> William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, was one of the greatest physicists of the nineteenth century—the creator of the absolute temperature scale, one of the authors of the second law of thermodynamics. In 1862 Kelvin undertook to calculate the age of the Earth: the planet is cooling from a molten state, and from the rate of cooling one can learn how much time has passed. The method was elegant and rested on heat conduction—a field Kelvin knew better than anyone. The calculation gave about 98 million years, within limits of 20 to 400 million, and by 1897 Kelvin had narrowed the estimate to 20–40 million. This was several times less than geologists and Darwinian evolution required: in 1859 Darwin estimated the denudation of the Weald alone at roughly 300 million years. Kelvin was unbending: “British popular geology at the present time,” Kelvin declared in 1868, “is in direct opposition to the principles of natural philosophy.”
>
> Three of the fallacies of reflection analyzed above converged in Kelvin. *Sunk costs*: decades of work, lectures, and polemics had tied Kelvin’s reputation to this conclusion, and to admit the error meant admitting those years wasted. *The Dunning–Kruger effect*: radioactivity was new physics, and an expert in the old paradigm may be incompetent in the new one. *Refusal to revise*: in 1903 Pierre Curie and Albert Laborde discovered that radium salts continuously *release* heat, and in the same year John Joly suggested that radium could sustain the heat of the Earth’s interior; in 1906 Robert Strutt found more radium in igneous rocks than was needed to account for the heat flowing from the interior. Kelvin’s calculation, by contrast, assumed that the Earth only cools, without internal sources, and moreover that it is solid and loses heat by conduction alone. As early as 1895 John Perry pointed to the transport of heat by the movement of matter in the interior; according to calculations of 2007, it was this assumption, not the unaccounted-for radioactivity, that decisively lowered the age. In 1904 Ernest Rutherford, in a lecture at the Royal Institution in Kelvin’s presence, connected the discovery of radium with Kelvin’s estimate. In the same year Kelvin acknowledged that the energy of radium is intra-atomic, but in 1906, in letters to *The Times*, denied that the heat of the Sun or the Earth is explained by radium, and died in 1907 without revising the estimate of the Earth’s age.
>
> The modern estimate is about 4.5 billion years: 110–230 times Kelvin’s late estimate and roughly 46 times that of 1862. Kelvin’s calculations were right—*given Kelvin’s own assumptions*; but the assumptions were incomplete, and reflection should have asked: what am I assuming to be known that I do not actually know? Kelvin did not ask this question—or asked it and rejected the answers. A correct calculation on an incomplete model gave a wrong result—and the refusal to revise preserved the error to the end of Kelvin’s life. Competence in one field does not guarantee being right; the elegance of a method does not protect against error; authority does not replace reflection.
>
> Sources: Thomson, W. “On the Secular Cooling of the Earth,” *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh* 23, pt. 1 (1864): 157–169; Darwin, C. *On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection* (London: John Murray, 1859), 287; Kelvin. “On Geological Time,” in Kelvin, *Popular Lectures and Addresses*, vol. 2 (London: Macmillan, 1894), 10–64; Kelvin. “The Age of the Earth as an Abode Fitted for Life,” *Journal of the Transactions of the Victoria Institute* 31 (1899): 11–35; Curie, P., Laborde, A. “Sur la chaleur dégagée spontanément par les sels de radium,” *Comptes rendus hebdomadaires des séances de l’Académie des sciences* 136 (1903): 673–675; Joly, J. “Radium and the Geological Age of the Earth,” *Nature* 68 (1903): 526; Strutt, R. J. “On the Distribution of Radium in the Earth’s Crust, and on the Earth’s Internal Heat,” *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London*, Series A, 77 (1906): 472–485; Eve, A. S. *Rutherford* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1939), 107; Thompson, S. P. *The Life of William Thomson, Baron Kelvin of Largs*, vol. 2 (London: Macmillan, 1910), 999, 1184, 1190; Patterson, C. “Age of Meteorites and the Earth,” *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* 10, no. 4 (1956): 230–237; England, P., Molnar, P., Richter, F. “John Perry’s Neglected Critique of Kelvin’s Age for the Earth: A Missed Opportunity in Geodynamics,” *GSA Today* 17, no. 1 (2007): 4–9.

> [!example]- Case: Semmelweis—refusal to revise
> In 1841–1846, in the First Obstetric Clinic in Vienna, where physicians were trained, an average of 9.9% of women in childbed died of puerperal fever; in the Second, where midwives were trained, 3.4%. Ignaz Semmelweis rejected the accepted explanations and in 1847 came to the conclusion: physicians and students carry cadaveric particles to women in labor on their hands, particles that soap does not wash off. From mid-May, hands were washed with a chlorine solution, then with chlorinated lime; in 1848, 1.27% died in the First Clinic and 1.33% in the Second.
>
> The doctrine required obstetricians to see the source of the deaths in their own hands. Semmelweis personally acknowledged this: “only God knows the number of those who went to an early grave because of me” (our translation). In 1848 Professor Josef Škoda proposed that the medical faculty appoint a commission to test the doctrine; the proposal was adopted by a majority of votes, but after a protest by the professor of obstetrics, the commission was not allowed, “by higher order” (our translation), to begin its work. In 1849 Semmelweis’s post of assistant was not renewed, and in 1850 Semmelweis left for Pest. Semmelweis’s successor, Carl Braun, kept the chlorine washings but rejected the doctrine; in 1854, 9.1% of women in childbed died in the First Clinic, and in 1864 Braun declared ventilation the chief measure of prevention.
>
> Sources: Semmelweis, I. Ph. *Die Aetiologie, der Begriff und die Prophylaxis des Kindbettfiebers* (Pest; Wien; Leipzig: Hartleben, 1861), 3, 46, 54–58, 65, 80–81, 140, 489–491; Sinclair, W. J. *Semmelweis: His Life and His Doctrine* (Manchester: University Press, 1909), 274–275.

## Questions

The guiding question—**“how did I reason about this?”**—along the axes of the [[Question#The Question and the Domains|question]]: reflection is a change of object: the path traveled itself becomes the object; the goals here are predominantly polar (“is it right?”, “has it been passed?”—the witness ascertains) and completing ones of the category of limit (“how far? under what conditions?”); this is the stage of **ascertainment**: here the invariant of the path is fulfilled—every answer that has taken place is closed by a polar check against the goal. The questions that serve the whole of reasoning—“is the base right?”, “have I strayed from the root?”—are asked by reflection.

The working questions of the domain—by the layers and rules of the domain:

- **Course**—“how did I reason about this?”—the guiding question itself;
- **Relevance**—“did I keep to the root—did every step work toward the goal?”—a step that did not advance the answer to the root question was a distraction;
- **Ladder**—“did I pass the tiers without skipping?”—depth without gaps;
- **Base**—“is the base of the root question right—a framework and something to turn to?”—the ground of the question ([[Question#The Structure of a Question|“The Structure of a Question”]]);
- **Limits**—“where does the conclusion work and where does it stop, on what assumptions does it rest—and what questions has it opened?”—the Principle of Limitation; the new questions lie in the second zone—“I know that I do not know” ([[Epistemology#Field, Slice, Boundary|“Field, Slice, Boundary”]]);
- **State**—“what in me is leading this move—and is it time for a pause?”—the passive mode: the trigger is a signal of choice.

The transition—by the [[#Grounds for Transition|grounds for transition]]: the answer is closed by a check against the goal, the limits are drawn, the open questions are named—an acknowledged limit opens a new turn; if it does not close—a return to the domains already passed.

## Summary

1. **Reflection is the comprehension of the reasoning that has been passed and of its limits;** the guiding question—“how did I reason about this?”—is the middle of three layers: the result, the process, the one who reasoned.
2. **Reflection is a rise to another level of observation:** the position of the witness’s consciousness above the slice; one cannot step out of the mental field, but one can rise above the slice—and so there is no full view of oneself.
3. **Two modes:** passive—constant observation, attention on the inner state; it often manifests intuitively—“there is something here”; the trigger is a signal of choice: the standard reaction or a pause; active—analysis by trigger and at checkpoints before transitions.
4. **The mechanism is the same domains, a new object:** reasoning with experience as its goal; a domain by its place in the path, a recursion by its mechanism.
5. **The Principle of Limitation:** every answer has a range of application, assumptions, and new questions; its support is Euclid’s axiom “the whole is greater than the part.” The grounding law is Identity, taken from the side of the boundary.
6. **The fallacies of the domain—group 2.6 of the catalog, 22 records:** illusion of completion, self-assessment distortion, past investment influence, immunization from testing; one thing unites them—limits are not outlined, revision does not take place.
7. **The domain completes the whole pass:** the answer is closed by a check against the goal, the limits are drawn, the open questions are named; an acknowledged limit opens the next turn of the spiral.
