Framework selection is the third of the six [[Domains|domains]] of reasoning: in it the boundaries of the field are taken—the region and the measure within which comparison and inference will proceed. Here: where the domain comes from and how its work goes, why frameworks form a hierarchy, what the Principle of Objectivity requires, and why this choice so often passes unnoticed.

## Etymology

**Framework**—in Russian *ramka* ← *rama* “frame” (through Polish *rama* or directly from German *Rahmen* “frame, framing”): what is taken into the frame is what is seen—the frame cuts off the rest. **Selection**—in Russian *vybor* ← *vy-brat’* ← **brat’** “to take”: to take one out of many. Framework selection is **the taking of a boundary of view**: the decision as to what will enter the field of consideration and along which axis it will be measured. For contrast: the English noun *frame* names both a border and a skeleton, and the verb *frame* means “to build, to put together” (a Middle English sense; Old English *framian* meant “to be of use, to prosper”): the frame as what *builds* the view; hence *frame of reference*, the same role in the exact sciences. Both lines converge on the main thing: the one who looks sets the frame.

## Definition

**Framework selection is the determination of the system of coordinates in which comparison is possible.**

[[2 Clarification|Clarification]] has established *what* the object is. But one and the same object looks different depending on what one looks at it through. The way of looking determines what will be seen—and hence what conclusions will be reached. There is no “just looking”: one always looks *through* something; the only question is whether this choice is conscious.

- *Selection* is an active act, not a passive acceptance: one and the same data are compatible with many frameworks.
- *A system of coordinates* is that relative to which comparison becomes possible: London and Tokyo are incomparable until a measure is chosen—population? area? climate?—and each gives its own relations between the same cities.
- *For the sake of comparison*—a framework exists for the next domain: it creates the conditions under which [[4 Comparison|comparison]] will take place.

Recognition fixes *what is there*; clarification, *what it is*; framework selection determines *in what terms* it will be compared with something else.

## Origin: A Function of the Mental Field

Recognition has fixed the units, clarification has disclosed their essence; to compare, one must take a **region** of the field. Framework selection is the choice of a specific region of the field where comparison will take place: the domain answers the question “**what boundaries of the field do we take?**”—and from then on the work of the fourth and fifth domains proceeds within these boundaries. The boundaries are taken by the operator, as are the boundaries of any system ([[Theory of Systems#The Interaction of Systems|“Theory of Systems”]]); the region is not the whole—hence every framework has a price: what has remained beyond the boundary. Selection is a function of **the will** in the mental field: the decision of the will enters reasoning exactly once—here, as the framework ([[Question#The Question and the Domains|“Question”]]). And the pool of frameworks is not given in advance: **determining the zone of the pool is the first step of the domain’s work**. The course has three steps, out of the whole potential field: to determine what the criteria of selection should be, then to determine those that fit the criterion, and only then to take the chosen one into work; the taking is closed by the will, whose manifestation is attention directed at the region ([[Metaphysics#The Operator and the Will|“The Operator and the Will”]]). The boundaries taken are experienced as a given—which is why framework selection so often passes unnoticed. The grounding law of the domain lies in the [[#The Principle of Objectivity|Principle of Objectivity]]: Non-Contradiction—one cannot at once seek absolute truth and fit the answer—together with the Sufficient Reason of the choice. A framework is not an arbitrary choice but the choice of an aspect that the thing itself admits: reality is many-sided ([[Domains#Correspondence to Reality|“Domains”]]).

## The Domain as a System

Analysis by the generating order—from the goal ([[Theory of Systems]]):

- **Goal**—to take the boundaries of the field: to choose a region and a measuring axis within it, so that comparison becomes possible; within these boundaries the work of the domains of comparison and inference will proceed.
- **Rules**—from the goal: the course in three steps (the criteria of selection, then those that fit the criterion, then the taking of the chosen one); the [[#The Principle of Objectivity|Principle of Objectivity]] (the framework is chosen by the nature of the phenomenon and the goal, not to suit the result); the ground of the choice—Sufficient Reason (“why *this* one?”); the hierarchy of frameworks—begin from the highest level ([[Order]]).
- **Roles**—the operator of the choice—the witness who takes the boundaries (the taking is closed by its will)—and the object: the clarified units that are to be compared.
- **Elements**—possible frameworks: criteria, scales, systems of coordinates; the clarified units that are to be compared; at the output—the region taken, with its measuring axis.

## The Hierarchy of Frameworks

Frameworks are not of equal standing—they form a hierarchy, and the [[Order|Law of Order]] bids one begin from the highest—the most general—level. A framework can work at the level of data (whether something *is*), of information (*what exactly* this is), of qualities (which categories of difference apply: color, shape, size), of characteristics (the concrete values within them: red, square, large)—the same ladder along which [[1 Recognition|recognition]] proceeds. The rule: before comparing characteristics, check comparability at the level of information; before comparing information, check that there are data. To grab the first framework that catches the eye—usually the most conspicuous one—is to choose by perceptual salience, not by level.

## The Principle of Objectivity

Framework selection is guided by the **Principle of Objectivity**: *the framework is chosen by the nature of the object and the goal; an orientation toward truth excludes an orientation toward confirmation*.

Objectivity is the witness’s orientation toward what does not depend on its position ([[Objectivity and Subjectivity#The Witness Is Subjective: Objectivity Is an Orientation|“Objectivity and Subjectivity”]]); it is often misunderstood—as “the view from nowhere” (the title of a book by Thomas Nagel, 1986), a standpoint outside any position. There is no such thing ([[What Is Not and Cannot Be|the registry of the impossible]]): one always looks from somewhere, through some framework. The principle requires something else: the framework must correspond to the nature of the phenomenon and to the goal of the inquiry—and at the same time one cannot seek absolute truth while fitting the result to what is desired: absolute truth above all. Two laws converge here: [[Non-Contradiction]] (one cannot within a single orientation seek *A* and fit not-*A*) and [[Sufficient Reason]] (the choice of a framework requires a ground—why *this* one? does it suit the phenomenon? does it serve the goal of understanding or the goal of confirmation?).

Behind this stands a logical necessity, not a psychological recommendation. A dishonest choice of framework is built like this: a person wants to arrive at a certain conclusion and chooses a framework that guarantees that conclusion. But then that person asserts at once that they are seeking absolute truth (otherwise why reason?) and that they already know the answer (otherwise why fit the framework to it?). “I am seeking absolute truth” and “I already know the answer” exclude each other; one cannot seek what has been found. This is a violation of the Law of Non-Contradiction in thinking—an attempt to assert *A* and not-*A* at once. Hence the Principle of Objectivity is a requirement of logical consistency: whoever truly seeks absolute truth cannot choose a framework to suit the desired result, or else the “search” is a fiction.

Hence the practical criterion: *am I ready to accept any result?* If the framework is chosen so that one result is impossible in principle, this is rationalization, not inquiry. In terms of order such a choice is [[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|reverse movement]]: the conclusion goes ahead of the reasoning and itself chooses the framework.

## The Invisible Domain

Framework selection, like recognition, regularly takes place without the reasoner’s awareness—for different reasons: perception seems immediate, and the projection in it is not visible (the case “The Dress”—[[1 Recognition|“Recognition”]]); a framework seems a natural way of seeing, and the choice is not noticed as a choice. Clarification is experienced as effort, comparison as work, inference as a result, while framework selection slips by unnoticed, disguised as a given: one’s own choice is experienced as a property *of the object itself*.[^1] A framework is acquired early—absorbed with language and culture before the capacity for reflection matures—and holds the more firmly the more pleasant it is: a framework in which I am right is more comfortable than one in which I was wrong.

A framework is always there—the only question is whether it is conscious. Becoming aware of it is the first step: a framework cannot be removed—without a framework there is no comparison—but it can be seen, and having seen it, one can ask: is this the framework I need? what changes if I look through another? A practical technique: for an important decision, present the matter in two frameworks at once; if the preference is stable, it is genuine; if it changes, further discussion is needed.

> [!example]- Case: The Framing Effect—“Will Be Saved” and “Will Die”
> In a 1981 paper Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described an experiment: two groups were given one problem—an unusual disease is expected to kill 600 people. The first group was told: Program A will save 200 people; Program B will save everyone with probability 1/3 and no one with probability 2/3. Program A was chosen by 72%. The second group: under Program C, 400 people will die; under Program D, with probability 1/3 nobody will die, and with probability 2/3 everyone will die. Program D was chosen by 78%. The outcomes in the pairs are the same: to save 200 of 600 is to lose 400. The word changed: “will be saved” sets the reference point among gains, “will die” among losses. The participants thought they were choosing between programs, but they were responding to a framework they did not see.
>
> A year later Barbara McNeil and colleagues obtained the same effect in the choice between surgery and radiation therapy for lung cancer—among clinic patients and physicians alike (the participants were asked to imagine having lung cancer): when the outcomes were given as numbers of survivors, surgery was chosen on average by 75%; when the same data were given as numbers of deaths, by 58%. Hence the technique named above—to look at the matter in both frameworks.
>
> Sources: Tversky, A., and Kahneman, D. “The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice,” *Science* 211, no. 4481 (1981): 453–458; McNeil, B. J., Pauker, S. G., Sox, H. C., Jr., and Tversky, A. “On the Elicitation of Preferences for Alternative Therapies,” *New England Journal of Medicine* 306, no. 21 (1982): 1259–1262.

> [!example]- Case: Champollion—a change of framework solves the problem
> Egyptian hieroglyphs resisted decipherment for almost a millennium and a half. The framework seemed obvious and was shared by all: a hieroglyph is a symbol, a picture stands for an idea; sound signs were admitted only as a late graft of Greek influence—for foreign names in royal cartouches. Within this framework the Rosetta Stone, found in 1799, helped little: the Greek text could be read, while in the Egyptian ones only individual names could be made out—thus Thomas Young in 1819 read the name of Ptolemy.
>
> The decisive step came in 1822 from Jean-François Champollion, who as late as 1821 had also regarded the Egyptian scripts as signs of ideas. The supposition now was different: what if the sound signs were not a late graft but a part of the script long before the Greeks? Champollion knew Coptic—the last form of Egyptian, preserved in the liturgy of the Coptic church—and, applying sound reading to the cartouches of the pharaohs, read the names of Ramesses and Thutmose, explicable through Coptic: the sign of the sun—“re,” “sun”; the signs “ms”—“to give birth.” On 14 September 1822, having worked through copies of inscriptions from Abu Simbel, Champollion is said to have run into the study of Champollion’s brother crying “Je tiens l’affaire!”—“I’ve got it!”
>
> Champollion did not “guess right” but rose a level higher—from the question “what does this symbol mean?” to the question “how is this system of signs built?” Almost fifteen hundred years of failure rested on a framework no one thought of choosing: it seemed a property of the object itself.
>
> Sources: Champollion, J.-F. *Lettre à M. Dacier… relative à l’alphabet des hiéroglyphes phonétiques employés par les Égyptiens pour inscrire sur leurs monuments les titres, les noms et les surnoms des souverains grecs et romains* (Paris: Firmin Didot père et fils, 1822); Hartleben, H. *Champollion: sein Leben und sein Werk*, vol. 1 (Berlin: Weidmannsche Buchhandlung, 1906), 418–422.

## Grounds for Transition

The transition to [[4 Comparison|comparison]] takes place by [[Sufficient Reason|the Law of Sufficient Reason]]: the framework has been taken with a ground—it has been said why *this* one—and it has been said what it leaves beyond the boundary. The choice is complete when it is conscious and the framework can be named; when the framework has passed the double check of the Principle of Objectivity—by the nature of the phenomenon and by the goal; and when the choice is open to revision. The practical criterion is whether one can begin to compare with this framework. The danger lies at the edges ([[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|forms of order failure]]): to pass to comparison without naming the ground of the choice is premature transition; to go through frameworks endlessly, seeking “the best” when a fitting one is already there, is stalling. If in comparison the measure cannot be applied or the levels are incomparable, the work returns here—to revise the region and the measure ([[4 Comparison#Grounds for Transition|“Comparison”]]).

## Fallacies of the Domain of Framework Selection

When the choice is not conscious and not checked, the framework sits askew on the object—and reasoning goes on as if the coordinates had been set correctly. In the [[Fallacy Catalog|catalog]] the failures of the third domain are assigned the group [[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection|2.3 “Domain 3: Framework Selection”]]—**16 fallacies**: a mismatch between the framework of evaluation and the object of inquiry. Within it are three mechanisms: [[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection#Category Mismatch|category mismatch]] (3), [[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection#Irrelevant Criterion|irrelevant criterion]] (9), and [[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection#Framework for Result|framework for the result]] (4). Analyzed below as well are forms that do not yet have records in group 2.3: the superficial transfer of a framework—false analogy (its record in the catalog currently stands in the comparison group)—and the confusion of fact and norm.

**[[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection#Category Mismatch|Category Mistake]]**—a framework of the wrong category: an optic designed for phenomena of another kind is applied to the phenomenon. “What color is the number seven?”, “How much does justice weigh?”—grammatically correct, categorially empty, for a number is not of the kind of colored things, and justice is not of the kind of things with weight.[^2] Check: does the object belong to the kind for which the framework was made?

**[[False Analogy]]**—the transfer of a framework on the strength of a superficial similarity, bypassing an essential difference. “The atom is like the solar system: electrons revolve around the nucleus as the planets revolve around the sun.” This analogy was useful at a certain stage of physics—and misleading at the next: electrons do not have trajectories in the classical sense. So an analogy becomes false when the difference concerns precisely what one wants to understand. Check: is the similarity relevant to this question—and do the differences touch precisely what is being investigated?

**The naturalistic fallacy** (a term of G. E. Moore’s, here in a broad sense)—the inference of a norm from a fact: “The frontal lobes are an evolutionarily new structure. Animals without developed frontal lobes live normally. Therefore a human being can do without fully developed frontal lobes”—this is how the naturalistic fallacy appears in the analysis of lobotomy (case below). From “is” there does not follow “ought”: a description of how the world is built does not by itself prescribe what it ought to be.[^3] Its mirror image is **the moralistic fallacy** (a term of B. D. Davis’s, 1978)—the inference of a fact from a norm: “People cannot be selfish by nature—that would be terrible. Therefore selfishness is the result of bad upbringing.” The desired is declared actual. Check: can the fact be acknowledged without accepting the norm—and am I not passing off the desired as what is?

**[[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection#Irrelevant Criterion|An Irrelevant Feature as a Criterion]]** (appeal to tradition / novelty / nature / popularity)—an external mark is put in place of an evaluation of content: [[Appeal to Tradition|age]], modernity, [[Appeal to Nature|naturalness]], [[Bandwagon Fallacy|the number of adherents]]. “We have always done it this way”; “this is the newest approach”; “the product is natural, so it is safe”; “millions believe it.” Each feature *can* correlate with quality but does not determine it—slavery lasted for millennia, arsenic is natural, millions are mistaken. Check: what bearing does this feature have on the quality I am interested in?

**[[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection#Framework for Result|A Framework for the Result]]**—the framework is chosen not by its correspondence to the object but by its capacity to confirm a belief already held. In the catalog [[Confirmation Bias|confirmation bias]] is a syndrome that affects reasoning as a whole; in the third domain its mechanism is the framework for the result. Someone convinced that a colleague dislikes them reads every action of the colleague through the framework of hostility: no greeting means being ignored, a remark means being undermined, praise means sarcasm. This is a direct violation of the Principle of Objectivity and a logical contradiction at once: “I am seeking absolute truth” and “I already know the answer” exclude each other. Check: would I have chosen this framework if I wanted the opposite result?

> [!example]- Case: Lobotomy—one framework, tens of thousands of lives
> In 1935 the Portuguese neurologist Egas Moniz heard of experiments on chimpanzees: after the removal of the frontal lobes, animals that had previously become irritated and angry at their mistakes grew indifferent to them. Moniz extrapolated—if the connections of the frontal lobes are destroyed in the mentally ill, they too will calm down—and in November 1935, under Moniz’s direction, the neurosurgeon Pedro Almeida Lima performed the first operation. Walter Freeman simplified the procedure to “transorbital lobotomy”: with an instrument like an ice pick inserted through the eye socket, the connections of the frontal lobes were destroyed in ten minutes. Freeman operated on an outpatient basis and toured psychiatric hospitals: in West Virginia in 1952 the tally was 228 operations in two weeks, 25 of them on a single day. By August 1949 more than ten thousand operations had been performed in the United States alone, and as many again in England and Wales over 1942–1954; in 1949 Moniz received the Nobel Prize.
>
> Mortality, by various counts, ranged from one to five percent, and among Freeman’s own patients it was about fourteen; many survivors lost initiative, emotional depth, the capacity for complex thought. Rosemary Kennedy, the sister of a future president, after a lobotomy at twenty-three remained disabled for more than sixty years.
>
> At the root of the tragedy lies one framework. The psyche was regarded as a mechanism, illness as a breakdown, treatment as the removal of a faulty part; within this framework lobotomy was logical. Almost all the fallacies of the domain are present here: the *category mistake* (the psyche is not a machine), *false analogy* (the apathy of chimpanzees equated with the healing of a human being), the *naturalistic fallacy* (the frontal lobes are a late acquisition of evolution, so a human being can do without them), the *appeal to novelty* (surgery as a breakthrough against “outdated” therapy), the *framework for the result* (“success” meant that the patient had become manageable, not that the patient had recovered; long-term outcomes were not rigorously studied); alongside them, the appeal to authority: after the Nobel Prize, criticism became difficult. The alternative framework—the psyche as a system of meanings, illness as a disturbance of meaning—required a different patience and promised no quick solution. The framework “mechanism” was more attractive than the framework “meaning”—and tens of thousands of operations became the price of an unchosen choice.
>
> Sources: El-Hai, J. *The Lobotomist: A Maverick Medical Genius and His Tragic Quest to Rid the World of Mental Illness* (Hoboken, NJ: J. Wiley, 2005); Larson, K. C. *Rosemary: The Hidden Kennedy Daughter* (Boston: Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2015); Jansson, B. “Controversial Psychosurgery Resulted in a Nobel Prize,” NobelPrize.org, October 29, 1998; Torkildsen, Ø. “Lessons to Be Learnt from the History of Lobotomy,” *Tidsskrift for Den norske legeforening* 142, no. 18 (2022), doi:10.4045/tidsskr.22.0505.

## Questions

The guiding question—**“through which framework do I look?”**—along the axes of the [[Question#The Question and the Domains|question]]: an alternative goal—**a question of decision**, “which one from the pool?”; the decision of the will enters reasoning exactly once—here, as the framework. The pool of frameworks is not given in advance: determining its zone is the first step of the work; on the path of cognition this is the stage of **choice**; the taking is closed by the will, and within these boundaries comparison and inference will proceed.

The working questions of the domain—along the three steps of the work and the guard of the choice:

- **Framework**—“through which framework do I look?”—the guiding question itself;
- **Criteria of selection**—“what should the criteria for selecting a framework be?”—from the goal and the nature of the phenomenon;
- **Zone of the pool**—“which frameworks fit the criterion?”—the selection of those that fit, from the potential field;
- **Taking**—“which one do I take—and why *this* one?”—the choice into work, with a ground;
- **The price of the boundary**—“what will the chosen framework hide?”—what has remained beyond the boundary;
- **The guard of the choice**—“is the choice conscious—or was the framework accepted by default? would I choose it if I wanted the opposite?”—the Principle of Objectivity.

The transition—by the [[#Grounds for Transition|grounds for transition]]: the framework has been taken with a ground—next comes [[4 Comparison|comparison]].

## Summary

1. **Framework selection is the determination of the system of coordinates in which comparison is possible;** the guiding question is “through which framework do I look?”.
2. **The domain takes the boundaries of the field**—the region where comparison will take place and the measuring axis within it; comparison and inference work within these boundaries; every framework has a price—what has remained beyond the boundary.
3. **The pool of frameworks is not given:** the course has three steps—determine the criteria of selection, then those that fit the criterion, then take the chosen one into work; the decision of the will enters reasoning exactly once—here.
4. **Frameworks form a hierarchy**—data, information, qualities, characteristics; begin from the highest, most general level.
5. **The Principle of Objectivity:** the framework is chosen by the nature of the object and the goal; an orientation toward truth excludes an orientation toward confirmation. The grounding law is Non-Contradiction; the ground of the choice is Sufficient Reason.
6. **Framework selection, like recognition, easily passes unnoticed:** one’s own choice is experienced as a property of the object; a framework cannot be removed, but it can be seen and checked against another.
7. **The fallacies of the domain**—the group 2.3 of the catalog, 16 records: category mismatch, irrelevant criterion, framework for the result; alongside them, false analogy and the confusion of fact and norm.
8. **One passes to comparison when the framework has been taken with a ground and what it leaves beyond the boundary has been named.**
