Inference is the fifth of the six [[Domains|domains]] of reasoning: in it a consequence is extracted from established grounds and its strength is marked—necessary, probabilistic, evaluative. Here: where the domain comes from and how its work goes, what the Principle of Rationalism requires, how the degrees of certainty and the types of inference differ, when the honest outcome is suspension of judgment, when the domain has been passed, and where inference breaks down.

## Etymology

**Inference**—in Russian *vyvod* ← *vy-* “out” + **vesti** “to lead” (Indo-European root *wedh-*, “to lead”): to lead out—to conduct from within outward, from grounds to consequence. An inference is not taken but *led*—along a path each step of which can be shown. For contrast: Latin *conclusio* ← *concludere* “to close up”—the Russian *zaklyuchenie*, “conclusion,” is built as a calque of it: reasoning *closes*; Latin *inferre* “to carry in” (whence English *inference*) adds a third image—the consequence is *carried into* knowledge. Path, lock, and deposit are three sides of one undertaking: the consequence is led out, the chain is closed, knowledge is replenished.

## Definition

**Inference is the extraction of a consequence from established grounds.**

[[1 Recognition|Recognition]] has given presence, [[2 Clarification|clarification]] essence, the [[3 Framework Selection|framework]] a perspective, [[4 Comparison|comparison]] relations. Inference takes the last step: what *follows* from all this? Its material is not the raw stuff of the world but the already processed result of the four domains; that is why inference rests on them as the fifth.

## Origin: A Function of the Mental Field

[[1 Recognition|Recognition]] fixes a difference. [[2 Clarification|Clarification]] fixes identity. The [[3 Framework Selection|framework]] has taken the boundaries of the field and the measure. [[4 Comparison|Comparison]] has filled the comparison list with records of relations. Inference is movement along grounds: from the records already lying in the witness’s subjective field, what *follows* from them is extracted, and the consequence settles as a new record—one that was not yet in the slice. Here the slice is neither replenished from outside (the point of entry is recognition) nor reordered—it is **built up**: the conclusion rises as a new rung, a ground from which the next step will proceed. The grounding law is [[Sufficient Reason]]: the consequence rests on grounds and only on them; in the domain it works as the [[#The Principle of Rationalism|Principle of Rationalism]]—a conclusion must be earned. Inference traces the relation of following that is present in reality itself: reality is *necessary*—one thing in it flows from another ([[Domains#Correspondence to Reality|“Domains”]]).

## The Domain as a System

Analysis by the generating order—from the goal ([[Theory of Systems]]):

- **Goal**—a true record of a consequence: of what follows from established grounds, and only of that—marked with the degree of strength of the following. *Of a consequence*—a new record extracted from those already lying there; *from established grounds*—the material of inference is the outcome of the four preceding domains; *and only of that*—beyond the grounds it is not inference but speculation; *marked with a degree*—the strength of following differs, and the mark is part of the record itself.

- **Rules**—from the goal, each from its own component. From *the consequence*—**the direction of movement**: the consequence is extracted from the grounds, rather than grounds being selected to fit a ready conclusion; the transition has one correct direction ([[Order]]; [[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|reverse movement]] is fabrication, rhetoric instead of logic). From *established grounds*—**only what is established**: support is drawn only from records that have passed the preceding domains; what is missing is not completed on the spot—there is a return for it to the domain needed (a cycle). From *and only of that*—**not beyond the ground**: the conclusion claims no more than the ground has given—neither in breadth nor in strength; where grounds are insufficient—suspension of judgment. From *marked with a degree*—**marking of strength**: each inference-record carries its own mark; one degree is not to be passed off as another—the ground must be sufficient for precisely this degree ([[Sufficient Reason]]). The first three rules give earnedness, the fourth gives marking; together they are the Principle of Rationalism unfolded into four moves.

- **Roles**—the witness who draws the consequence (the move is led by the witness’s will, whose manifestation is attention: it holds the direction, checks sufficiency, does not let desire lead), and the object—the grounds as supports: records of the preceding domains in the role of supports (a support is a role, not the record itself: the same record in another move is a side of a comparison or material for clarification). The outcome is held by two checks: are there enough grounds for an inference—and is there enough strength to designate it; if either is lacking, the outcome becomes **suspension of judgment** with the lack named.

- **Elements**—the records of the four preceding domains: recognized and clarified units, the framework taken, records of relations from comparison—they fill the role of supports; the types of transition—deduction, induction, abduction—the toolkit of the link of following, each with its own limiting strength; the scale of degrees—necessary, probabilistic, evaluative—from which the mark is taken; at the output—consequence-records with marks, or suspension of judgment.

A peculiarity of the domain: its output returns into its own input—the conclusion becomes the ground of the next step, and the ladder of reasoning grows by this recursion; that is why a defect of inference does not stay local—it is inherited by all the rungs built on it.

## The Principle of Rationalism

Inference is guided by the **Principle of Rationalism**: *a conclusion is earned by the preceding work; the mark of its strength is set by its grounds*. The principle requires two things.

*First: a conclusion follows from the grounds and does not precede them.* This is an application of [[Order|the Law of Order]] through the Principle of Sequence: the transition from premises to conclusion has one correct direction. When a conclusion *follows* from premises, the link is logical—checkable, reproducible by anyone who accepts the same premises. When premises are *selected* to fit a conclusion known in advance, the link is rhetorical—it persuades but does not prove. Outwardly both processes are alike: premises, a conclusion, the appearance of a transition. But the direction is opposite: in the first case the conclusion is earned, in the second it is fabricated. Selecting grounds to fit a ready conclusion is [[A Priori Argument|rationalization]]; in terms of order it is reverse movement.

*Second: a conclusion must be marked with a degree of certainty.* That a conclusion follows or does not follow is binary: there are two options, and there is no third—this is [[Excluded Middle|the Excluded Middle]], a law derived from Identity and Non-Contradiction. But the *strength* of the link differs, and it must be distinguished. Here too [[Sufficient Reason]] is at work: to earn a conclusion means to present a sufficient ground for precisely this conclusion and not another.

The principle protects against two opposite violations: against *a conclusion beyond the ground*—concluding more than the data allow—and against *evading an earned conclusion*—refusing a conclusion that follows with sufficient strength: the endless “more data are needed” when the data suffice. The principle requires balance: no more and no less than what has been earned; an earned conclusion is accepted even when it is uncomfortable. The price of refusing an earned conclusion is shown by the Semmelweis case on the page [[6 Reflection#Fallacies of the Domain of Reflection|“Reflection”]].

## Degrees of Certainty

The degree of an inference is of three kinds. A *necessary* inference cannot be otherwise: if the premises are true, the conclusion is true with logical inevitability. “All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal”—there is no probability here, only necessity. A *probabilistic* inference is grounded but admits exceptions: “the sun has risen every day—therefore it will rise tomorrow” is strong but not necessary. An *evaluative* inference concludes about value within accepted criteria: “for this goal option *A* is better than *B*”—change the criteria, and the conclusion may change.

A simple test helps to tell them apart—an attempt to deny the conclusion while the premises are true. If the denial produces a contradiction, the inference is necessary: given the premises “all mammals are warm-blooded” and “whales are mammals,” the statement “whales are not warm-blooded” contradicts them. If denial is possible without contradiction, the inference is probabilistic: given the premise “all swans observed have been white,” the statement “there exists a non-white swan” does not contradict it and may turn out true. If the conclusion depends on the criteria chosen, the inference is evaluative.

The principle requires: *mark the degree*. Do not pass off the probabilistic as necessary or the evaluative as factual, and do not hide uncertainty behind a confident tone. Mixing degrees is a source of endless disputes: someone passes off the probabilistic as necessary (“science has proven”) and meets justified resistance; someone rejects what is grounded, demanding an impossible necessity (“these are only models”); someone passes off the evaluative as factual (“we are obliged”), hiding a choice of values behind an appearance of objectivity. Honesty requires precision: *this follows necessarily*; *this follows with high probability*; *this follows if such-and-such values are accepted*.

## Three Types of Inference

The degree of certainty is one axis; there is another: the *type* of transition from premises to conclusion. There are three, and each does its own work.

*Deduction* goes from the general to the particular: “all planets of the Solar System revolve around the Sun; the Earth is a planet of the Solar System; therefore the Earth revolves around the Sun.” It transmits truth-value without loss—but neither does it expand knowledge: the conclusion was already contained in the premises, and deduction only makes it explicit. In return it is reliable as a mechanism: with true premises the conclusion is necessary.

*Induction* goes from the particular to the general: many swans observed are white—therefore all swans are white (Mill’s example). It expands knowledge beyond what has been observed—but gives no guarantee: the next swan may turn out black. Hume showed this once and for all: from the fact that something has been, it does not follow with necessity that it will be so henceforth.

*Abduction* goes to the best explanation: thirst, frequent urination, and weight loss are explained by diabetes better than by the alternatives—the patient probably has diabetes. It generates something new—a hypothesis that explains the facts—but remains a supposition until it has been tested: the same symptoms may point to another disease as well. Most living reasoning begins with abduction: to seize an explanation and then test it by deduction and observation.

An honest thinker knows which type they are using and does not confuse their strengths: to pass off an inductive generalization as deductive necessity is the very error of degree against which the principle warns.

> [!example]- Case: Neptune—an earned inference and a risky prediction
> By the 1840s Uranus was not behaving according to calculation: the observed orbit diverged from the one predicted by Newtonian mechanics. The discrepancy was small but persistent and admitted two kinds of explanation—either Newton’s mechanics was wrong, or something unaccounted for was acting on Uranus: the resistance of the ether, a large satellite, the impact of a comet, an unknown planet. The choice between the branches is the Principle of Rationalism in action: to discard mechanics confirmed by thousands of observations for the sake of one anomaly is a conclusion exceeding the ground; to suppose an unaccounted-for factor is a conclusion proportionate to the ground.
>
> Urbain Le Verrier in France and John Couch Adams in England independently took the second branch; Adams’s calculation was not published before the discovery. Le Verrier followed it through by exclusion. To change the law of gravitation, in Le Verrier’s words, is permissible only as a last resort, when the other causes have been exhausted; traces of the resistance of the ether have barely been glimpsed even in the motion of the least dense bodies, and a satellite and a comet do not agree with the observations; what remains is a body acting on Uranus continuously—a planet still unknown. Then abduction passes into deduction: if the perturbations are caused by an unknown planet, then from the magnitude of the deviations one can calculate *where* it must be. Le Verrier’s conclusion was extremely concrete—not “there is a planet somewhere” but the longitude at which it should be sought.
>
> The conclusion was earned—a calculation from data and laws, every step of which can be checked—and *risky*: point the telescope at the indicated spot, find nothing, and the hypothesis is refuted, with no retreat. On September 23, 1846, Johann Gottfried Galle at the Berlin Observatory received Le Verrier’s letter and that same evening, together with Heinrich d’Arrest, pointed the telescope at the indicated region. At first the planet could not be identified by its disk. On d’Arrest’s suggestion they took a new star chart of the Berlin Academy, not yet distributed, and after several comparisons a star of the eighth magnitude turned up that was not on the chart—about a degree from the predicted place. Observation the next night showed that it was moving among the stars. Galle wrote to Le Verrier on September 25: “La planète, dont vous avez signalé la position, réellement existe”—“the planet whose position you indicated really exists” (our translation).
>
> The inference solved the problem and discovered an object of whose existence no one had known. The ground was complete, the transition checkable, the prediction refutable; such is inference in its pure form. The same Le Verrier later applied the same move to Mercury: in 1859 Le Verrier found that Mercury’s perihelion advances 38″ per century more than calculated and ascribed the excess to matter between Mercury and the Sun—a planet or, more probably, a swarm of small bodies. The planet, nicknamed Vulcan, received no confirmation: the method was applied twice, and it proved right once, and the difference was decided by observation, not by the elegance of the calculation.
>

F. “Schreiben des Herrn Professors Encke an den Herausgeber,” *Astronomische Nachrichten* 25, no. 580 (1846): cols. 49–52; Galle, J. ## The Art of Suspending Judgment

Sometimes an earned conclusion is the conclusion that there is no conclusion yet. Suspension of judgment is an honest result of reasoning where one of the two checks of the outcome fails: are there enough grounds for an inference—and is there enough strength to designate it; if either is lacking—suspension. Suspension is a pause: it says “there are no grounds yet” and keeps the question open until new data arrive. It is easy to confuse with evading an earned conclusion; the difference is that honest suspension acknowledges a *specific* lack—which ground exactly is missing and what will supply it—while evasion hides behind a vague “the question is complicated.” The test is simple: can I name what would make me draw a conclusion? If yes, the suspension is honest; if every argument bounces off, caution has turned into an unwillingness to conclude.

## Grounds for Transition

The transition to [[6 Reflection|reflection]] takes place by [[Sufficient Reason|the Law of Sufficient Reason]]: the consequence is recorded and marked—or suspension of judgment is recorded with the lack named; in both cases the course of the domain is complete, and the path is ready to be checked.

Returns are built into the process itself—a regular move of the architecture ([[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|“Movement through the Domains”]]): if a ground is lacking, attention returns for it to the domain needed—for a unit, to [[1 Recognition|recognition]] and [[2 Clarification|clarification]]; to revise the region and the measure, to [[3 Framework Selection|framework selection]]; for a missing relation, to [[4 Comparison|comparison]]. The named lack of a suspension itself indicates where to go to supply it. The danger lies at the edges: to go further without formulating the conclusion or setting the mark is premature transition—a process without a result is an unfinished inference; to keep the question open when the conclusion has been earned is stalling—evasion of an earned conclusion in the guise of caution.

## Fallacies of the Domain of Inference

When the Principle of Rationalism is not observed, the conclusion claims more than the ground has given—and the danger is that it *seems* to follow: the feeling of following is easily taken for following itself. Violations are of two kinds: *formal* ones break the very structure of the transition; *material* ones keep the form but conclude more than is given. In the [[Fallacy Catalog|catalog]] the failures of the fifth domain are assigned the group [[2.5 Domain 5 Inference|2.5 “Domain 5: Inference”]]—**20 fallacies**: the conclusion exceeds its ground. Within it are four mechanisms: [[2.5 Domain 5 Inference#Logical Gap|logical gap]] (1), [[2.5 Domain 5 Inference#Causal Error|causal error]] (7), [[2.5 Domain 5 Inference#Chain Error|chain error]] (2), and [[2.5 Domain 5 Inference#Scale Error|scale error]] (10). Analyzed below are two formal fallacies—by mechanism these are a logical gap, and they do not yet have records in the catalog—and three material ones: one each from the causal, chain, and scale mechanisms.

The two formal fallacies mirror each other and are rooted in one thing—confusing a sufficient condition with a necessary one. The statement “if *A*, then *B*” says that *A* is sufficient for *B*, but not that *A* is necessary for *B*: *B* may have other sufficient conditions as well.

**Affirming the Consequent**—from “if *A*, then *B*” and “*B*,” one infers “*A*.” “If it is raining, the streets are wet; the streets are wet—so it is raining”: but streets are wet from a sprinkler truck, too, and from a burst pipe. Check: does this consequence have other possible causes?

**Denying the Antecedent**—from “if *A*, then *B*” and “not-*A*,” one infers “not-*B*.” “No diploma—so incompetent”: a diploma is sufficient but not necessary for competence. Only the move from denying the consequent to denying the condition is correct—modus tollens: “if *A*, then *B*; not-*B*; therefore not-*A*.” Check: are there other ways to reach this consequence?

**[[Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc]]**—a temporal sequence is taken for a causal one. “After the policy was introduced, the economy grew—the policy caused the growth”: but the growth may have come from global trends, the actions of others, the delayed effect of earlier decisions. Causality needs more than “after”: a mechanism of connection, the exclusion of alternatives, reproducibility. Check: what mechanism connects *A* and *B*, and have other explanations been excluded?

**[[Slippery Slope]]**—a chain of causal links in which each link is passed off as inevitable: *A* will lead to *B*, *B* to *C*, and so on to catastrophe. But each link is a separate inference requiring grounds, and if even one is ungrounded, the chain breaks. “Allow one thing—and we will inevitably slide all the way to the worst”: the possible is passed off as inevitable, and the points where the chain can be broken are ignored. Check: is each link grounded—and is the transition inevitable or only possible?

**[[Overgeneralization]]**—a conclusion is extended wider than the data allow. Comparison has established a regularity within certain bounds—these cases, these conditions; the conclusion leaps to “always, everywhere, for everyone.” “In three studies the method worked—it always works.” The signal is the words “always,” “never,” “all,” “no one.” Check: what are the bounds of the data—and does the conclusion go beyond them?

> [!example]- Case: John Snow—inference through the exclusion of alternatives
> On the night of August 31 to September 1, 1854, cholera broke out in London’s Soho (isolated cases had occurred at the end of August as well); within ten days more than five hundred people died, and over the whole outbreak, 616. The medical establishment was convinced: cholera is caused by miasmas—poisonous vapors of rotting organic matter. The logic seemed flawless: the disease rages where it is dirty and foul-smelling; where the smell is stronger, there are more sick. But this was *affirming the consequent*: if miasmas cause cholera, there will be a stench near the sick; there is a stench—so, miasmas. Meanwhile both the smell and the disease could be consequences of one cause—poor sanitary conditions.
>
> The physician John Snow had doubted this as early as 1849: cholera affects the intestines, not the lungs—so the cause enters the body, most likely with water. During the outbreak Snow copied out at the General Register Office the deaths of the past week and went round the houses: almost all the deaths had occurred in houses near the water pump on Broad Street. Later Snow plotted the deaths on a map—the dots clustered around the pump. But correlation is not yet a cause, and Snow sought a check through the exclusion of alternatives. The workers of the brewery at the epicenter—there were more than seventy of them—did not fall seriously ill: they had an allowance of malt liquor, in the owner’s opinion did not drink water at all, and the brewery had its own well. In the workhouse, more than three-quarters surrounded by houses of the dead, five of 535 inmates died—the workhouse had its own well. A widow who died of cholera in Hampstead, where there was no cholera at the time, had not been to Broad Street for a long time but preferred the water of this pump—it was brought to the house every day; the widow’s visiting niece also died from the same water. If the cause had been miasmas, the brewers would have breathed the same air and fallen ill; if poverty, the mortality, as Snow observed, would not have been distributed among all classes roughly in proportion to their numbers. Apart from the water of this pump, no other common circumstance was found in the focus of the outbreak: of the 73 who died near the pump, 61, according to their relatives, drank its water; in six cases nothing could be learned, and in only six was it said that the deceased had not drunk it.
>
> Snow’s conclusion was *earned*: it followed from the data through the exclusion of alternatives. The miasma theory seemed obvious; the theory of waterborne infection demanded work—data, a map, an analysis of exclusions. But the second was correct. Obviousness is no criterion of absolute truth; groundedness is.
>
> Sources: Snow, J. *On the Mode of Communication of Cholera*, 2nd ed., much enlarged (London: John Churchill, 1855), 9–15, 38–55; Johnson, S. *The Ghost Map: The Story of London’s Most Terrifying Epidemic—and How It Changed Science, Cities, and the Modern World* (New York: Riverhead Books, 2006); Brody, H., Rip, M. R., Vinten-Johansen, P., Paneth, N., Rachman, S. “Map-making and myth-making in Broad Street: the London cholera epidemic, 1854,” *The Lancet* 356, no. 9223 (2000): 64–68.

## Questions

The guiding question—**“what follows from this?”**—along the axes of the [[Question#The Question and the Domains|question]]: a completing goal of the category of ground (why? from what?); this is the **root step** of the path: movement along grounds is movement along the root—along logic. On the path of cognition this is the stage of **choice**, and it is completed here: the choice proceeds by grounds—the selection of hypotheses remains a move within the domain, and the step is closed by the extraction of the consequence; depth grows by one tier: the conclusion rises as a new tier of the root, from which the next step will proceed.

The working questions of the domain—by the rules of the domain and the two checks of the outcome:

- **Following**—“what follows from this?”—the guiding question itself;
- **Direction**—“does the conclusion follow from the grounds—or have the grounds been selected to fit what is desired?”—the rule of the direction of movement;
- **Support**—“has a premise been added that was not in the preceding domains?”—the rule “only what is established”;
- **Sufficiency**—“are there enough grounds—or is it more honest to suspend judgment?”—the first check of the outcome;
- **Earnedness**—“does the conclusion go beyond the ground, in breadth or in strength?”—the rule “not beyond the ground”;
- **Strength**—“what is the strength of the grounds—and has the mark been set by it: necessary, probabilistic, evaluative?”—the second check of the outcome, and marking.

The transition—by the [[#Grounds for Transition|grounds for transition]]: the consequence is recorded and marked—or suspension of judgment is recorded with the lack named—then [[6 Reflection|reflection]]: a check of the path traveled.

## Summary

1. **Inference is the extraction of a consequence from established grounds;** the guiding question is “what follows from this?”.
2. **The material of inference is the records of the four preceding domains;** the consequence settles as a new record, and the slice is built up: the conclusion becomes the ground of the next step, and a defect of inference is inherited by all the rungs built on it.
3. **The Principle of Rationalism:** a conclusion is earned by the preceding work; the mark of its strength is set by its grounds; no more and no less than what has been earned. The grounding law is Sufficient Reason; with it, Order (from grounds to consequence) and the Excluded Middle (follows or does not follow).
4. **The degrees of certainty are necessary, probabilistic, evaluative;** the denial test tells them apart. The types of inference are deduction, induction, abduction, each with its own limiting strength.
5. **The outcome is held by two checks**—are there enough grounds, and is there enough strength to designate the conclusion; if either is lacking—suspension of judgment with the lack named, and the lack indicates where to return to supply it.
6. **The fallacies of the domain—group 2.5 of the catalog, 20 records:** logical gap, causal error, chain error, scale error; affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are also a logical gap and do not yet have records.
7. **One passes to reflection when the consequence is recorded and marked—or suspension of judgment is recorded;** returns for a missing ground are a regular move.
