Comparison is the fourth of the six [[Domains|domains]] of reasoning: in it the measure taken by the framework is applied to the sides, and the relations between them are revealed. Here: where the domain comes from and how its work goes, how the ladder of levels of data sets the order of the operation, why comparison is the nodal domain, how the result depends on scale and sample, when the domain has been passed, and where comparison breaks down.

## Etymology

**Comparison**—in Russian *sravnenie* ← *s-* + **ravnyy** “equal” (Proto-Slavic *orvьnъ*, “level, equal”): to set side by side *as equals*—to apply one measure to two objects. Here is a rare case of full agreement between languages: Latin *comparatio* ← *com-* + *par* “equal”—literally the same composition, “co-equaling.” The word itself carries the condition of the domain: only what a common measure applies to is comparable—without the “equal” there is no comparison.

## Definition

**Comparison is the revealing of relations between objects through the application of a single criterion.**

[[1 Recognition|Recognition]] has given presence, [[2 Clarification|clarification]] essence, [[3 Framework Selection|framework selection]] a perspective. Now come relations: how does this relate to that? Before anything is concluded, the material must be set side by side, and the result of this juxtaposition will become the ground of the inference.

*Revealing*: comparison discovers relations. The relation “greater” between two numbers is there without anyone comparing; comparison makes it explicit. *Of relations*: the result is a link between objects, not a property of one of them. “Red” is a property; “redder” is a relation arising *between*. *Between objects*: a plurality is needed, at least two—hence the consequence that the unique is incomparable: what is one of a kind falls outside the logic of comparison and can only be accepted as a given. *Through a single criterion*: this is the core. Comparison is possible only where one gauge is applied to different objects. An apple and a pear are comparable by sweetness; an apple and justice are incomparable, because there is no measure applicable to both.

## Origin: A Function of the Mental Field

[[1 Recognition|Recognition]] fixes a difference (*A* / not-*A*). [[2 Clarification|Clarification]] fixes identity (what makes *A* = *A*). [[3 Framework Selection|Framework selection]] has fixed *what* to compare and *how*: the boundaries of the field and the measure. Comparison carries out the operation itself: it looks at structures and compares their build. The relation is not created here but discovered—it is there in the build of what is; the outcome of the operation is a revealed relation that has settled as a record into the witness’s subjective field—material with which [[5 Inference|inference]] works next.

## The Domain as a System

Analysis by the generating order—from the goal ([[Theory of Systems]]):

- **Goal**—a true record of a relation: of what is *between* the things compared, by the measure taken. *True*—what the measure shows is fixed, not what is expected; *record*—the outcome settles in the field as material for inference; *relation*—what is between the sides; *by the measure taken*—the measure has already been taken by the framework, and the work of the domain is to apply it. The domain receives its task from the [[3 Framework Selection|framework]]: what to set against what, and by what.

- **Rules**—from the goal, each from its own component. From *true*—**only what is present**: actually present attributes are compared, not projections and not ideals (the Principle of Presence). From *the measure*—**uniformity and invariance**: one criterion for all sides without exceptions (the Principle of Objectivity), and the measure does not change throughout the course—changed midway through the operation, it nullifies the relations gathered (Order; its violation is “moving the goalposts”). From *the relation between*—**comparability before application**: the sides must have a level to which the measure is applied—checked from the top down along the hierarchy of levels of data. From *the task*—**completeness of the list**: the measure is applied to everything assigned, not to a convenient part; the transition is by sufficient reason, when the list is filled. The Principle of the Root here is a boundary rule: the ground of the measure is the business of the framework; if the question of the ground arises or the measure cannot be applied, the work returns to framework selection.

- **Roles**—the witness who applies the measure (the operation is led by the witness’s will, whose manifestation is attention: it holds the measure, checks comparability, goes down the list), and the object—the sides of the relation (a side is a role, not the thing itself: the same thing in another comparison is another side); the comparison list—what is drawn up according to the task set by the framework; its filling is the ground for transition.

- **Elements**—the clarified unit-records from [[1 Recognition|recognition]] and [[2 Clarification|clarification]] with their present attributes by levels of the hierarchy (data, information, qualities, characteristics); the framework taken, with its measuring axis; pairs and groupings according to the task—from them the list is drawn up; at the output—records of relations that have filled the list.

A peculiarity of the domain: almost all elements arrive ready-made from the preceding domains—comparison works on the material gathered; the only things born here are the records of relations. If a unit is lacking, there is a return for it to recognition and clarification, not a completion on the spot.

## The Hierarchy of Levels of Data—in Action

[[1 Recognition|Recognition]] proceeds from the general to the particular along the ladder: **data, information, qualities, characteristics**. First it is noticed that something *is* (data); then *what exactly* it is (information); then which categories of difference apply (qualities: color, shape, size); and only then the concrete values within them (characteristics: red, square, large). The ladder is irreversible: one cannot determine the characteristic “red” without having the quality “color”; one cannot have a quality without having information about the object; one cannot have information without data.

In comparison this ladder works as the order of the operation itself: build is compared level by level, from the top down. Before comparing characteristics (red against blue), check that both sides have the quality itself (color); before qualities, that the information is comparable; before information, that data have been taken at all. Comparing values when the upper level is incomparable is a category mistake, analyzed below: the measure is applied to a level that one of the sides does not have.

## The Principle: The Nodal Domain

Comparison is **the nodal domain**: it is guided by three principles at once—Presence, Objectivity, and the Root. It stands in the middle of the domains for a reason: it is the node through which all the links of the architecture pass. Where recognition rests on one law and inference on several, comparison engages both primary [[Logic#The Unity of the Laws|laws]]—Identity and Non-Contradiction—and both meta-laws—Order and Sufficient Reason—at once. That is why this domain is the nodal one; below, one at a time.

**[[Identity]]** holds the very presence of what is compared—and holds it twice. Objects must remain themselves: if *A* changed while attention was on *B*, one thing was compared and the result attributed to another. And it makes sense to compare only what is distinguishable: were *A* and *B* identical in every respect, it would be one object, and there would be nothing to compare. Hence the **Principle of Presence** in comparative form: one may compare only *actually present* attributes—not projections, not ideals, not imagined alternatives: these change depending on who imagines them and when. Here, too, lies a subtle and frequent error—comparison with an *absence*. One can compare *A* with *B*; one cannot compare *A* with “the absence of *B*”: that is already a construction that passes off “is not” as “is.” The absence of a feature is not a feature, and to turn it into a property is to pass off the result of a comparison as the initial given.

**[[Non-Contradiction]]** holds the unity of the criterion. Comparing *A* and *B* by the measure *C*, one cannot in the same comparison also apply the measure not-*C*: otherwise “*A* is greater than *B* by *C*” and “*A* is less than *B* by not-*C*” would yield a contradiction from which no inference can be drawn. Hence the **Principle of Objectivity**: to apply the chosen criterion uniformly to all elements, making no exceptions in anyone’s favor. Double standards, the selection of convenient data, an imperceptible shift of the measure—these are violations of one law in thinking. And the same law governs intention: absolute truth above all, and one cannot at once seek absolute truth and fit the result to what is desired.

**[[Order]]** enters with both of its dimensions. *Sequence* requires that the measure hold throughout the course of the operation: changed midway, it nullifies the relations gathered. *Hierarchy* requires a common scale: to relate two objects, a measure is needed in which both have a value. “Which weighs more—an elephant or the kindness of a deed?”—the question is empty until there is a common scale; different scales are incommensurable, and an attempt to measure through them gives rise to a category mistake. And the same Order bids one begin from the highest level: before comparing characteristics, check comparability at the level of information—to compare by color an object with one set of data and an object with two is like comparing the color of an apple with the color of a pair of apples.

**[[Sufficient Reason]]** requires a ground for the measure itself: an arbitrarily taken measure is a whim, not cognition. The measure is taken by the framework, and the **Principle of the Root** here is a boundary rule: to trace the criterion back to the ground that justifies it; most often such a ground is the problem at hand: a criterion is appropriate if it answers the question for the sake of which the comparison was undertaken; if no ground is found, the work returns to [[3 Framework Selection|framework selection]].

So the architecture closes. Identity through Presence holds the presence of attributes and the stability of objects; Non-Contradiction through Objectivity—the unity of the criterion; Order—comparability and the invariance of the measure; Sufficient Reason through the Root—the groundedness of the measure. All four are necessary: violate any one—and comparison becomes either impossible or unreliable. That is why comparison is the node in which the whole architecture works at once.

## Scale, Sample, Common Criterion

Comparison unfolds at different *scales*, and the result depends on the scale. One can compare individual objects (this candidate and that), classes and aggregates (men and women—averages, shares, distributions), systems (whole structures with emergent properties: water is wet, although neither hydrogen nor oxygen is wet), and theories (the very ways of understanding). Transferring a regularity from level to level is dangerous: data about groups are not yet data about the people who make them up. The relations between variables at one level can be the reverse of those at another—such is the reversal known as [[Simpson's Paradox|Simpson’s paradox]][^1]. The reversal is a property of reality, not a statistical trick; it only seems a paradox: there is no contradiction, the defect lies in the expectation that what is better in each part is better in the whole as well. When drawing an inference from a comparison, it is worth asking: at what level was it carried out, and will the result survive a change of scale?

Bound up with scale is the *sample*: only what has come into view is compared, and selection can distort the picture. Survivorship bias[^2] arises where only the survivors are visible: returns are shown by the funds that survived, the ones that went bankrupt have vanished from the statistics, and the average of the survivors is inflated; advice is given by those who succeeded, not by the thousands who did the same and went bankrupt; old buildings seem more beautiful than new ones because the ugly ones were torn down. The structure is the same everywhere: the sample is distorted by selection, and an inference from a comparison based on it is systematically mistaken. This does not contradict the Principle of Presence: only present attributes are compared, but first one must make sure that all the relevant objects are in view. Those absent from the sample are data too; their absence is information. Before comparing what is visible, ask: are there those I do *not see*, and why?

Comparison also serves joint thinking, and here its condition is a *common criterion*, named aloud. Without agreement on *what* to measure by, comparison is impossible, and what remains is a clash of monologues. That is why the question “by what criterion are we evaluating this?” is one of the most valuable in any dispute: it moves the conversation from positions to grounds, and it often turns out that the disagreement is rooted in criteria, not in facts.

> [!example]- Case: The Judgment of Paris—a single criterion against prejudice
> On 24 May 1976, at the InterContinental hotel in Paris, the British wine merchant Steven Spurrier organized a blind tasting: French wines against Californian ones; the judges were nine French experts, the top of the French wine establishment. The blindness of the procedure was its very point: the label is removed, the wine remains.
>
> The Californian wines won—among both reds and whites. The French experts were shocked; one of the judges, Odette Kahn, editor of the prestigious magazine *Revue du vin de France*, according to George Taber, demanded the ballot back so that no one would learn the scores given; the ballot was not returned.
>
> The arrangement of the case is purely architectural. Comparison by origin (“French against Californian”) is an irrelevant feature in place of an evaluation of content; the criterion “taste of the wine” applies to both, the criterion “country” does not measure quality. The blind tasting did not make the judges more objective as people—it made it impossible to apply the criterion non-uniformly: without knowing the label, one cannot go easy on one’s own. A single criterion applied to all without exception is the Principle of Objectivity in working form.
>
> Sources: Taber, G. M. *Judgment of Paris: California vs. France and the Historic 1976 Paris Tasting That Revolutionized Wine* (New York: Scribner, 2005); Godoy, M. “The Judgment of Paris: The Blind Taste Test That Decanted the Wine World,” NPR, May 24, 2016.

## Grounds for Transition

The transition to [[5 Inference|inference]] takes place by [[Sufficient Reason|the Law of Sufficient Reason]]: when the process is completed according to the established goal of the comparison—**the comparison list is filled**. The goal and the measure taken by the framework set what to set against what, and by what; the transition is grounded when the measure has been applied to all the assigned material—not only to what is convenient and lies on the surface—and the regularities have stabilized: new cases confirm the structure revealed. As long as each new case requires revision, comparison is still under way; when they fit in, saturation has been reached.

Returns are built into the process itself—a regular move of the architecture ([[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|“Movement through the Domains”]]): if an attribute is lacking, attention returns to [[1 Recognition|recognition]] and [[2 Clarification|clarification]] for the missing unit (the cycle of units of information); if the measure cannot be applied or the levels are incomparable—back to [[3 Framework Selection|framework selection]], to revise the region and the measure. The danger lies at the edges: to pass to inference when the measure has been applied only to a convenient part is premature transition (fatigue disguises itself as completion); to keep comparing when the list is filled and new cases only confirm what has been revealed is stalling.

## Fallacies of the Domain of Comparison

When the discipline of the domain is not observed, comparison breaks down while remaining convincing in appearance. Aristotle already noted in the *Topics* that the question of which of two things is preferable is posed about things that are close, not far apart from each other, and showed incomparability where a predicate is said in different senses: a “sharp” flavor and a “sharp” sound can be called neither equally sharp nor one sharper than the other[^3]. Below are five ways of violating the discipline of the domain. In the [[Fallacy Catalog|catalog]] the failures of the fourth domain are assigned the group [[2.4 Domain 4 Comparison|2.4 “Domain 4: Comparison”]]—**8 fallacies**: a defective application of the framework to the material. Within it are three mechanisms: [[2.4 Domain 4 Comparison#False Equation|false equation]] (4), [[2.4 Domain 4 Comparison#Unstable Criteria|unstable criteria]] (3), and [[2.4 Domain 4 Comparison#Comparison with Nonexistent|comparison with the nonexistent]] (1). Two of the five forms are not in the group: comparing the incomparable is, by its address, a failure of the framework ([[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection#Category Mismatch|category mismatch]]), and incomplete comparison does not yet have a record in the catalog.

**[[2.4 Domain 4 Comparison#False Equation|False Equivalence]]**—essentially different things are declared equivalent on the strength of a superficial similarity in one respect. “He was five minutes late, she was an hour late—both broke the agreement”: formally true, but equating five minutes and an hour erases an essential difference in degree. Check: in what exactly are the objects equal, in what are they different—and is the difference essential to the question?

**[[2.3 Domain 3 Framework Selection#Category Mismatch|Comparing the Incomparable]]** (comparing apples and oranges)—the objects have no common ground at all with respect to the given criterion. “Who is better—Beethoven or Einstein?”—better at what? As a composer and a physicist they are incomparable. Check: does the criterion apply to both objects—do they have a common ground? If there is no common ground, the work returns to [[3 Framework Selection|framework selection]].

**[[Moving the Goalposts]]**—[[2.4 Domain 4 Comparison#Unstable Criteria|the criterion changes]] in the course of the comparison, and it is never completed. “Prove that you are competent.”—“Here is my diploma.”—“A diploma means nothing; where is the experience?”—“Here are my projects.”—“They were team projects; where is your personal result?” Each time a condition is met, a new one is put forward; behind this there is usually an unwillingness to accept an undesirable conclusion. Check: were the criteria set in advance—and do they shift every time a “wrong” answer approaches?

**Incomplete Comparison**—the criteria are stable but insufficient: the comparison proceeds by convenient attributes, bypassing relevant ones. “Our product is cheaper”—in price, yes, but in quality, durability, service? Check: has the comparison been carried out by all relevant criteria—which parameters are not mentioned, and why?

**[[2.4 Domain 4 Comparison#Comparison with Nonexistent|Utopia Fallacy]]**—the real is compared with a nonexistent ideal and therefore loses in advance. “This marriage is imperfect. True love would have no conflicts”: real relationships are compared with a romantic ideal, but the ideal is not present as an option—only other real relationships are present, with imperfections of their own. Comparison with an ideal is legitimate for *orientation* (“what are we striving for?”); the error arises when the ideal serves to *condemn* what exists, since the real is always worse than the ideal by definition. Check: does what I am comparing with exist—and what does the comparison serve, direction or rejection?

> [!example]- Case: The *Challenger*—an incomplete comparison and a rejected inference
> On 28 January 1986 the space shuttle *Challenger* broke apart seventy-three seconds after launch; seven people died. The technical cause, according to the conclusion of the presidential commission, was the failure of the seal in the joint between the two lower segments of the right solid rocket booster: the design of the joint was unacceptably sensitive to a number of factors, among them temperature—in the cold the O-rings lost elasticity, and hot gases broke through the joint. But behind it stood an incomplete comparison and a rejected inference.
>
> The morning of the launch was unusually cold: the air at the pad was about 2 °C (36 °F), 8 °C (15 °F) colder than at any previous launch. The knowledge was there: erosion of the rings had been observed since 1981. The evening before, the engineers of Morton Thiokol, knowing that rubber hardens in the cold, recommended not launching while the temperature of the rings was below 12 °C (53 °F)—the lowest in previous flight experience. *Incomplete comparison*: in the materials for the teleconference, damage to the rings was shown only for the flights where it had occurred, and the temperature only for two of the twenty-four previous launches; the flights without damage, which had taken place in warm weather, did not enter the comparison, and the link between cold and damage remained hidden. The full history shows it: thermal damage to the rings occurred in all four launches with a ring temperature of 17 °C (63 °F) or below, and in only three of twenty at 19 °C (66 °F) or above. *False equivalence*: Thiokol’s management reversed its position and wrote in its final conclusion that this launch “will not be significantly different” from the coldest of the previous ones—although, by its own calculation, the rings would this time be 11 °C (20 °F) colder. The engineers’ inference was rejected, and the engineers themselves were required to *prove* that the launch was dangerous: ordinarily safety is what must be proved, not catastrophe, and a failure cannot be proved before the failure. This is [[Shifting the Burden of Proof|burden inversion]]—a failure of order; what was rejected was reflection ([[Domains#In Ethics|“Domains,” “In Ethics”]]).
>
> At the hearings the physicist Richard Feynman dipped a piece of the ring material into a glass of ice water and showed that in the cold the rubber does not recover its shape. This was the very comparison that should have been made before the launch—simple, clear, honest. “For a successful technology,” Feynman wrote in the report, “reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled.” The defective comparison convinced people—but the ring lost its elasticity regardless of which tables were shown to the managers.
>
> Sources: *Report of the Presidential Commission on the Space Shuttle Challenger Accident*, vols. 1–5 (Washington, D.C., 1986), vol. 1, chs. III–VI; vol. 2, app. F: Feynman, R. P. “Personal Observations on Reliability of Shuttle”; vol. 4, 679–680, transcript (hearing of February 11, 1986); Vaughan, D. *The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1996); Tufte, E. R. *Visual Explanations: Images and Quantities, Evidence and Narrative* (Cheshire, CT: Graphics Press, 1997), ch. 2, “Visual and Statistical Thinking: Displays of Evidence for Making Decisions,” 27–53.

## Questions

The guiding question—**“how does this relate to that?”**—along the axes of the [[Question#The Question and the Domains|question]]: a completing goal of the category of relation and measure (how? how much?); on the path of cognition this is the stage of **choice**: the framework has given the criterion, comparison applies it to all the sides—the choice here proceeds by grounds; the step is closed by the revealed relation.

The working questions of the domain—in the order of work:

- **Relation**—“how does *A* relate to *B*?”—the guiding question itself;
- **Holding the measure**—“by what criterion am I comparing—the one taken by the framework?”;
- **Comparability**—“are the things compared of one level—is there a measure applicable to both?”—along the hierarchy of levels of data, before comparing;
- **Presence**—“am I comparing what is present—not an ideal, not a projection, not an ‘absence’?”—the Principle of Presence in comparative form;
- **Uniformity**—“has the criterion been applied to all without exception?”—the Principle of Objectivity within comparison;
- **The ground of the measure**—“what does the measure itself stand on?”—the Principle of the Root; the question of the ground returns the work to framework selection;
- **Completeness of the list**—“is the comparison list filled—has all the assigned material passed through the measure?”—the ground for transition ([[Sufficient Reason]]).

The transition—by the [[#Grounds for Transition|grounds for transition]]: the relations have been revealed and the material of inference gathered—next comes [[5 Inference|inference]].

## Summary

1. **Comparison is the revealing of relations between objects through the application of a single criterion;** the guiding question is “how does this relate to that?”.
2. **Framework selection has fixed what to compare and how; comparison carries out the operation itself**—it looks at structures and compares their build; it receives its task from the framework, and its outcome is records of relations.
3. **The ladder of levels of data is the order of the operation:** data, information, qualities, characteristics; comparability is checked before the measure is applied, beginning from the highest level.
4. **The nodal domain—Presence, Objectivity, and the Root together;** the grounding laws are Identity, Non-Contradiction, Sufficient Reason, and Order.
5. **The result depends on scale and sample;** in joint thinking the condition of comparison is a common criterion, named aloud.
6. **The fallacies of the domain—group 2.4 of the catalog, 8 records:** false equation, unstable criteria, comparison with the nonexistent; alongside them, comparing the incomparable (by address—framework selection) and incomplete comparison.
7. **One passes to inference when the comparison list is filled:** the measure has been applied to all the assigned material; returns to recognition, clarification, and framework selection are a regular move.
