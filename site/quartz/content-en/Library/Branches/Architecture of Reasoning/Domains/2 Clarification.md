Clarification is the second of the six [[Domains|domains]] of reasoning: in it, what makes *A* = *A* gets fixed—the name is unfolded to the essence. Here: the two directions of clarification, the depth of understanding, the Principle of the Root, the forces that resist clarity, and the fallacies of the domain.

## Etymology

**Clarification**—in Russian *proyasnenie* ← *pro-* + *yasnit’* ← **yasnyy** “clear.” The axis of meaning is **light and distinctness**: Russian *yasnyy* holds both sides—the bright (a clear sky, a clear day) and the distinct (a clear thought, a clear answer); the clear is what is lit through and visible in its outlines. The prefix *pro-* carries the action through and to fullness—*pro-svetit’* “to shine through,” *pro-zret’* “to regain sight,” *pro-yasnit’* “to clarify.” The same image—“to make clear is to bring to light”—lives in the Latin line as well: Latin *clarus* “bright, clear” gave English *clear* and German *klar*, and through *clarificare* (in Church Latin, “to glorify”) English *clarify*, *clarification*; from *lux* “light” come *lucidus* “clear” and *elucidare* “to illuminate, to make clear.” Distinctness is given by Latin *distinguere* “to separate”: the clear is what is distinguished, seen apart. Another image of the same work is unfolding: Latin *explicatio* ← *explicare* “to unfold what is folded,” where the meaning folded into a word unfolds into understanding. Light and unfolding are the two sides of the domain: to bring meaning to light by unfolding the name to the essence.

## Definition

**Clarification is movement from name to essence, from word to understanding.**

[[1 Recognition|Recognition]] works with presence—*what is here?* Clarification works with essence—*what is this?*

A name is not yet understanding: the familiarity of a word passes itself off as an understanding of the thing and turns out to be mere knowledge of the word. Clarification begins where the name ends.

Recognition has supplied the material; clarification asks: *what* is this? what is it that I have recognized? how is it to be named precisely? These are several different questions—of *identity* (what is it, to what kind does it belong?), of *essence* (what makes it what it is, that without which it would cease to be itself?), of *difference* (how does it differ from what is similar?), of *meaning* (what does it mean *in this context*?).

## Origin: A Function of the Mental Field

Recognition fixes a **difference**: *A* / not-*A*—the boundary is drawn, information has settled as a record into the witness’s subjective field, its slice of the objective field. Clarification fixes **identity**: what makes *A* = *A*—that without which *A* would cease to be itself. Such is the work of the two domains: distinction lays down the record—clarification discloses its essence; and the essence is the constitution: what must stand as long as *A* is *A* ([[Identity]])—which is why the essential level of understanding is the seeing of the constitution.

In terms of the field, clarification is **the building of a record into a structure**: to connect *A* with its kind, its boundary, its meaning—with other records; this is an ascent along the [[Truth and Absolute Truth#The Rungs of Representation|rungs of representation]]—from opinion through knowledge-that (in [[Epistemology#Types of Knowledge: Knowledge-About, Knowledge-How|“Epistemology”]], knowledge-about) to understanding; the levels of depth of understanding are the rungs of linking. Perception and understanding are one act of the intellect at two depths: to perceive is to distinguish, and the data receive a form; to understand is to carry the form through to the structure, to see what makes *A* precisely *A* ([[Metaphysics#The Intellect|“Metaphysics,” “The Intellect”]]); recognition works at the first depth, clarification at the second. When the links lack material, clarification returns attention to recognition—for the manifestations of *A* and for what is next to *A* but is not *A*: [[1 Recognition|the cycle of the domains]].

## The Domain as a System

Analysis by the generating order—from the goal ([[Theory of Systems]]):

- **Goal**—to fix what makes *A* = *A*—to unfold the name to the essence far enough that reasoning can rest on the concept.
- **Rules**—from the goal: the [[#The Principle of the Root|Principle of the Root]] (dig down to a support; stopping is by sufficient reason relative to the goal); the identity of the term (*A* remains *A*—the Law of Identity applied to meaning); a request to recognition when material is lacking (the cycle); the levels of understanding are the rungs of depth: nominal, dictionary, functional, essential.
- **Roles**—the witness who leads from name to essence (the move is led by its will, whose manifestation is attention), and the object—the recognized unit *A* with its term: the term carries a meaning and is bound to keep it along the whole path.
- **Elements**—the recognized unit-records and their names; meanings, definitions (a record-link of kind and boundary); at the output—a **concept**: a record built by links into the structure of the slice.

## The Two Directions of Clarification

Clarification goes in two directions. **Into the system**—what makes *A* = *A*: the very arrangement of *A*, its constitution, the systems nested in *A*. **Outward**—what stands next to *A* but differs—the boundary with what is similar; and how *A* interacts with other systems. The four questions of definition fall on these directions: essence and arrangement—inward; difference and meaning-in-context—outward. And both branches of the cycle with [[1 Recognition|recognition]] are the same directions: the inner one sends for the manifestations of *A*, the outer one for what is next to *A*.

## The Depth of Understanding

Not every clarification is equally deep; understanding has levels:

- *Nominal*: I recognize it and can tell it apart—but I do not know why it is what it is.
- *Dictionary*: I can give a definition—but I do not know how it works.
- *Functional*: I understand the mechanism and can predict the behavior.
- *Essential*: I grasp the nature—that without which the object would cease to be itself.

Which level is needed depends on the goal, and the error lies in stopping earlier than the goal requires—this is [[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|premature transition]]. A politician discussing “freedom” at the nominal level—by pointing to examples—will not be able to resolve the conflict between freedom of speech and protection from hatred: for that, at least the functional level is needed—how freedom *works* in society.

> [!example]- Case: “Gene”—clarification transforms a discipline
> Gregor Mendel discovered the laws of heredity—reporting them in 1865 and publishing them in 1866—without having the concept “gene”: Mendel called the hereditary units “elements” (*Elemente*); it was Mendel’s followers in the early twentieth century who began to call them “factors.” The word was introduced by Wilhelm Johannsen in 1909—and Johannsen stressed its caution: “The word ‘gene’ is completely free from any hypothesis; it expresses only the established fact that, at any rate, many properties of the organism are conditioned by special, separable and hence independent ‘states,’ ‘foundations,’ ‘dispositions’ in the gametes…”
>
> But what is a gene? The answer kept changing. *A unit of heredity*—what is passed on from parents and determines a trait. *A segment of a chromosome*—after the work of Thomas Morgan’s school (the first genetic map, by Alfred Sturtevant, 1913) it became clear that genes are arranged linearly on chromosomes. *A DNA sequence coding for a protein*—after the discovery of the structure of DNA and of the genetic code. Each refinement seemed final.
>
> And then it turned out: one “gene” can code for many different proteins through alternative splicing; there are sequences that code for no protein at all but regulate others. How many genes does a human have? In 1990 the working figure was a rough estimate—100,000; reading the genome gave a quarter of that: in 2004, 20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes. And today the answer depends on the definition: protein-coding genes number about 19,500, and together with the genes of non-coding RNAs about 63,000. In 2007 Mark Gerstein and colleagues, summarizing the results of the ENCODE project, proposed redefining the concept.
>
> For decades geneticists used the word “gene” as a working term, although its vagueness had been noted earlier (in 1957 Seymour Benzer proposed dividing the “gene” into the cistron, the recon, and the muton): the word was familiar, and the subject seemed understood. Clarification—the honest admission “we do not know exactly what a gene is”—did not stop science but opened new research. Clarity is an unspectacular result: it makes all the others possible.
>
> Sources: Mendel, G. “Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden,” *Verhandlungen des naturforschenden Vereines in Brünn* 4 (1865), Abhandlungen (Brünn, 1866): 3–47; Johannsen, W. *Elemente der exakten Erblichkeitslehre* (Jena: G. Fischer, 1909), 124 (our translation); Sturtevant, A. H. “The Linear Arrangement of Six Sex-Linked Factors in Drosophila, as Shown by Their Mode of Association,” *Journal of Experimental Zoology* 14, no. 1 (1913): 43–59; Pertea, M., and Salzberg, S. L. “Between a Chicken and a Grape: Estimating the Number of Human Genes,” *Genome Biology* 11, no. 5 (2010): 206; Gerstein, M. B., et al. “What Is a Gene, Post-ENCODE? History and Updated Definition,” *Genome Research* 17, no. 6 (2007): 669–681; Benzer, S. “The Elementary Units of Heredity,” in *A Symposium on the Chemical Basis of Heredity*, ed. W. D. McElroy and B. Glass (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins Press, 1957); GENCODE Human Release 50, statistics (gencodegenes.org/human/stats.html).

## The Principle of the Root

Clarification is guided by the **Principle of the Root**: *dig down to a support; stopping is by sufficient reason relative to the goal*.

There is no natural stopping point in clarification itself—it is given by the [[Sufficient Reason|meta-law of Sufficient Reason]]: as soon as a concept has been clarified enough for reasoning to rest on it, there is sufficient reason to pass to the next domain. Deepening goes as far as this ground—and no further; for greater depth one comes back by a deepening return, when understanding is too little for the task. Clarification is completed not absolutely but relative to the *goal*.

The **Principle of the Root** leads not only deep into one object but also between objects. Often the first question only seems right: a deeper analysis reveals that the answer lies in the encompassing system—and the path to it runs through the root of the nested system to the root of the parent one. The root question ([[Question#The Ladder of Questions|“The Ladder of Questions”]]) is the one that leads to *the essence of the system under consideration*; the criterion of completion is a sufficient reason for the next step, not “that will do for me.” The signs of sufficient clarification are more objective than “it seems to me that’s enough”—they are named in the section [[#Grounds for Transition|“Grounds for Transition.”]]

## Why Clarity Is Avoided

Clarification requires effort, but laziness is not the whole story: it is actively resisted. The chief enemy is *the illusion of understanding*: the word is familiar, so it seems understood. The illusion is fed more deeply by the very [[Epistemology#Types of Knowledge: Knowledge-About, Knowledge-How|arrangement of knowledge]]: at second hand, from a word, only a distillate arrives—that part of knowledge-*about* which can be passed on, and it is less than what is held; the meeting with the thing and the skill that is given only by going through—knowledge-*how*—remain with the one who has gone through.[^1] The abundance of what has been passed on is then taken for an understanding of the essence, though what has been collected is only what is easiest to pass on. For years people use “justice,” “freedom,” “love,” sure that they know what these are; Socrates showed the Athenians the emptiness behind familiar words—and was disliked for it: no one is glad to discover their own ignorance. The illusion is insidious in that false understanding is experienced as true[^2]; the only defense is a check: to explain to another, to apply, to meet a counterexample.

Clarification is also resisted by cruder forces: impatience (on to conclusions quickly—but a problem posed in unclarified terms is unsolvable) and social pressure—“everyone understands, it is awkward to ask”[^3]—because of which in a dispute each hears their own meaning in the common word and refutes a position the other did not hold. But deepest of all is *the fear of being wrong*, one of the enemies of truth. To admit that a word was not understood and that a position rested on an empty term is to admit one’s own error; whoever mistakes their records about themselves for themselves defends a record—the justification of one’s position—rather than absolute truth ([[Metaphysics#The Ego Zone|“The Ego Zone”]]). Here runs the boundary: **absolute truth above all**—and first of all above one’s own ego; whoever is not ready to turn out wrong guards their own image, and clarification—the path to absolute truth—is a threat to them. The antidote is Socratic not-knowing: the habit of asking oneself “what do I actually mean?”, especially when everything seems clear—and the readiness to hear the answer, all the more if it goes against the one asking: then the fire of absolute truth cleanses one of illusions.

## Grounds for Transition

The transition to [[3 Framework Selection|framework selection]] takes place by [[Sufficient Reason|the Law of Sufficient Reason]]: the key terms are defined explicitly and understood in the same way, a term does not change its meaning along the way; hidden assumptions are named; the goal of the question is formed. To stop earlier is premature transition; not to pass when the ground is there is stalling ([[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|“Movement through the Domains”]]).

## Fallacies of the Domain of Clarification

When this discipline is not observed, clarification breaks down: reasoning goes on, resting on words that only seem clear; the [[Identity|Law of Identity]] requires that *A* remain *A*—and there are several ways to fail to hold to this in thinking. In the [[Fallacy Catalog|catalog]] the group [[2.2 Domain 2 Clarification|2.2 “Domain 2: Clarification”]] counts **13 fallacies**: insufficient clarity of concepts and terms. Within it are four mechanisms: [[2.2 Domain 2 Clarification#Meaning Drift|meaning drift]] (a term imperceptibly changes its sense—7, the most populated), [[2.2 Domain 2 Clarification#Hidden Agent|hidden agent]] (1), [[2.2 Domain 2 Clarification#Incomplete Analysis|incomplete analysis]] (the analysis of possibilities is cut short—3), and [[2.2 Domain 2 Clarification#Excessive Clarification|excessive clarification]] (2). Analyzed below are three frequent representatives of the group—two from meaning drift, one from incomplete analysis—and a fourth case that by classification belongs to the [[03 Sequence Violations|violations of sequence]] (type 3): a question-begging definition is reverse movement, but it shows itself precisely in clarification, and so it is analyzed here.

**[[Equivocation]]**—meaning drift. One term imperceptibly changes its sense in the course of reasoning: *A* seems equal to *A*, but these are different *A*s under one name. “The end of a thing is its goal; death is the end of life; therefore death is the goal of life”: “end” first means *purpose*, then *completion*. Check: write out the meaning of the term at each place where it occurs—do the meanings diverge?

**[[Equivocation|Ambiguity]]**—indeterminacy from the very start: the term is ambiguous before reasoning even gets under way, and reasoning continues although it is unclear what exactly is meant. Two different statements are hidden in one phrase, and the participants may understand it differently without noticing it. Check: is there another way to read this—and which sense is meant?

**[[Either Or Reasoning|False Dilemma]]**—incompleteness of the space of possibilities. Two options are offered where there are more. This is a failure of clarification, not of framework selection, because *exploring* the possible precedes choosing from it: before choosing, one must know what one is choosing from. The [[Excluded Middle|Law of Excluded Middle]] guarantees “*A* or not-*A*”; it does not guarantee “*X* or *Y*” if *Y* is only one of many variants of not-*X*. Check: are these really all the possibilities? why exactly these two?

**A question-begging definition**—a conclusion hidden in the very definition of a term rather than in a premise; by type it is a [[03 Sequence Violations|violation of sequence]]. The analytic form of the [[Circular Reasoning|vicious circle]]: reverse movement disguised as a neutral preparatory step. “A true patriot supports this policy; you do not support it—so you are not a patriot”: “patriotism” is defined through support for the policy, and the conclusion is inevitable only because it is already built into the definition. Check: does the definition contain what is to be proved?

> [!example]- Case: The Martian Canals—a word that created a civilization
> In 1877 the Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli made out dark lines on Mars and, following Angelo Secchi, called them “canali.” The Italian word does not distinguish the man-made from the natural: *canale* can mean a dug canal, a natural channel, or a strait; in translation into English it became “canals”—man-made canals (a natural watercourse is a “channel”). This unclarified ambiguity shaped almost ninety years of research.
>
> Percival Lowell, having read about the canals, built an observatory specially for Mars and for twenty years mapped a network of straight lines, seeing in it *the irrigation system of a dying civilization*: the Martians, saving their decrepit planet, drive the meltwater of the polar caps to the equator and beyond, and the visible lines are bands of vegetation revived by the water, converging in “oases.” Other observers saw canals too, but the drawings often diverged, and with large telescopes the network was not confirmed: Edward Barnard at the Lick Observatory (1894) failed to see Schiaparelli’s canals “as straight narrow lines,” and Eugène Antoniadi with the 83-centimeter refractor at Meudon (1909) did not find their network.
>
> When in 1965 *Mariner 4* transmitted the first close-up images of Mars, there was not a single canal on them, although the imaging track crossed several “canals” from the maps—a cratered surface, like the Moon’s. The images covered about one percent of the surface; *Mariner 9* imaged almost the entire planet in 1971–1972—and there were no canals there either. The lines turned out to be an artifact of perception—as the experiment of Evans and Maunder (1903) had already shown: the eye at the limit of resolution joins scattered dark spots into lines, and the brain, seeking a pattern, finds it where there is none.
>
> The root of the error is an unclarified term. As long as astronomers spoke of “canals,” they *saw* canals: the word already carried the category “artificial structure” and prejudged the question—they asked not “what are these lines?” but “who built these canals?”, and the question contained the answer. Had Schiaparelli written “dark linear structures of unknown nature,” almost ninety years might have gone differently.
>
> Sources: Schiaparelli, G. V. “Osservazioni astronomiche e fisiche sull’asse di rotazione e sulla topografia del pianeta Marte… durante l’opposizione del 1877,” *Atti della R. Accademia dei Lincei. Memorie della Classe di scienze fisiche, matematiche e naturali*, ser. 3, vol. 2 (Rome, 1878); Lowell, P. *Mars and Its Canals* (New York: Macmillan, 1906); Evans, J. E., and Maunder, E. W. “Experiments as to the Actuality of the ‘Canals’ Observed on Mars,” *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* 63, no. 8 (1903): 488–499; Leighton, R. B., Murray, B. C., Sharp, R. P., Allen, J. D., and Sloan, R. K. “Mariner IV Photography of Mars: Initial Results,” *Science* 149, no. 3684 (1965): 627–630; Barnard, E. ## Questions

The guiding question—**“what is this?”**—along the axes of the [[Question#The Question and the Domains|question]]: a completing goal of the category of essence; the stage of **construction**: the domain builds categories and definitions, and the step is closed by the constructed concept. And a special work of the domain: **here the goals of future questions are formed**—the passage of a motive into a question is the formation of a goal, and it is accomplished by clarifying the category of what is sought.

The working questions of the domain—along the two directions of clarification.

- **Essence**—“what is this?”—the guiding question itself.

**Inward questions**—what makes *A* = *A*:

- **Constitution**—“what makes *A* what it is?”—that without which *A* would cease to be itself;
- **Arrangement**—“how is *A* arranged?”—what *A* consists of, which systems are nested in it;
- **Manifestations**—“what are the manifestations of *A*?”—how the characteristics of *A* are manifested: the elements in their roles; the inner branch of the cycle, a request to recognition.

**Outward questions**—boundary and interaction:

- **Neighborhood**—“what stands next to *A*?”—what is next to *A* but is not *A*; the outer branch of the cycle, a request to recognition;
- **Boundary**—“how does *A* differ from what is similar?”;
- **Links**—“how does *A* interact with other systems?”;
- **Meaning**—“what does *A* mean in this context?”—the sense relative to the system in which *A* stands.

**Questions of the course**—they serve the path of the domain:

- **The goal of what is sought**—“of what category is what is sought—has the goal of the question been formed?”—the formation of the goals of future questions;
- **Stopping**—“has it been clarified enough to rest on?”—the Principle of the Root, Sufficient Reason.

The transition—by the [[#Grounds for Transition|grounds for transition]]: the goal is formed and the concept bears weight—next comes [[3 Framework Selection|framework selection]].

## Summary

1. **Clarification is movement from name to essence, from word to understanding;** the guiding question is “what is this?”; here the goals of future questions are formed.
2. **Recognition fixes the difference *A* / not-*A*; clarification fixes what makes *A* = *A*:** the essence is the constitution; at the output—a concept.
3. **Two directions:** inward—the arrangement and constitution of *A*; outward—the boundary with what is similar and the links with other systems.
4. **The Principle of the Root:** dig down to a support; stopping is by sufficient reason relative to the goal.
5. **The chief enemy is the illusion of understanding;** the defense is a check: to explain, to apply, to meet a counterexample.
6. **The fallacies of the domain—group 2.2 of the catalog, 13 records:** meaning drift (the most populated), hidden agent, incomplete analysis, excessive clarification.
7. **One passes to framework selection when the terms are defined explicitly and do not change their meaning, hidden assumptions are named, and the goal of the question is formed.**
