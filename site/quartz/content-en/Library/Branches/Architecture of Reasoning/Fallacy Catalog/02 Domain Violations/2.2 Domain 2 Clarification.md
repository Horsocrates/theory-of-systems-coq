**Group 2.2** · [[02 Domain Violations|Domain Violations (Type 2)]]

Insufficient clarity of concepts and terms.

**Principle:** the Principle of the Root.

<!-- PROSE:BEGIN -->
## Why the Domain Is Vulnerable

The fallacies of clarification are insidious because they disguise themselves as understanding: the word is familiar, so it is understood; the term is in use, so it is defined; the question has been asked, so it is clear. And the more habitual the word, the more dangerous it is. We do not clarify what seems understood—and what seems understood is precisely what we use most often. “Freedom,” “justice,” “truth” are uttered from childhood and are therefore the least clarified of all: a rare technical term we make precise willingly, for we know that we do not know it, while a habitual word does not raise even a shadow of doubt. Socrates showed this once and for all: Athenian generals had spoken of courage all their lives and could not say what it is (Plato, *Laches*, 190d–194c). In a dialogue the trouble doubles: each puts their own sense into the word and supposes that the interlocutor’s sense is the same—and the dispute passes the other by, in parallel monologues in which each refutes a position the other did not hold. Clarification, meanwhile, requires work—to stop, to ask, to check understanding—and this slow work is easy to mistake for pedantry and skip.

## The Unity of the Fallacies of Clarification

The fallacies of the second domain are different ways of leaving the conceptual foundation unfit: to let a meaning drift, to leave it indeterminate, to cut short the exploration of possibilities, to hide the conclusion in a term. One thing unites them: reasoning moves on although the words it rests on have not yet been clarified—they only seem clarified because they are habitual. “Canal”—what is unclear about that? And for eighty years people search for Martians. It is the Principle of the Root that decides whether a concept is sufficiently defined, however habitual the word: if equivocation is possible, it is not sufficiently defined; if the space of possibilities has not been explored, it is not sufficiently defined; if the definition prejudges the conclusion, this is not clarification but disguise.

The work of the domain, an analysis of frequent fallacies (equivocation, ambiguity, false dilemma, the question-begging definition), and the cases “Gene” and “The Martian Canals” are on the domain page [[2 Clarification|Clarification]].

Basis: the chapter “Violations of Clarification” in *Architecture of Reasoning*, vol. 2.
