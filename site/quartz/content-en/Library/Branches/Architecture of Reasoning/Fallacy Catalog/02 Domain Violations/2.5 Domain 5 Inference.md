**Group 2.5** · [[02 Domain Violations|Domain Violations (Type 2)]]

The conclusion exceeds its ground.

**Principle:** the Principle of Rationalism.

<!-- PROSE:BEGIN -->
## Why the Domain Is Vulnerable

The danger of inference is that it *seems* to follow—and that is enough: we trust the feeling without checking the structure. When two things are linked in the mind by association or by closeness in time, the passage from one to the other feels natural—“after this, therefore because of this” describes the work of intuition rather than logic. The feeling of following is not following; telling one from the other takes an effort that we rarely make—all the more so under impatience with incompleteness and the pressure of expectations. The fallacies of this domain are of two kinds: *formal* ones violate the very structure of inference (confusing a sufficient condition with a necessary one), *substantive* ones keep the form but exceed the ground.

## The Unity of the Fallacies of Inference

The fallacies of the fifth domain are different ways of failing to earn a conclusion: to affirm the consequent, to deny the antecedent, to take “after” for “because of,” to build a chain without justifying its links, to inflate the particular into the universal. One thing unites them: the conclusion claims more than the ground has given. The Principle of Rationalism does not stand in the way of conclusions—it requires that they be grounded: a conclusion must follow from what has been established, not exceed it, and know its limits. Self-evidence is not a criterion of absolute truth; groundedness is.

The work of the domain, an analysis of frequent fallacies (affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, “after this, therefore because of this,” the slippery slope, overgeneralization), and the cases “Neptune” and “John Snow” are on the domain page [[5 Inference|Inference]].
