**Group 2.1** · [[02 Domain Violations|Domain Violations (Type 2)]]

Distortion of what is perceived as the object of reasoning.

**Principle:** the Principle of Presence.

<!-- PROSE:BEGIN -->
## Why the Domain Is Vulnerable

Consciousness does not perceive passively—it directs attention, filters, and completes; without this work we would drown in a stream of raw data, but this same work opens the way to error. Between reality and its perception stands a filter ([[1 Recognition|Perception]]): the biological layer sets a limit to what is accessible to the senses; the cultural layer gives the categories into which we divide what we see; the philosophical layer gives the deep assumptions about what counts as real; the personal layer, built up from one’s own history, makes one thing hypervisible and another invisible. Over them emotion works like weather: fear sharpens attention to threats, desire makes one see what one wants to see. And where data are lacking, the brain does not leave the gap empty—it completes it with expectation, with a habitual scheme, and passes off what it has completed as what it has seen. Recognition proceeds quickly and automatically; checking it is a separate, slow work for which there is neither time nor a signal, since the habitual does not seem to be a problem. Here lies the root of the danger: an error of recognition is invisible precisely because it is committed before we begin to think about what we see.

## The Unity of the Fallacies of Recognition

The fallacies of the first domain are different ways of distorting the material at the input: to deform the object, to substitute it, to sift the data, to project what is expected, to distort the source—the five mechanisms of the group; errors in the very posing of the question strike earlier than the domain—group [[1.A Question Framing Errors|1.A]]. One thing unites them: the distortion enters *before* conscious reasoning and is therefore invisible from within. Everything that follows may be impeccable—and still lead to a false result, because it works with distorted material. Protection requires a special kind of attention—attention to attention itself: what exactly have I recognized? is it what is there—or what I expected to see? what am I answering—the argument or the person?

The work of the domain, an analysis of frequent fallacies (substitution of the thesis, selective presentation, ad hominem), and the cases “The Dress,” “The Invisible Gorilla,” “Warm—Cold,” and “Polywater” are on the domain page [[1 Recognition|Recognition]].
