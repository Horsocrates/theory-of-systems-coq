## The Nature of Order Violations

The [[Order|Law of Order]] requires that the [[Domains|domains]] be passed in sequence—recognition, clarification, framework selection, comparison, inference, reflection—and each rests on the completeness of the preceding ones. Among all fallacies, violations of this order stand apart: where other errors spoil the work of a single domain, these undermine the very structure in which the domains work. A domain may be passed flawlessly and still lead to error if it is passed out of its place. A brilliant analysis is useless if what is analyzed is a projection rather than what has been recognized; a precise comparison is meaningless if the framework was not chosen consciously; a logically clean inference is false if it is drawn before the comparison has been made. A failure at the beginning cascades through everything that follows it; a premature conclusion turns the preceding work into rationalization.

**Why we are vulnerable.** The vulnerability is no accident—it is woven into the way we think. Fast thinking constantly offers shortcuts: on seeing a familiar pattern, the mind instantly produces an answer, and a conscious effort is needed to go back and check whether the order has been kept. Impatience hurries us: the conclusion is needed now, and the desire for an answer outruns the readiness to earn it. Emotional investment turns the sequence upside down: when we *want* a particular conclusion, it becomes the starting point, and reasoning becomes a search for confirmation; and most often we sincerely believe that we are following logic, while logic is following desire. Group pressure adds its share: another’s conclusion is accepted without passing through the domains oneself, for the sake of social comfort. Each of these factors is adaptive on its own; together they create a steady pull toward violating the order. Defense therefore requires not good will but knowledge of the concrete forms the violation takes. There are eight forms—premature transition, reverse movement, the skipped stage, mixing, object substitution in the course, burden inversion, stalling, blocked return; all of them are treated on the index of the branch: [[Architecture of Reasoning#Movement through the Domains|“Movement through the Domains.”]]

## Fallacies as Violations of Order

Each form has fallacies of its own.

**[[Overgeneralization|Hasty generalization]]**—a premature transition from comparison to inference: a general conclusion is drawn from a handful of cases. “Two rude Frenchmen—so the French are rude”; “this company cheated me—so the whole industry is a pack of crooks.” The comparison is not complete—not all relevant cases have been considered, the sample has not been checked for representativeness—but the conclusion has already been drawn, as if the work were finished. The part is passed off as the whole. Check: are there enough cases? Is the sample representative? Which cases have been left out?

**[[Circular Reasoning|The vicious circle]]**—reverse movement: the conclusion is already contained in the premise, and the argument presupposes what it is supposed to prove. “He is a reliable person because he is honest”—the illusion of an argument made by renaming the same thing. “Democracy is the best form of government because the people should make the decisions”—but why should they? Because democracy is the best. The circle is closed; there is no way out to an independent ground. Check: can the premise be accepted without already accepting the conclusion?

**The Texas sharpshooter**—reverse movement in framework selection. The name comes from a shooter who first fires at the side of a barn and then draws a target around the holes: the criterion of significance is chosen *after* the result is known. A researcher who has not found the expected connection keeps splitting the data until a subgroup turns up in which the connection holds—and announces it as a discovery. An analyst advertises the forecasts that came true and keeps silent about the failed ones. The target is drawn around the holes. In the catalog the same work is shared by [[Half Truth|card stacking]] (selecting data to fit the conclusion) and the syndrome of [[Confirmation Bias|confirmation bias]]. Check: was the criterion set *before* the data became known?

**The halo effect**—a mixing of recognition and evaluation: one quality colors the perception of all the others, and operations that ought to go in turn happen at once. An attractive person is automatically taken to be smarter, kinder, more competent; a famous scientist, an authority in any field. The framework of evaluation was set by the first impression and spread to what has no connection with it. In the catalog the halo is recorded as associative transfer: [[Transfer]] is the positive halo, [[Guilt by Association]] the negative one. Check: on what basis am I evaluating this quality—has an impression been carried over from something else?

**[[Red Herring|The red herring]]**—object substitution: reasoning begins about one subject and imperceptibly switches to another. “You say the politician broke the law—but look how much he has done for veterans!”: the question about the law has been replaced by a question about merits. The subsequent domains are passed conscientiously—but already about another subject, and the conclusion is drawn about something other than what was under discussion. Check: is the discussion still about what it was about at the start?

**The appeal to authority or to emotion**—skipping reasoning itself: instead of passing through the domains, a ready-made answer is taken. “X said so” replaces “X is true”; “I feel” replaces “this is justified.” The distinction matters: an expert’s opinion and intuition are relevant evidence, but evidence is not proof. Authority says “this is worth a closer look,” not “here you can stop thinking.” In the catalog the branch of authority is carried by [[Star Power|star power]] and [[Blind Loyalty|blind loyalty]], the emotional branch by [[Trust Your Gut Emotional Reasoning|emotional reasoning]]. Check: has the reasoning been carried out on the merits—or has the conclusion been accepted because of its source?

**[[Non Sequitur|Non sequitur]]**—a skipped connection: between premise and conclusion there is no logical bridge, and reasoning leaps over the gap without noticing it. “He wears an expensive watch—so he is a successful businessman.” The transition is made only because the conclusion comes after the premise in time, not because it follows from it in substance. Check: can this connection be made explicit? If it cannot, it probably does not exist.

> [!example]- Case: “Tunnel Vision”—a violation on an institutional scale
> The same failures, once they become systematic, come to cost human lives. *Tunnel vision* in criminal justice is reverse movement raised to an institutional norm: instead of “gather evidence → name a suspect → test the hypothesis → conclude guilt,” the sequence is inverted—“choose a suspect → look for evidence of his guilt → interpret what is found as confirmation.” The conclusion determines the premises.
>
> On July 15, 1992, Rachel Nickell, twenty-three years old, was killed on Wimbledon Common in front of her two-year-old son. The crime shocked Britain, the press demanded results, and under this pressure the investigation slid into inversion. Colin Stagg, who had been walking in the same park that same morning, “fit the psychological profile” of the killer—solitary, withdrawn—although there was not a single piece of physical evidence against him. Instead of admitting that “the data are insufficient,” the police *designated* Stagg the culprit, and the task became proving Stagg’s guilt rather than finding the killer. For five months an undercover policewoman built a sham romance with him, trying to draw out a confession; when she prompted him outright—“if only you had killed her, it would be all right”—Stagg replied: “I’m terribly sorry, but I haven’t.”
>
> Every step was a violation. The profile was *fitted* to Stagg after he had become a suspect—reverse movement. His fantasies were interpreted as evidence, with the framework chosen to fit an already known result—the Texas sharpshooter. The profiler’s opinion was taken for proof—skipping reasoning. And the real killer, Robert Napper, had already come to the attention of the police—but the alternative hypothesis was not considered, because the conclusion had been drawn. In 1994 the judge threw out the case, finding “deceptive conduct of the grossest kind”; Stagg received an apology and compensation only in 2008, sixteen years after the killing. And while the police were pursuing Stagg, Napper, still at large, killed two more people (1993).
>
> Tunnel vision persists for understandable reasons: pressure demands a result; confirmation bias notices what confirms and fails to see what refutes; invested resources and reputation make retreat unbearable. This is a structural violation of sequence—neither a moral failure nor stupidity—and it can be diagnosed and prevented. Institutional counterweights—a “devil’s advocate,” checklists of alternative hypotheses, blind expert review—derive from a single understanding: reasoning has an architecture, and that architecture can be violated.
>
> Sources: IPCC report (2010); R v Colin Stagg, Central Criminal Court, 1994—ruling of Justice Ognall, September 14, 1994 (“deceptive conduct of the grossest kind”); the exchange on the undercover tapes as quoted in the press (N. Cohen, *The Guardian*, June 25, 2006); Robert Napper was convicted of Nickell’s killing in 2008 thanks to advances in DNA analysis.

## The Unity of the Violations

The fallacies of this type are different ways of breaking one order: the vicious circle and the Texas sharpshooter go backward from the conclusion; the non sequitur and the appeal skip a link; the halo effect mixes domains; the red herring substitutes the object. All of them *look* like reasoning—with their own vocabulary, structure, and appearance of justification. Their defect is structural rather than one of content: correct operations in the wrong order. That is why they are so tenacious and so contagious: the form is observed, and an eye that seeks the error in the content does not find it.
