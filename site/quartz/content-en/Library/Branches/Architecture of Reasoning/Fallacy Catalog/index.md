**A fallacy is a violation of the architecture of reasoning.** The structural approach measures fallacies against the architecture of right reasoning—the laws of [[Logic|logic]] and the six [[Domains|domains]]—and yields three results: fallacies are **locatable** (the diagnosis shows *where* in the architecture the failure occurred), **explicable** (each one traces back to the violation of a specific principle), and the classification is **complete** (the five types correspond to the structural features of reasoning as such). Every fallacy in the catalog carries an address in the architecture, a formulation, a mechanism of the violation, an example, and a diagnostic question.

## The Nature of Fallacies

**The catalog and its walls.** Fallacies have been collected for as long as logic itself. The first list was given by Aristotle: in the treatise *On Sophistical Refutations* he analyzed thirteen kinds of false arguments.[^1] Behind the list stood a sound intuition: a sophism is the *appearance* of a refutation—counterfeit gold that the inexperienced cannot tell from the real thing, while one who knows the nature of the genuine recognizes it at once. Hence, to recognize errors one must understand health—how right reasoning is built.

But the catalog did not provide such an architecture—it only accumulated cases. Medieval logicians expanded the list. Francis Bacon noticed that distortion enters before proof and called such distortions the “idols of the mind.”[^2] In the twentieth century Charles Hamblin judged the learned tradition of fallacies to be a wastebasket—a collection of examples without a unifying theory.[^3] Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky described the systematic deviations of the mind—heuristics and biases.[^4] By the end of this path the lists ran to hundreds of names—and came up against three walls.

*Taxonomy*: classifications multiply and compete, because there is no single basis of division. *Surface*: definitions describe the form of an error, not its source—the symptom is recognized, not the disease. *Isolation*: each fallacy is studied separately, while behind the variety of forms only a few mechanisms can be discerned.

**The architectural turn.** All three walls fall once the question is changed: not “what kinds of errors are there?” but “*what is violated* when reasoning goes astray?” The answer is given by the architecture of the six [[Domains|domains]]: thought moves toward absolute truth through recognition, clarification, framework selection, comparison, inference, and reflection; this order is the Principle of Sequence, a facet of the [[Order|Law of Order]]. Fallacies are precisely violations of this architecture: [[Ad Hominem Argument]] is more than a “dishonest trick”—it is a failure of the first domain (the object is substituted at the input); meaning drift is a failure of the second (the term is not fixed); [[False Analogy]] is a failure of the third (a framework is stretched over a phenomenon foreign to it). One principle of classification—by the violated place in the architecture; one source—a specific failure at a specific step; one kinship—a common mechanism. The three walls are removed at once. All these fallacies violate the *horizontal*—the sequence of the domains; violations of the second axis, the vertical of levels, give rise to [[Paradox Catalog|paradoxes]] rather than fallacies.

**Error and sophism.** *An error* is an unintended failure: a person wanted to reason rightly and did not manage to. *A sophism* is an intended one: the same architecture is violated deliberately, for the sake of victory. The mechanism is one and the same, and the architecture does not judge the motive—it records the structure; that is why the catalog does not separate the error from the sophism: they have one anatomy, and only the will of the one who commits the violation tells them apart (how an understanding of the architecture disarms a deliberate sophism is shown in the [[Architecture of Reasoning]]). And one last boundary: an error of the *architecture* is corrected by knowledge of the architecture; an error of the *input data*—the architecture is observed, but the information is wrong—is not cured by form: impeccable reasoning on a false premise yields a false conclusion. A question helps to tell them apart: could I have avoided the error with due attention?

**A single method.** For any fallacy, known or new: *localization*—go through the domains and find where the failure began; *mechanism*—how exactly the domain was violated: skipped, passed carelessly, distorted by pressure, replaced by a surrogate; *correction*—return to the point of failure and pass through the domain anew ([[Architecture of Reasoning|Navigation]]). The method does not require remembering hundreds of names—it requires seeing the structure. Knowledge makes an error visible; the will to absolute truth makes it unbearable. Both are needed.

## The Five Types

| Type | What is violated | Page | Fallacies |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Violations of Conditions | Reasoning does not begin—its place is taken by manipulation or a defective question | [[01 Violations of Conditions\|open]] | 38 |
| 2. Domain Violations | Reasoning proceeds but fails within a specific domain | [[02 Domain Violations\|open]] | 105 |
| 3. Violations of Sequence | Reasoning moves backward, in a circle, or with inverted roles | [[03 Sequence Violations\|open]] | 3 |
| 4. Syndromes | Systemic distortions of the whole process | [[04 Syndromes\|open]] | 6 |
| 5. Context-Dependent Methods | Methods valid in some contexts and fallacious in others | [[05 Context Methods\|open]] | 6 |

In all, the catalog counts 158 fallacies. **The core of the catalog is Type 2**: its 105 fallacies are distributed across the [[Domains|domains]] unevenly, and the unevenness itself speaks of the structure:

| Domain | Fallacies | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Recognition | 26 | foundational: errors propagate further |
| 2. Clarification | 13 | meaning is constrained |
| 3. Framework Selection | 16 | there are many criteria |
| 4. Comparison | 8 | the operation is the most constrained |
| 5. Inference | 20 | there are many ways to exceed the grounds |
| 6. Reflection | 22 | self-criticism is difficult |

How each domain fails: 
Recognition—by distorting *what is perceived* (deformation and substitution of the object—[[Ad Hominem Argument|ad hominem]]: the argument is replaced by the one arguing; data filtering, projection); 
Clarification—by distorting *meanings* (meaning drift—[[Equivocation|equivocation]], the hidden agent, false dilemma); 
Framework Selection—by distorting *the measure* (irrelevant criteria—“everyone thinks so,” the measurability fallacy); 
Comparison—by distorting *relations* (false equation, unstable criteria); Inference—by distorting *what follows* (non sequitur, [[Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc|post hoc]]—causality inferred from a temporal sequence alone, overreach); 
Reflection—by distorting *the limits* (the illusion of completion, errors of self-assessment). 

The maxima belong to Recognition and Reflection, and the two are structurally linked: reflection provides the “observer above the process,” without which the errors of recognition are invisible; the weakness of the sixth domain is part of the invisibility of the errors of the first.

The small count of Type 3 is natural: there are many ways to go wrong but few directions; the three primary patterns are [[A Priori Argument|rationalization]] (movement from a ready conclusion back to “grounds”), [[Circular Reasoning|circular reasoning]] (the conclusion among the premises), and [[Shifting the Burden of Proof|shifting the burden of proof]]. 

Syndromes (Type 4) are patterns that pervade the whole process rather than local errors: pervasiveness across domains, self-reinforcement, invisibility to the one affected (the distortion is experienced as clear thinking); six syndromes in two groups—cognitive dissonance ([[Confirmation Bias|confirmation bias]], [[Two Truths|compartmentalization]], [[Motivated Reasoning|motivated reasoning]]) and systemic distortion ([[Echo Chamber|echo chamber]], [[Cognitive Closure|cognitive closure]], [[Groupthink|groupthink]]); a syndrome is treated by working with the pattern rather than by correcting a single error—seek refutation, separate the person from the belief, diversify sources, reward disagreement. 

Type 5 consists, by definition, of *methods* rather than errors—methods valid under their own conditions: expert intuition is reliable in a field with fast feedback; tradition encodes surviving wisdom (Chesterton’s fence: before you tear it down, understand why it was put up); the argument from silence works when silence is expectedly informative; a method becomes a fallacy outside its conditions—the task of the type is to discern the conditions rather than to rule the method out.

**In ethics.** Type 1 is the ethical boundary of reasoning: manipulation takes the place of argument, that is, a refusal to reason is presented as reasoning. Rationalization (Type 3)—the selection of grounds to fit a ready conclusion—creates a lie ([[Order#In Ethics|Order, the ethical slice]]). Syndromes are dangerous precisely through their invisibility: the distortion is experienced as clarity—which is why honesty requires external supports: seek refutation, listen to those who disagree.
