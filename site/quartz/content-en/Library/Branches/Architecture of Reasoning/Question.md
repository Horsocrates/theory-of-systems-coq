Philosophy begins with a question, not with wonder—and so does reasoning: each domain is an application of a question, each has its own guiding question, and the path from “what is here?” to “how did I reason about this?” is a consecutive questioning. Before going through the domains, it is worth analyzing the instrument itself.

## Etymology

**Question** ← Lat. *quaestio* ← *quaerere* “to seek; to ask”: to ask is to seek, and the axis of the English word is **seeking**. The Russian word *vopros* is built otherwise: *vo-* + *prosit’* “to request,” a question as a petition—one asks not for a thing but for the missing knowledge of it; its cognates are *pros’ba* “request,” *spros* “demand,” *voproshanie* “questioning.” The same root gave Sanskrit *praśná-* “question” and German *fragen* “to ask”: in it, to request and to ask are one.[^1] The axis of the Russian word is **petition**: to ask is to request knowledge where its edge is seen.

## Definition

**A question is a directed act: a request given form, which turns attention to a region of the field of possible knowledge.** From the side of the goal, a question is a method of focusing attention in order to widen understanding. A request is wider than a question: a question sets a definite framework, while a request leaves the field open, only pointing to the will to receive an answer ([[Intuition]]).

A gap in knowledge is passive—it is unfilledness; a question is an act, a turning of attention. The three zones of knowledge (section “The Question and Knowledge”) also make Meno’s paradox impossible—“one can seek neither what one knows nor what one does not know” (our translation):[^2] what is sought lies in the second zone—“I know that I do not know”—which the dilemma missed.

The composition of a directed act is the composition of every act of [[Will|will]]: the **goal** (what is sought), the **direction** (where to), the **ground** (where from), the **path** (by what course). Questioning inherits this structure because it is itself a formed act of directing attention. To ask a question is to make a choice: *which* question to pose. A question differs from observation by the position of its goal, not by having one—every directed attention has a goal: in observation the goal is immanent, reached in the act itself; in a question the goal is deferred—closure requires a path. That is why a question has a path and a cycle; observation closes in the act itself.

## The Question and Knowledge

Everything knowable is connected into one **field**: an object stands among objects, a tier rests on a tier, and their relations themselves are also knowable ([[Epistemology#Field, Slice, Boundary|field, slice, boundary]]). What belongs to the knower at each moment is a **slice**—the knower’s subjective field: the part that has already passed the ladder up to a record. The slice has a **boundary**; relative to the slice the field divides into three zones:

- **I know**—the record is made: this is the slice itself; one does not ask about what is resolved;
- **I know that I do not know**—the gap itself is recorded: the object is distinguished, a question is possible, but the content is not there;
- **I do not know that I do not know**—neither a record nor a record of the gap; from inside the slice this zone is not seen at all.

A question is read from this boundary. Completing questioning is a pointing from the first zone into the second: the object is already distinguished—otherwise the question has no direction; the content is not yet there—otherwise there is nothing to ask about; the second zone is the only one where not-knowing is already distinguished and a bond from what is recorded already exists. Questions of status and of choice work within the slice itself: their material is known, and what remains a distinguished gap is only the status or the decision not yet made. No goal points into the third zone—there is nothing there yet to take into work.

The boundary also sets a limit to the very method of knowing: one can come to know only that to which there is a bond from what is already recorded—a new record is built into the earlier ones, understanding is built on what is understood. That is why movement goes through the second zone, while the third opens otherwise—by the growth of the slice and by inflow through the channels, perception and insight ([[Intuition]]): what one did not know one did not know becomes first a distinguished gap and only then knowledge. Hence questioning is a process. And the process is not hopeless: a correctly posed question has an answer. The answer itself and its content are different things: the existence of the answer depends on the right construction of the question, while its content may be negative—“there is no such thing”—or “unknown”: to the question “will it rain on this day a year from now?” no one can say for certain now, but there is an answer—“unknown.” There is no completed corpus of all answers ([[Formalization]]).

## The Structure of a Question

The posing of a question has three parts, and the three-part structure follows from the definition itself: a directed act necessarily has *what will close it*, a place *where* it is directed, and a place *from which*:

- **Goal**—the form of what is sought: what will *count* as an answer; reaching the goal—the answer—closes the act; without a goal, what arrives cannot be recognized as an answer;
- **Direction**—the region taken into work: “a question about everything” is not a question ([[Identity]]);
- **Ground**—the already-known from which the question follows: a pointing from nowhere points nowhere ([[Sufficient Reason]]).

The path does not enter into the posing of a question: it opens when the three parts are complete—it is the cycle from posing to answer.

This is the triad of the [[Theory of Systems]] on the question itself, starting from the goal: the goal is the ground of the whole act; the rule of the question is the assignment of the status “answer” by the goal; the direction is the role: which region is taken into work; the ground is the elements: the already-known from which the question follows. The order of construction holds here too: first the goal—the root question points to *what* is sought; the pointing is definite only given the goal; the known becomes a ground only under an operative pointing. This order is constitutive; the temporal one is free: an anomaly in the known may come first, but it becomes a question only when all three parts are closed.

**The ground is two-faced**—by the law of Sufficient Reason a correct question has two faces of its ground. The **content ground** is part of the composition: there is a base—a framework and something to turn to—and there is an open distinction: something genuinely unresolved. The **active ground** is why the act was performed: there is something with which to turn one’s gaze, and something has drawn attention; if strongly enough—it has aroused interest and fixed the boundaries of the region: it is marked as “I do not know” and set apart from the unknown unknown. The faces are independent: the composition may be sound without the act being performed, and a motive may push a corrupted composition.

Reachability does not enter into the ground: to pose a question, one need not see the road to the answer—it is enough to understand that there is a field of not-knowing. **Pointing is not access:** a correctly posed question has an answer, while what is sought may be inaccessible now—then the answer is “unknown” (section “The Question and Knowledge”).

In terms of the [[Mental Field|two fields]]: the ground stands in the subjective field, the direction takes a region into work—in the known or at its boundary, the goal sets the rule of the future transition. **A question is a request given form—for a transition from the objective field into the subjective one:** data lie in the objective field, and information comes to the witness through the channels.

The anatomy makes the [[1.A Question Framing Errors|defects of posing]] transparent—each is a corruption of one of the parts: the built-in assumption is a false *ground*; merged questions are a doubled *direction*; the exclusion of topics and the exclusion by context are a truncated *goal*. The anatomy also predicted a fifth defect, which the classical lists of fallacies did not single out: the [[Impossible Goal|**impossible goal**]]—a goal that outlines what cannot be in the field (“name all N”—a demand for the completed corpus of a process). The completeness of the classification by structure is proved ([[Formalization]]).

## Kinds of Goal and the Path to an Answer

A goal is of three kinds—by the measure to which what is sought is already given:

| Goal                                  | Given          | Lacking            | Closing act                          |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| **Polar** (yes/no)                    | Both sides     | Status             | Ascertainment—the witness            |
| **Alternative** (which?)              | Pool A1…An     | One-of             | Choice—the will                      |
| **Completing** (what? why? how?)       | Only a category | What is sought itself | Completing—inflow or construction |

There is no fourth kind: without a category there is no goal—the question is not formed. The direction stands where the goal points: for the polar and the alternative goal—**within the known itself**: the pair of sides or the pool of candidates is known material, and what is lacking is only the status or the choice (“where should I go on vacation?” works with a known list); for the completing goal—**at the boundary of the known**, in the second zone. The pool may also be not given: in framework selection its construction is the first step (section “The Question and the Domains”). Hence a division deeper than grammar: the polar question is a question of **fact** (closed by the witness), the alternative one a question of **decision** (what is lacking is a choice, not knowledge—closed by the will), the completing one a question of **content** (closed by inflow or construction). The pull of goals toward the operations of the [[Mental Field|field]] is determined by the closing act: “is there life on Mars” is polar by its goal, although the path to the answer is long reasoning.

A full act of cognition unfolds the completing goal into a **path to an answer**: completing—building a pool; choice—singling out a candidate; ascertainment—witnessing the correspondence to the goal. The scientific method is this path word for word: “what causes X?”, then hypotheses, then “is H₃ true?”. The path is begun by the root question—it is what says *what* is sought; the path also binds the intermediate questions into one act of cognition. A single question is a truncated path, classified by its starting stage; a pool may consist of one element (intuition begins, discursive thought checks), the path may be iterated (returns), and at the stage of construction an honest stasis is possible: pointing is not access. The invariant is one: **every answer that has taken place is closed by a polar check against the goal**—at the end the goal works as a rule of [[Ethics|evaluation]].

## The Ladder of Questions

The questions of a connected act of cognition stand on three tiers:

- **The target question** is the question of the whole action: what it leads toward; its goal is the essence. The **depth** of a question is the distance from it to the goal, measured in tiers of the root: a deep question requires a long path, a shallow one a short path. The distance may exceed the current depth of the slice—then the path honestly includes its growth; but the path is passed step by step, tier by tier, without leaps;

- **Process questions** open the cycles of work in the domains and direct them. The **root** question takes a step down along the root: every answer has a question—and a chain of questions, a path—that leads to it; this is the root question. **Movement along the root is movement along logic**: all systems are connected in the common field, and a path from any point to any other exists—a logical chain; the root is not invented but found: it is the optimal one among the existing paths. The **context** question asks about the systems that influence the system where the essence is sought: an inspection of the node’s connections—outward (enclosing systems, up to the system of rules itself), inward (nested ones), sideways (neighbors on the level)—in order to determine the next root step;

- **Domain questions** are within the cycle of each step: the guiding questions of the six [[Domains|domains]], from “what is here?” to “how did I reason about this?”.

And every question—of any tier—**itself bounds its working field** (the goal and the direction cut out a region) and **carries its own cycle**: the process of the path from posing to answer. Questions within questions are fields within fields, cycles within cycles.

The asymmetry of the road: **outward the path is finite**—the chain of grounds runs into the first ground, and this is not the last object but the first law: the law of [[Sufficient Reason]] itself; **inward and sideways the field is open**—the generation of nested systems is not completed, and it is precisely there that the [[Impossible Goal|impossible goal]] lives: there is no “complete corpus.” Connectedness, the ladder, duality, and asymmetry are proved ([[Formalization]]).

> [!example]- Case: Agrippa’s trilemma—the regress ends in a law
> The ancient skeptic Agrippa, known through Diogenes Laertius, reduced the skeptics’ arguments to five modes; three of them—the infinite regress of grounds, the circle, and the arbitrary assumption—were later called a trilemma.[^3] The structure of the field of the knowable closes the trilemma with an exit that it counted as arbitrary: the chain of “why?” is finite because the hierarchy of systems has a summit—the system of rules—and within it stands the first ground: the law of Sufficient Reason itself, the meta-law that gives grounds to all the others. The answer to the ultimate “why?” is “by Sufficient Reason,” and this law is self-grounded: the regress ends in the law of ground. This is not a circle: a circle derives a claim from itself, whereas Sufficient Reason is derived from nothing—every derivation presupposes it; one can deny it only by presenting a ground for the denial, that is, by applying it ([[Sufficient Reason#The Regress of Grounds and the Trilemma|“The Regress of Grounds and the Trilemma”]]). Schopenhauer gave the same argument: a proof is the presentation of a ground, so the demand to prove Sufficient Reason itself already rests on it, and it is the one who demands who falls into the circle—“the circle of exacting a proof of our right to exact a proof.”[^5] Aristotle defended the law of non-contradiction by the same move: a direct proof of the first principle would beg the question, while a refutation would not: the one who denies it, in doing away with speech, holds to speech.[^6] Proved: the self-grounded summit is unique, and the ascending path of every system is finite ([[Formalization]]).

> [!example]- Case: Collingwood—the duality of question and answer
> Robin George Collingwood insisted that meaning, truth, and falsity belong to a complex of questions and answers, not to separate propositions; a proposition torn from its question cannot even be evaluated.[^4] In the structure of questioning this position becomes a duality with two proved sides: a correctly posed question has an answer—and every answer has a root question and a path that lead to it. An answer whose root question and path cannot be restored is an assertion without an address. This is what the sophist makes use of: the sophist’s formulations hide the logic—the existing root—and are therefore uncheckable; an honest answer shows its root. Proved: everything that is an answer carries a root ([[Formalization]]).

## A Map of Question Words

The categories of direction—what is asked about—language has typed itself, by question words:

| Category of direction          | Words                       | Layer of the system             |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Essence · Element              | Who? What?                  | Elements (Identity)             |
| Quality · Measure              | What kind? How much?        | Hierarchy of recognition        |
| Choice from a pool · Belonging | Which? Whose?               | Pool of candidates              |
| Place · Time                   | Where? Whither? When?       | Order                           |
| Goal                           | What for?                   | Roles (Sufficient Reason)       |
| Ground                         | Why? From what?             | Goal—the ground of the system (Sufficient Reason) |
| Manner                         | How?                        | Rules in action (Order)         |
| Limit                          | How far? Under what conditions? | Meta-level                  |
| Resolution                     | The particle *li*           | Polar goal: *A* / not-*A* (Non-Contradiction and Excluded Middle) |

A comparison across language lines—Indo-European, Semitic, Chinese—yields four conclusions:

- **One root—one act**: the whole Indo-European line builds its question words from one interrogative stem \*kʷo-/\*kʷi-—*who, what, where, when, how, which, whose* (Russian *kto, chto, gde, kogda, kak, kotoryi, chei*) are attires of one word; a question is one as an act of pointing, and only the categories of direction differ;

- **The set of categories is stable across language families**—the categories of direction are structural, not grammatical;

- **“Why” and “what for” are superstructures over “what”**: Russian *po-chemu / za-chem / ot-chego*—a preposition + a case of “what”; Sanskrit *kasmāt*—the ablative of “what”; Hebrew *lāmmā*—“to” + “what”; Chinese *为什么* (wèishénme)—“for the sake of” + “what”; Latin *quārē*—“by what thing,” an ablative; English *why*—a frozen instrumental of the interrogative stem. A second-order question is built by language as a case of the first-order question + a direction: toward the ground, after the goal, from the source;

- **The three goals are separated morphologically**: the completing goal is carried by pronouns; the polar one by a particle (*li*, Latin *-ne*, Chinese *吗* (ma)), by word order (English *Are you…?*), or by intonation alone; the alternative goal keeps the ancient suffix of choice-from-a-pair \*-tero-: Russian *kotoryi*, Latin *uter*, Greek *πότερος* (poteros), English *whether*—and *whether*, a word of choice, came to introduce polar questions: a polar question is a choice between two.

## The Question and the Domains

Each domain of reasoning is led by its own question, and the guiding questions are marked out along the axes of the anatomy:

| Domain                                 | Guiding question                   | Kind of goal                  | Category of direction                          | Stage of the path | Closure                 |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------------- |
| [[1 Recognition\|1. Recognition]]      | What is here?                      | Completing                     | The present                                    | Construction      | Inflow + ascertainment  |
| [[2 Clarification\|2. Clarification]]  | What is this?                      | Completing                     | Essence; **here the goals of questions form**  | Construction      | Concept                 |
| [[3 Framework Selection\|3. Framework Selection]] | Through which framework do I look? | **Alternative—a question of decision** | Which-from-the-pool             | Choice            | Will                    |
| [[4 Comparison\|4. Comparison]]        | How does this relate to that?      | Completing                     | Relation · measure                             | Choice            | Revealed relation       |
| [[5 Inference\|5. Inference]]          | What follows from this?            | Completing                     | Ground—**the root step**                       | Choice            | Extraction of the consequence |
| [[6 Reflection\|6. Reflection]]        | How did I reason about this?       | Polar + limit                 | The path itself (a change of object)           | **Ascertainment** | Witness                 |

In framework selection the pool is not given: determining its zone is the first step of the domain’s work, out of the whole potential field: determine what the criteria of selection should be, then determine what fits by the criterion, and only then take the chosen one into work. In inference the selection of hypotheses is a move within the domain; it is closed by the extraction of the consequence.

The cycle of domains is the path in miniature: domains of construction, of choice, and of ascertainment; a decision of the will enters reasoning exactly once—as the framework, while in comparison and inference choice goes by grounds; the root step is taken by inference, and the invariant of polar closure is performed by reflection. The detailed marking and the working questions are in the section “Questions” of each domain page. A question turns attention to one region at a time—that is why the domains are passed by work, one by one. By what does the witness choose what to cognize? By which question it poses: selection is questioning.

## Errors of Posing a Question

A broken question breaks reasoning before it begins—that is why in the [[Fallacy Catalog|catalog]] these failures are assigned to [[01 Violations of Conditions|violations of conditions (type 1)]], not to the domains: group [[1.A Question Framing Errors|1.A “Question Framing Errors”]]—dishonest questions that distort the conditions of reasoning before it begins. The principle of the group: **an honest question is directed toward truth and understanding**; what a question seeks is absolute truth. All five errors are analyzed:

**[[Loaded Question]]**—an assumption is built into the question itself, and any direct answer accepts it: “have you stopped deceiving your clients?”—both “yes” and “no” agree that there was deception. Check: what does the question take as given—and do I agree with it?

**[[Complex Question|Merged questions]]** (double-barreled question; in Aristotle’s words, in our translation, “making two questions one”)—several different questions are merged into one and demand a single answer: “did you like our service and our prices—yes or no?”—while the answers to the parts may diverge. Check: are different questions glued together here—and what would the answer to each be separately?

**[[Taboo|Exclusion of topics]]** (taboo)—topics or arguments are declared “sacred,” not subject to discussion: the field of questioning is narrowed to what was permitted in advance. Check: who closed the region to questions, and on what ground?

**[[Venue Fallacy|Exclusion by context]]**—a valid argument is rejected because of the “wrong” place or forum: the question is not examined but expelled. Check: am I answering what was said—or where it was said?

**[[Impossible Goal]]**—the goal of the question outlines the impossible: the completed corpus of an unbounded process or a contradictory category (“name all the prime numbers”); the ground and the direction are intact, the question looks correct, but in the form its goal demands it cannot be closed: the honest answer is “there is no such thing” or a process of generation. The only record of the group predicted by the anatomy of the three parts. Check: can what the goal demands exist—at least potentially? Remedy: a processual reformulation—“give the process of generation,” not “give everything.”

> [!example]- Case: “what is the largest number?”
> An impossible goal set without intent is not a corruption: here too an answer is possible. “What is the largest number?” is a deep question: it forces one to work out what a number really is, what its properties and characteristics are—and from there to understand how to ask it rightly. At least in the [[Mathematics|mathematics]] of this corpus everything consists of units: the unit—the unit of distinction—is both the smallest and the largest; then units are combined—and then a clarifying question is needed: what essence exactly does a number carry? Numbers not tied to what they are units of become illusory constructions without connection to reality. The question “how many distinctions are the most?” duplicates the question about number—and at once shows that without the clarification “distinctions of what exactly?” it is formulated incorrectly. And once it is understood that the question implies the quantity of distinctions, the answer comes too: there is no limit—and, consequently, there is no maximum of possible distinctions from which a number would be formed.

The five defects of the group are one mechanism, and it is volitional: **the choice of a question with an intention other than truth**. The same question asked out of not-knowing is an entrance to clarification, not a corruption (the case “what is the largest number?”). The corruption of the parts is a trace of a distorted intention: the loaded question imputes, bypassing the will of the other; merged questions force one choice where there are two; an imposed taboo is power over another’s field of questioning, a self-imposed one a walled-up gap; the evasion “not here and not now” judges circumstances instead of content; the impossible goal sets an unfulfillable demand or obtains an alibi of not-knowing. Some distortions are directed against the other, others against oneself; all are against truth. Hence the principle of the group becomes a conclusion: **honest questioning is movement along the root, that is, along logic, and therefore it is right**: the right (the rightful) is order, and order is an aspect of logic ([[Ethics]]). Sophistry is the hiding of logic behind formulations, a leading away from the root; turned against the will of another for one’s own ends, it is **evil at the entrance of reasoning**, turned toward oneself, harm.

## Summary

1. **A question is a directed act: a request given form, which turns attention to a region of the field of possible knowledge;** from the side of the goal, a method of focusing attention in order to widen understanding. A request is wider than a question: a question sets a framework.
2. **Three parts—goal, direction, ground;** starting from the goal: the goal is the ground of the act, the assignment of the status “answer” by the goal is the rule, the direction is the role, the ground is the elements. The path opens when the three parts are complete.
3. **The ground is two-faced:** the content ground—a base and an open distinction; the active ground—attention, interest, the fixed boundaries of the region. Reachability does not enter into the ground: pointing is not access.
4. **Three kinds of goal**—polar, alternative, completing: a question of fact, of decision, of content. Every answer that has taken place is closed by a polar check against the goal.
5. **The ladder of questions:** the target question, process questions—root and context—and domain questions. Movement along the root is movement along logic; outward the path ends in the first law—Sufficient Reason.
6. **An answer and its content are different:** a correctly posed question has an answer; its content may be negative, and it may be “unknown.”
7. **Five errors of posing**—a corruption of the goal, the direction, or the ground with an intention other than truth; honest questioning is right.
