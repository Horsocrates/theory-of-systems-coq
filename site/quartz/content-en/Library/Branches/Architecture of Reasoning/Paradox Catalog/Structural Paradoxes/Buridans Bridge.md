**S.8** · [[Structural Paradoxes|structural paradox]] · subtype: Semantic

**Essence of the error.** The truth-value of the condition depends on its execution

## Exact Formulation

Plato guards a bridge and swears: “I will let you pass if the first thing you say is true; if you lie, I will throw you into the water.” Socrates answers: “You will throw me into the water.” If Plato throws him—Socrates’ words are true, and by the oath he should have been let through. If Plato lets him pass—the words are false, and by the oath he should have been thrown. Any action breaks the oath.

## Origin

Jean Buridan, fourteenth century, *Sophismata*—the seventeenth sophism: scholasticism brought the ancient loop ([[Crocodile Dilemma|the crocodile]]) to a pure scene with Plato and Socrates and seriously analyzed *what the keeper of the bridge is obliged to do*. A second birth came in Cervantes (*Don Quixote*, pt. II, ch. 51; trans. J. Ormsby, 1885): Sancho Panza, governor of the island, judges a traveler who, before a bridge with a gallows, swore that “he was going to die upon that gallows that stood there, and nothing else”—the law hanged those who lied and let the truthful pass. Sancho’s counsel became proverbial: when justice is at a dead end—“lean to mercy”; literature guessed the same thing as logic: the rule here yields no verdict, and the decision has to be made outside the rule.

## Consequence

The Bridge is the performative loop taken from the side of *obligation*: of the crocodile one asks “what will it do?”, of Buridan’s keeper “what *must* he do?”—and the knot exposes the boundary of normative systems: a rule whose condition refers to the execution of the rule itself yields no obligation. Hence a direct line to the jurisprudence of self-referential norms (a law on punishment for lying, applied to a statement about the punishment itself) and to the analysis of promises in speech-act theory: an oath without a performable subject is no oath.

## Domain Analysis

**Domain 1—Recognition: what is given?** Components: an oath-rule (let pass ⟷ the truth of the first word); Socrates’ first word—about Plato’s action under this rule. Structure: the condition of the oath is closed on its execution. No signal yet.

**Domain 2—Clarification: what do the terms mean?** “True,” said of Socrates’ word, is set by Plato’s future action; Plato’s action is set by the oath through the truth-value of the word. First signal: a circle of definitions—truth-value and action hold each other up without touching the world.

**Domain 3—Framework Selection: is the instrument adequate to the object?** Here lies the defect. The oath is an operation on the ready truth-value of the input (level 2 over level 1); Socrates supplies an input whose truth-value is the output of the operation itself. The evaluation is invited to evaluate its own verdict before the verdict is passed. [[Identity]] and [[Non-Contradiction]]—as with the crocodile; [[Sufficient Reason]]—a circle of grounds instead of a support. Signal: a performative loop in normative dress.

**Domain 4—Comparison:** not engaged.

**Domain 5—Inference:** both branches are flawless. The problem is not in the inference.

**Domain 6—Reflection:** every action breaks the oath—a signal: with this input the oath sets no obligation; a return to domain 3.

## Diagnosis

**Domain of the error:** Domain 3 (framework selection). **Type:** structural—vertical level confusion, normative form. **Violated laws:** Identity · Non-Contradiction (the input of the evaluation = its output) · Sufficient Reason (an obligation without a ground outside the circle). **Essence of the defect:** the truth-value of the condition is made dependent on the execution of what it conditions. On the E/R/R triad: the Rule is fed, as an Element, its own verdict.

## Why There Is No Paradox

Given Socrates’ answer, Plato’s oath **does not set an action**: the set of deeds that fulfill it is empty—the obligation “what Plato must do” does not exist; the question has no subject. Socrates did not catch Plato—he showed the boundary of his oath: it is defined only for words whose truth-value comes from outside (“say whether it is raining”), and undefined for words about the verdict itself. Buridan decided from within scholasticism (Socrates’ word in such a setting is simply false—but then the oath obliges a throw, which makes it true: words do not open the circle); Sancho decided more honestly—outside the rule, by mercy: where a norm is incoherent, the verdict is taken from another ground. This is the resolution: not to “find what is due,” but to see that nothing due has been assigned here—and to assign it by a legitimate external move.

## Lesson

**On the limits of a norm.** A rule obliges only where its condition stands on an external ground; a self-referential norm is a hole in the system of obligations, and it is filled by a decision *outside* it. **On two questions.** “What will he do?” and “what must he do?” break on one loop—the descriptive and the normative are held by one vertical. **On Sancho’s mercy.** The literary verdict is logically exact: a dead end of the rule is not a dead end of the court; to acknowledge incoherence and decide from another ground is an honest way out, not a weakness.
