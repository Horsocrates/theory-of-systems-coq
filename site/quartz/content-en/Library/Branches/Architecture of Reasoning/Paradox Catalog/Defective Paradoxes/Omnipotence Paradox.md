**D.9** · [[Defective Paradoxes|defective paradox]] · subtype: Contradiction in the Definition

**Essence of the error.** “Omnipotence” as an unlimited ability is contradictory

## Exact Formulation

Can an omnipotent being create a stone that it itself cannot lift? If yes, there is something it cannot do: lift that stone. If no, there is something it cannot do: create such a stone. Either answer leads to the denial of omnipotence.

## Origin

The question in its modern form is a fruit of monotheism; antiquity was not troubled by it: Zeus is the mightiest, but he is subject to the Moirai, and stones beyond his strength exist as a matter of course. Antiquity did know kin by structure: Epicurus tested the attributes of the deity for consistency (the paradox of evil), and Plato in the *Euthyphro* asked about the relation of the divine will to an independent standard. The theme received detailed development in Islamic kalam earlier than in Christian scholasticism: the Asharites asserted absolute omnipotence, all the way to occasionalism; al-Ghazali attacked the philosophers for “limiting” God by logic; Ibn Rushd answered with a formula that has outlived the centuries: logic is not a cage over God but the structure of reality, expressing His nature. The Christian line: Peter Damian (God can make Rome, once founded, unfounded), Anselm (the “inability” to sin is not weakness but perfection), and the summit—Thomas Aquinas: “whatever implies contradiction does not come within the scope of divine omnipotence, because it cannot have the aspect of possibility”—a square circle is not a difficult thing but nonsense to which nothing corresponds. Descartes brought back radicalism (the eternal truths are created by the will); Leibniz objected: the arbitrariness of truths would destroy goodness itself and truth-value itself. The twentieth century: Mackie (1955) made the paradox an argument against theism; Plantinga (1967) sharpened Aquinas’s line; Geach (1973) separated omnipotence from almightiness; Frankfurt (1964) brought back Damian’s move in analytic packaging.

## Consequence

The structure of the knot lives far beyond theology. Sovereignty: can a sovereign enact a law limiting his power to enact laws (Hart’s solution—to distinguish continuing from self-embracing sovereignty)? Artificial intelligence: can a superintelligence set a task that it itself will not solve? Metamathematics: a sufficiently powerful system does not prove its own consistency—a structural feature of self-application, captured as a theorem. The main consequence, however, is the question of the status of the laws of logic: are they external constraints imposed on reality, or the structure of reality itself? The answer determines what “possible” means.

## Domain Analysis

**Domain 1—Recognition: what is given?** Components: the being, the stone, the actions (to create, to lift), the condition “a stone that its creator cannot lift.” Structure: an ability is directed at creating a limitation for itself. Signal: a construction of self-application—a figure familiar from the [[Liar Paradox|liar]].

**Domain 2—Clarification: what do the terms mean?** Here is the defect. “Omnipotence” splits in two. The naive reading: the ability to do absolutely everything, including the logically impossible. The philosophical reading: the ability to do everything logically possible—everything that can be consistently described. Signal: ambiguity; under the different readings the knot behaves differently—the naive reading is self-contradictory even without the stone, and under the philosophical one the question does not arise.

**Domain 3—Framework Selection:** not reached—the concept needs clarifying before the framework.

**Domain 4—Comparison:** not engaged.

**Domain 5—Inference:** both horns of the dilemma are valid—under the naive reading. The problem is not in the inference.

**Domain 6—Reflection:** every answer destroys omnipotence—signal: ask what the word means; return to domain 2.

## Diagnosis

**Domain of the error:** Domain 2 (clarification). **Type:** defective—contradiction in the definition: the self-contradictoriness of the naive reading. **Violated laws:** [[Non-Contradiction]]—“a stone that cannot be lifted by one who can lift everything” requires an object at once liftable and unliftable; [[Identity]]—two different concepts go about under one word. **Essence of the defect:** the concept is undefined; naive omnipotence is incoherent in itself, and the philosophical one produces no knot. On the E/R/R triad: “omnipotence” is a Role over all the Rules; the naive reading requires the Role to cancel the very Rules that make it meaningful.

## Why There Is No Paradox

Under the naive reading, “a stone that the omnipotent cannot lift” is not the description of an object but a verbal construction to which nothing corresponds: “a married bachelor,” “a square circle.” Grammatically correct—logically empty; the defect belongs to the concept, not to the problem. Under the philosophical reading, Aquinas’s answer closes the question: the contradictory is not something to be done; to say “cannot create a square circle” is not to point to a lack of power but to acknowledge that “a square circle” describes nothing. Deeper lies the question of the status of logic. The Law of Non-Contradiction does not say: “things cannot be A and not-A, although they could have been”; it says: “‘A and not-A’ describes no state of affairs.” Therefore “to violate the Law of Non-Contradiction” is not an action that one may or may not perform, but a meaningless combination of words, like “to jump over Monday.” Logic is not an external rule to which a being submits: it is the condition of the meaningfulness of any action, the architecture of being. Even for a hypothetical omnipotent being, “to create” differs from “not to create” only thanks to logic.

## Objections and Replies

**The line of Damian, Descartes, and Frankfurt.** “God is above logic: omnipotence means precisely the ability to do the contradictory.” Reply—that of Ibn Rushd, Leibniz, and Aquinas at once: logic does not stand over a being like a fence—it is the structure of meaningfulness; “to do the contradictory” is not to do something, for the contradictory is not anything. Voluntarism pays an exorbitant price: if truths are arbitrary, God could make evil good—and the very goodness that the position wanted to exalt collapses.

**“So omnipotence is limited after all.”** Reply: the list of what “cannot be done” is empty—there are no things in it, only failed descriptions. The absence of nonsense among abilities is not a limit of power, just as the absence of a “round square” in geometry is not a gap in it.

## Lesson

**On concepts.** Grammatical correctness does not guarantee meaningfulness: before arguing about the existence of X, check whether the concept of X is coherent. **On logic.** The laws of logic are conditions of meaningfulness, not surmountable constraints; the question “can X violate logic?” betrays a misunderstanding of what logic is. **On pseudo-problems.** The omnipotence paradox is neither an argument against theism nor a riddle for faith: it is a signal that the concept needs clarification—and once it is clarified, the question closes.
