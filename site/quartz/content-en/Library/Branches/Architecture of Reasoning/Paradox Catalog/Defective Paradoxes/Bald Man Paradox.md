**D.2** · [[Defective Paradoxes|defective paradox]] · subtype: Indeterminacy of a Concept

**Essence of the error.** “Bald” is a graded concept without a sharp boundary

## Exact Formulation

A man with a full head of hair is not bald. Losing one hair does not make him bald. If losing one hair never changes the status, then by repeating the step a man who has lost all his hair is not bald either: bald people do not exist. The reverse move: a man without hair is bald; one hair does not cancel baldness; so a man with a full head of hair is bald as well—people with hair do not exist.

## Origin

Eubulides of Miletus, fourth century BCE—the same list in Diogenes Laertius as the [[Liar Paradox|liar]] and the [[Sorites Paradox|sorites]]. The Greek name is φαλακρός (phalakros), “bald.” This is the paired sorites: hairs instead of grains, decrease instead of accumulation; antiquity kept both forms side by side as one argument in two dresses. Its fate is shared with the sorites: two millennia in the margins of logic, and in the twentieth century a place at the center of the debates on vagueness (Russell, Zadeh, Williamson—see the analysis of the sorites).

## Consequence

As a pair to the heap, it shows the symmetry of the mechanism: the sorites works in both directions—by accumulation and by decrease, on “many” and on “few,” on any graded feature with a binary label. The practical progeny is found wherever a gradation meets a label: medicine marks the stages of baldness with a conventional scale (and declares it a convention—an honest move), law draws age and quantity thresholds. The lesson is the same as with the heap—but precisely the recurrence in new material is the bald man’s contribution: the mechanism is recognized regardless of the scenery.

## Domain Analysis

**Domain 1—Recognition: what is given?** Components: a man, hairs, a count, the operation of losing one, the inductive step. No signal yet.

**Domain 2—Clarification: what do the terms mean?** Here is the defect. “Bald” is a comparative concept without a numerical threshold: “balding,” “almost bald”—a grammar of degrees. Any threshold of “N hairs” is arbitrary, and this is a property of the concept, not a gap in knowledge. To demand a binary boundary of a graded concept is to demand that A be not-A ([[Identity]]). Signal: a binary question has been put to a comparative concept.

**Domain 3—Framework Selection:** the second defect, as in the sorites: induction requires a strict step and an invariant property—the step is approximate, and the status of each new state is relative to the context of comparison.

**Domain 4—Comparison:** works properly (“balder,” “less bald”); only the “yes/no” breaks.

**Domain 5—Inference:** valid, given the accepted premises. The problem is not in the inference.

**Domain 6—Reflection:** the absurdity of the conclusion—return to domains 2 and 3.

## Diagnosis

**Domain of the error:** Domain 2 (clarification); Domain 3—secondary. **Type:** defective—indeterminacy of a concept, vagueness. **Violated laws:** Identity (a binary boundary is demanded of a graded concept; the inductive step calls different states “the same”). **Essence of the defect:** a precise instrument on a vague concept. On the E/R/R triad: “bald” is a Role relative to a context of comparison, presented as a property of an Element.

## Why There Is No Paradox

It inherits from the sorites word for word: the inductive step is a practical generalization, not a strict truth, and it does not withstand induction; the question “how many hairs make a man bald?” demands from the concept what, by the way it is built, it does not contain. The full derivation—including the reply to epistemicism and the defense of classical logic on its legitimate territory—is in the analysis of the [[Sorites Paradox|sorites]]. Where a boundary is needed in practice, it is assigned by a scale and declared a convention—this is what medicine does, and there is no paradox in it.

## Lesson

Recognize the sorites in any dress: sand, hairs, dollars, pixels—the mechanism is one, and so is the laying open. A change of scenery does not create a new paradox; it tests whether the lesson of the old one has been learned.
