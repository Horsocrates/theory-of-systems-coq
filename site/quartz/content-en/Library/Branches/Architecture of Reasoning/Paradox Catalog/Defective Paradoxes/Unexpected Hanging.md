**D.7** · [[Defective Paradoxes|defective paradox]] · subtype: Contradiction in the Conditions

**Essence of the error.** “An announced surprise” is a performative contradiction

## Exact Formulation

A judge announces to a prisoner: “You will be executed next week. The day will be a surprise—you will not know which day it is until the executioner comes in the morning.” The prisoner reasons by backward induction: Friday drops out (by the end of Thursday it would be obvious—no surprise); with Friday excluded, Thursday drops out; by the same logic, Wednesday, Tuesday, and Monday. Conclusion: an unexpected execution is impossible. The prisoner calms down—and on Wednesday morning the executioner comes, and the prisoner is genuinely surprised. The reasoning seemed flawless. Where is the error?

## Origin

One of the few classical paradoxes whose birth is known precisely—and it was not born in a study. In 1943–1944 Swedish radio announced a civil defense exercise: it would take place the following week, and the day would be a surprise. The mathematician Lennart Ekbom noticed the oddity: the prisoner’s reasoning proves that a surprise is impossible—yet the exercise took place and was a surprise. In 1948 D. J. O’Connor gave the first philosophical analysis, in the form of a surprise military exercise—a “Class A blackout”; in the 1950s the knot put on the dramatic dress of an execution. Variants reproduce the structure: a test sometime during the week, an egg in one of ten boxes. Then came half a century of debate: Quine (1953)—the prisoner refutes his own premise; Shaw (1958)—the reasoning is self-undermining: the conclusion “there will be no execution” itself restores unexpectedness to every day; Kripke—an analysis through ungroundedness, which relates the knot to the [[Liar Paradox|liar]]; Sorensen (1988)—the connection with the epistemic paradoxes. Hundreds of articles—and this is a symptom: when a problem resists solution for half a century, the question should be put to its formulation.

## Consequence

The central specimen of a defective paradox, and its value is pedagogical: it teaches one to distinguish genuine knots of construction (the structural ones) from problems with spoiled conditions. Its closest kin is Moore’s paradox: “It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining”—grammatically correct, performatively absurd: in asserting it, the speaker expresses a belief that he at once denies. The same family includes self-refuting prophecies and the oxymorons of practice: “mandatory voluntary attendance,” “planned spontaneity,” “a public secret.” A by-product of the debates is an honest one: epistemic concepts—“knowledge,” “expectation,” “surprise”—resist formalization, and this discovery is valuable in itself.

## Domain Analysis

**Domain 1—Recognition: what is given?** Components: the judge, the prisoner, the announcement (a speech act), the execution, the week, the condition of surprise. Structure: an announcement of an event that must be unexpected *for the addressee of the announcement*. The tension is visible already here.

**Domain 2—Clarification: what do the terms mean?** Here is the defect. What does the prisoner know after the announcement? That the execution will happen; that it will happen this week; and that he “will not know.” The announcement transmits knowledge—the promise denies it. [[Identity]]: a surprise is an event the subject did not know about; the judge calls something else a “surprise”—an event whose date is unknown. The unexpectedness of an event and the indeterminacy of its date are different concepts, and the condition demands that they be identical. [[Excluded Middle]]: knowledge is binary—the prisoner either knows about the execution or does not; the law gives no place to a state “knows, yet as if does not know.” Signal: a performative contradiction in the condition itself.

**Domain 3—Framework Selection:** not reached—there is nothing yet to reason about: the conditions need correcting.

**Domain 4—Comparison:** not engaged.

**Domain 5—Inference:** the prisoner’s backward induction is valid step by step—and this is part of the lesson: from contradictory premises correct inference extracts anything at all. The problem is not in the inference.

**Domain 6—Reflection:** the executioner came, “logic failed”—signal: logic worked honestly; it was the premises that let it down; return to domain 2.

## Diagnosis

**Domain of the error:** Domain 2 (clarification). **Type:** defective—contradiction in the conditions. **Violated laws:** Identity (the indeterminacy of the date is passed off as the unexpectedness of the event) · Excluded Middle (knowledge is a binary state; “to know, yet not to know” is not a state). **Essence of the defect:** a performative contradiction—the very act of announcing makes the promised unexpectedness impossible: “now you know about what you will not know.” On the E/R/R triad: the Rule of the problem requires the absence of an Element (knowledge) that the Rule itself supplies.

## Why There Is No Paradox

The prisoner’s right move would have taken a minute: “Your Honor, your statement is contradictory: by announcing the execution, you give me knowledge—by promising unexpectedness, you deny that knowledge.” Instead of a minute—days of sophisticated proof on a contradictory foundation. What happened on Wednesday: the indeterminacy of a *parameter* of a known event was resolved—the prisoner learned “when,” having known “what” since the moment of the announcement; this is a specification, not a surprise. His surprise is the collapse of his own conclusion “there will be no execution”: correct reasoning from incorrect premises gave a false result, as it is bound to. Wednesday is not the fulfillment of the judge’s promise but the prisoner’s payment for unchecked premises.

## Objections and Replies

**The epistemological interpretation.** “Backward induction is illegitimate: it makes use of future epistemic states.” Reply: granted—this explains why the prisoner’s reasoning is wrong, and it leaves the main point untouched: the announcement of unexpectedness is contradictory in itself, whatever conclusions the prisoner draws.

**The modal interpretation.** “‘Unexpectedness’ means: he will not be able to *prove* which day it is.” Reply: this shifts the problem without solving it: the judge uses a word that means one thing and secures another; “unexpectedness in the technical sense” is an admission that the ordinary word does not work in the condition.

**“The execution was unexpected after all.”** Reply: the prisoner did not know *when*—that is indeterminacy; he knew *what*—and that excludes unexpectedness. An expectation with an indeterminate date is not a surprise.

## Lesson

**On checking premises.** Before reasoning, check the coherence of the conditions: a minute of explication against days of proof on a rotten foundation. Faced with a “paradox,” first ask: is the formulation coherent? **On authority.** Judge, professor, expert—a contradiction remains a contradiction, whoever states it; deference to the source must not block the analysis of the content. **On complexity and simplicity.** Sophisticated analysis hides a basic defect—half a century of academic debate around an unchecked premise bears witness to this. **On the limits of formalization.** “Knowledge” and “expectation” resist precise definition—the knot has shown this boundary honestly.
