**D.10** · [[Defective Paradoxes|defective paradox]] · subtype: Contradiction in the Definition

**Essence of the error.** A popular formulation of the omnipotence paradox

## Exact Formulation

Can God create a stone that He Himself cannot lift? The dilemma is symmetric: if He has created it, He will not lift it; if He has not, He cannot do everything. The scene with the stone is the canonical dress of the [[Omnipotence Paradox|omnipotence paradox]]: one knot, one defect, one analysis.

## Origin

A folk form of a question that scholasticism posed in general terms: does the contradictory fall under divine power? The stone made the abstraction tangible—and therefore outlived the treatises: historians remember the dispute of Damian with Anselm and Aquinas, while the stone is put to students in their first year. The pair “scene—theory” here is the same as among the structural paradoxes: the [[Barber Paradox|barber]] is the folk dress of [[Russells Paradox|Russell]], the stone is the folk dress of omnipotence.

## Consequence

The lesson of concreteness is two-sided. The scene helps: the self-application of an ability is visible in one sentence, without a theological vocabulary. And the same scene hides: the dispute seems to be about power (“will He lift it or not?”), whereas it is about the meaning of the word “omnipotence.” Whoever has been drawn into discussing the weight of the stone has already accepted that the description of the stone is meaningful—and that is exactly what should have been checked.

## Domain Analysis

**Domain 1—Recognition: what is given?** A being, a stone, two actions, the condition “the creator will not lift it.” Structure: an ability is turned toward creating a limit for itself. Signal: self-application.

**Domain 2—Clarification: what do the terms mean?** Here is the defect. “A stone unliftable for one who can lift everything” is the description of an object with incompatible properties: liftable (for everything is lifted) and unliftable (by the condition). This is not a heavy stone—it is an empty description, like “a married bachelor.” The whole expression gets its meaning from the word “omnipotence,” and that word is unclarified—the full analysis of its two readings is in the card on [[Omnipotence Paradox|omnipotence]].

**Domain 3—Framework Selection:** not reached.

**Domains 4–5:** the dilemma is valid under the naive reading of the word; the problem is not in the inference.

**Domain 6—Reflection:** both answers destroy the premise—signal: check the concept; return to domain 2.

## Diagnosis

**Domain of the error:** Domain 2 (clarification). **Type:** defective—contradiction in the definition. **Violated laws:** [[Non-Contradiction]] (the object is at once liftable and unliftable) · [[Identity]] (two readings of “omnipotence” under one word). **Essence of the defect:** the problem asks for the creation of what is not anything. On the E/R/R triad: the Element of the problem (the stone) is described through the negation of the Role that is its very measure.

## Why There Is No Paradox

It inherits from omnipotence: under the philosophical reading, “to create such a stone” is not a task but a failed description; declining to carry it out is not powerlessness, for “that” is not something. The answer to the question is honest and short: the question has no subject, and this is a property of the question, not of the one who answers. Whoever wants a dispute on the merits should first define “omnipotence”; once it is defined, there turns out to be nothing to dispute.

## Lesson

A concrete scene is a strong instrument and a trap at once: it makes the knot visible and hides its real place. To recognize the pairs “folk scene—theoretical knot” (the stone—omnipotence, the barber—Russell) is to go straight to the defect, past the scenery.
