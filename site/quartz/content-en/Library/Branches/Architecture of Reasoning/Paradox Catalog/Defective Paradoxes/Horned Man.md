**D.17** · [[Defective Paradoxes|defective paradox]] · subtype: Error in Reasoning

**Essence of the error.** Equivocation: “not lost” presupposes “had”

## Exact Formulation

“What you have not lost, you have. You have not lost horns. Therefore you have horns.” The interlocutor readily accepts both premises—the first sounds like a truism, the second is indisputable—and a syllogism of honest form hands him horns.

## Origin

Eubulides of Miletus, fourth century BCE—the same Megarian arsenal as the [[Liar Paradox|liar]], the [[Sorites Paradox|heap]], and the [[Bald Man Paradox|bald man]]; Diogenes Laertius lists “the Horned One” among his seven dialectical tricks. Unlike the liar, the horned man has had a modest and telling fate: antiquity quickly took the mechanism apart—the Stoics honed on it a theory of the presupposition of a statement—and the knot left the paradoxes for the textbooks, becoming the canonical specimen of a *sophism*: a piece of reasoning whose persuasiveness is built on a hidden defect. In the catalog it holds this boundary: next to the genuine knots, an exhibit of how little is needed for the form of an inference to carry a falsehood.

## Consequence

The horned man is the ancestor of a whole family of tricks with a buried premise. Its direct heir is the loaded question: “have you stopped beating your father?”—any answer confirms the hidden “did beat”; the same mechanics is found in rhetoric (“when will you finally admit the obvious?”), in polls with a built-in assumption, in contractual wordings that take what is disputed as given. To logic the knot bequeathed the concept of presupposition: a statement has not only what it asserts but also what it tacitly presupposes—and agreement with what is said is not agreement with what is presupposed. The difference between “false” and “lacks a presupposition” would later grow into a whole theory (from the Stoics to the disputes about definite descriptions).

## Domain Analysis

**Domain 1—Recognition: what is given?** Components: the major premise (“not lost—so you have it”), the minor (“you have not lost horns”), the conclusion. The form of the syllogism is in order. Signal: the conclusion is absurd while the premises are harmless—there is contraband somewhere.

**Domain 2—Clarification: what do the terms mean?** Here is the defect. “Not lost” splits in two. The strong reading: had and kept—then the major premise is true, but the minor (“you have not lost horns” in the sense of “had them and kept them”) is false and accepted by no one. The weak reading: simply never happened to lose—including because one never had at all; then the minor premise is true, but the major is false: from “never happened to lose” it does not follow that “you have.” The truth-value of the premises rests on different readings—together they are never true. Signal: equivocation—the term changes its meaning between the premises.

**Domain 3—Framework Selection:** not engaged—the defect is purely verbal.

**Domain 4—Comparison:** not engaged.

**Domain 5—Inference:** the form is flawless—and that is the whole design: a sophism hides the defect of its premises behind an honest form. The problem is not in the form.

**Domain 6—Reflection:** an absurd conclusion from “obvious” premises—signal: look for a term that splits in two; return to domain 2.

## Diagnosis

**Domain of the error:** Domain 2 (clarification). **Type:** defective—error in reasoning: equivocation. **Violated laws:** [[Identity]]—“not lost” must mean the same thing in both premises, yet it means different things; the middle term of the syllogism splits in two—a syllogism of four terms. **Essence of the defect:** the major premise smuggles in the presupposition “had”; agreement with a truism is passed off as agreement with an assumption. On the E/R/R triad: the Rule of inference receives two different Elements under one name—a substitution of the Element while the Rule is in order.

## Why There Is No Paradox

Separate the readings—and the sophism falls apart before one’s eyes. “What you had and have not lost, you have” is true, but then the second premise would have to read “you had horns and have not lost them,” which no one has asserted. “What you have never happened to lose, you have” is false: I have never lost the throne of Portugal either. In no reading are both premises true at once—the conclusion finds support in no world. The answer to the interlocutor has been honed for centuries and fits in a line: “not lost” is true only of what one had; with horns, the question of having is exactly what is disputed—you have put the conclusion into the premise. The same move disarms the loaded question: neither “yes” nor “no,” but a rejection of the presupposition—“the question presupposes what never was.” The lesson of the Stoics is honest here: the mechanism of presupposition is not a vice in itself—language always presupposes more than it asserts; the vice is to smuggle the disputed in through a presupposition.

## Lesson

**On the middle term.** Check that the term linking the premises means the same thing in both: equivocation turns a syllogism into one of four terms—the form is intact, the connection is gone. **On presuppositions.** Agreement with what is said is not agreement with what is presupposed; on hearing a truism, ask what it tacitly brings in. **On honest form.** A flawless form is not a certificate of the inference: sophisms settle in the premises; that is why the check begins with Domain 2, not with Domain 5.
